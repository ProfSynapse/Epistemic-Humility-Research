## Abstract

When answering questions, LLMs can convey not only an answer, but a level of confidence about the answer being correct. This includes explicit confidence markers (e.g. giving a numeric score) as well as implicit markers, like an authoritative tone or elaborating with additional knowledge. For LLMs to be trustworthy knowledge sources, the confidence they convey should match their actual expertise; however, most current models tend towards overconfidence. To calibrate both implicit and explicit confidence markers, we introduce a pragmatic, listener-aware finetuning method (LACIE) that models the listener, considering not only whether an answer is right, but whether it will be accepted by a listener. We cast calibration as preference optimization, creating data via a two-agent game, where a speaker model's outputs are judged by a simulated listener. We then finetune three LLMs (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-70B) with LACIE, and show that the resulting models are better calibrated w.r.t. a simulated listener. Crucially, these trends transfer to human listeners, helping them correctly predict model correctness: we conduct a human evaluation where annotators accept or reject an LLM's answers, finding that training with LACIE results in 47% fewer incorrect answers being accepted while maintaining the same level of acceptance for correct answers. Furthermore, LACIE generalizes to another dataset, resulting in a large increase in truthfulness on TruthfulQA when trained on TriviaQA. Our analysis indicates that LACIE leads to a better confidence separation between correct and incorrect examples. Qualitatively, we find that a LACIE-trained model hedges more and implicitly signals certainty when it is correct by using an authoritative tone or including details. Finally, LACIE finetuning leads to an emergent increase in model abstention (e.g. saying "I don't know") for answers that are likely wrong.

## Summary

Stengel-Eskin et al. introduce LACIE (Listener-Aware Calibration for Implicit and Explicit confidence), a pragmatics-grounded finetuning method that calibrates LLM confidence by optimizing for how utterances will be received by a listener, not merely whether the answer is factually correct. The core insight is that LLM overconfidence stems from two compounding gaps: models do not receive direct training signal about whether answers are right (no factual grounding), and they do not generate pragmatically (no listener model). LACIE fills both gaps by running a two-agent speaker-listener game: a speaker model generates diverse long-form answers to TriviaQA questions, a simulated listener model (Mistral-7B) scores whether each answer sounds convincing, and a preference function over (correctness, listener-acceptance) outcome quadrants builds DPO training pairs. The preference ordering encodes a conservative bias: a false rejection (model sounds uncertain about a correct answer) is strictly preferred over a false acceptance (model sounds confident about a wrong answer), encoded in Eq. 1 as true-accept = true-reject greater than false-accept greater than false-reject.

The preference dataset is constructed from 10,000 TriviaQA questions with 10 responses each, balanced to 13,785 pairs across five outcome categories (each capped at 2,757 pairs). Finetuning uses DPO with QLoRA rank-16 for at most 250 steps. The method is evaluated on three speaker models of varying scale (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-70B) with a fixed Mistral-7B listener.

Results on a held-out 1,000-question TriviaQA set show consistent large gains versus base models: +20.7 points AUROC on average, -7.8 points ECE, +18 points absolute precision. LACIE beats a truthful-only DPO baseline by 13.3 AUROC points on average, showing the listener signal adds value beyond answer correctness alone. A human evaluation with 5 Mechanical Turk annotators (n=79 base / n=78 LACIE items, after excluding questions annotators already knew) confirms the pattern transfers to real listeners: false acceptances drop by 47% (32 to 17, p less than 0.05 McNemar's test) with no significant rise in false rejections (6 to 7, p=1.0), and human precision increases by 15 points (0.49 to 0.64).

Out-of-distribution generalization to TruthfulQA (817 questions, never seen during training) shows Mistral-7B LACIE reaches 0.55 truthfulness versus 0.27 base and 0.39 truthful-only, a +28-point absolute gain, at a cost of 9 points of informativeness (0.99 to 0.90) primarily driven by increased abstention.

A qualitatively striking emergent behavior appears: LACIE training produces 25-35% abstention rates (versus under 1% for base models) despite the training set containing no abstention examples at all. Accuracy on non-abstained examples remains comparable to base models, confirming that abstention correlates with genuinely difficult items rather than blanket refusal. Qualitative analysis shows LACIE models hedge more and use explicit uncertainty markers on incorrect examples while adopting a more authoritative tone and adding supporting details on correct ones.

## Relevance to experiment

LACIE is the most direct existing DPO calibration benchmark against which the Phase 1 experiment can be measured. Three connection points stand out.

First, the truthful-only DPO baseline in Table 1 is structurally identical to a calibration-DPO arm without listener grounding. The 13.3-point AUROC gap between LACIE and truthful-only DPO shows that listener-acceptance signal adds substantial value beyond answer-correctness pairs alone. Any Phase 1 arm that uses DPO with factuality pairs but not listener acceptance should expect to land in the truthful-only range, and should aim to close the remaining gap.

Second, the emergent abstention finding (25-35% abstention from training data with zero abstention examples) is convergent evidence for a broader mechanism the graph already documents through [[ternary-reward-enables-abstention-over-hallucination]] and [[dpo-reduces-over-abstention]]: sufficiently calibrated confidence training generates conservative behavior even without explicit abstention supervision. LACIE adds the observation that the mechanism works through the preference function shape (conservative utility ordering, not the training signal type), which the Phase 1 SFT/DPO/KTO comparison should probe by comparing abstention rates across arms trained with the same data but different loss functions.

Third, the OOD truthfulness gain on TruthfulQA (+28 absolute points for Mistral-7B) while only training on TriviaQA establishes that listener-grounded DPO calibration transfers across question domains. The 9-point informativeness cost is the expected abstention-driven recall tradeoff documented across the graph as [[calibration-hallucination-tradeoff]] and the [[rlhf-induces-over-caution-on-safe-inputs]] family. The question for Phase 1 is whether KTO, which does not require paired preferences and uses a reference-point utility function, achieves a similar transfer at lower abstention cost. This is a speculative comparison since KTO is not studied in this paper, but the LACIE preference function (conservative utility ordering) and KTO's prospect-theory asymmetry share structural features that make the structural comparison worth designing into the Phase 1 evaluation. The claim that [[dpo-reduces-over-abstention]] conflicts in direction with what LACIE shows (LACIE DPO greatly increases abstention) is resolved by context: that mechanism describes DPO correcting instruction-tuning over-refusal on answerable questions, whereas LACIE's abstention increase is appropriate and correctness-correlated.
