## Abstract

We investigate the origins of massive activations in large language models (LLMs) and identify a specific layer named the \textbf{Massive Emergence Layer (ME Layer)}, that is consistently observed across model families, where massive activations first emerge and subsequently propagate to deeper layers through residual connections. We show that, within the ME Layer both the RMSNorm and the FFN parameters jointly contribute to the emergence of massive activations. Once formed, the massive activation token representation remains largely invariant across layers, reducing the diversity of hidden representations passed to the attention module. Motivated by this limitation, we propose a simple and effective method to reduce the rigidity of the massive activation token. Our approach consistently improves LLM performance across multiple tasks, including instruction following and math reasoning, in both training free and fine tuning settings. Moreover, we show that our method mitigates attention sinks by selectively weakening their influence, elucidating their origin at the hidden state level and shedding new light on principled mitigation strategies.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experiment. The paper localizes massive-activation emergence to a single 'ME Layer' per model, where the first token's hidden state is amplified by hundreds of times via joint RMSNorm+FFN action, and shows this single-layer origin is consistent across model families and sizes (Figure 1, Figure 2-4, Appendix B, Appendix H).
- Evidence label: experiment. Once formed at the ME Layer, the massive-activation token's hidden-state direction is stable across subsequent layers and near-identical across different input instances (Figure 5, Appendix D, Appendix F).
- Evidence label: experiment. WeMask (weight-guided dimension masking) consistently improves downstream performance across instruction-following, math-reasoning, and safety-alignment tasks, both as a training-free inference-time intervention and when incorporated into SFT/DPO/GRPO training, but masking 100% of top-weighted dimensions catastrophically degrades performance (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4).
- Evidence label: analysis. Attention sinks emerge in the layer immediately following the ME Layer and reflect a low-rank, massive-activation-induced representation collapse rather than a pure softmax artifact; WeMask does not eliminate the sink but moderately attenuates it, and this partial (not complete) attenuation is associated with the best performance (Section 6, Figure 7, Figure 8).
