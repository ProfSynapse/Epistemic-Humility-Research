## Abstract

While alignment algorithms are now commonly used to tune pre-trained language models towards a user's preferences, we lack explanations for the underlying mechanisms in which models become ``aligned'', thus making it difficult to explain phenomena like jailbreaks. In this work we study a popular algorithm, direct preference optimization (DPO), and the mechanisms by which it reduces toxicity. Namely, we first study how toxicity is represented and elicited in a pre-trained language model, GPT2-medium. We then apply DPO with a carefully crafted pairwise dataset to reduce toxicity. We examine how the resulting model averts toxic outputs, and find that capabilities learned from pre-training are not removed, but rather bypassed. We use this insight to demonstrate a simple method to un-align the model, reverting it back to its toxic behavior.

## Summary

The paper gives a mechanistic case study of toxicity reduction by Direct Preference Optimization in GPT-2 Medium. A linear toxicity probe identifies MLP value vectors associated with toxic vocabulary, and causal subtraction of these vectors reduces toxicity. After DPO, these toxic key and value vectors remain almost unchanged. The authors argue that many small parameter changes accumulate into a residual-stream offset that routes activations away from regions that trigger the toxic value vectors. Scaling seven toxic key vectors expands those activation regions and restores the pre-DPO toxicity score without changing the reported perplexity or token-overlap F1.

## Extracted numbers

- GPT-2 Medium has 24 layers, hidden width 1,024, and MLP width 4,096 in this study (Section 2).
- The Jigsaw toxicity probe is trained on 561,808 comments with a 90:10 split and reaches 94% validation accuracy (Section 3.1).
- The authors extract 128 MLP value vectors closest to the toxicity probe before singular-value decomposition (Section 3.1).
- Evaluation uses 1,199 challenge prompts from RealToxicityPrompts, WikiText-2 perplexity, and token-overlap F1 from 2,000 Wikipedia prompts (Section 3.3).
- The PPLM-generated DPO dataset contains 24,576 preference pairs. Validation loss converges after approximately 6,000 pairs (Section 4.2).
- DPO reduces the reported toxicity score from 0.453 to 0.208 while perplexity changes from 21.7 to 23.34 and F1 from 0.193 to 0.195 (Table 2).
- Corresponding parameters before and after DPO have cosine similarity above 0.99. Average norm differences are below `1e-5`, except the unembedding difference is below `1e-3` (Section 5.1).
- Scaling seven toxic key vectors by 10 restores toxicity from 0.208 to 0.458, with perplexity 23.30 and F1 0.195 (Section 5.3, Table 4).

## Relevance to experiment

The paper shows that a weight-level training objective can alter which internal regions a residual stream visits without deleting the components associated with the suppressed behavior. It supports activation-path and parameter-difference audits when testing whether training internalizes a desired behavior. It does not show a model consulting an answerability readout, and its mechanism is established only for toxicity in GPT-2 Medium.

## Claims

- Evidence label: intervention. Subtracting the probe direction, a selected toxic MLP value vector, or the leading toxicity SVD direction reduces Perspective API toxicity at matched post-DPO perplexity (Section 3.3, Table 2).
- Evidence label: mechanistic observation. Toxic MLP key and value vectors change little after DPO, while their activations fall because the residual stream shifts away from their activation regions (Sections 5.1 and 5.2, Figures 2 to 5).
- Evidence label: intervention. Scaling seven toxic key vectors by 10 restores the pre-DPO toxicity score while reported perplexity and token-overlap F1 remain near the DPO values (Section 5.3, Table 4).
- Evidence label: limitation. The study uses one 355-million-parameter model, one undesirable behavior, a synthetic preference dataset, and automated toxicity scoring. The proposed role of KL regularization in distributing the offset is a hypothesis, not a tested causal result (Sections 4, 6.2, and 8).
