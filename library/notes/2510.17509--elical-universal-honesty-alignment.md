## Abstract

Honesty alignment-the ability of large language models (LLMs) to recognize their knowledge boundaries and express calibrated confidence-is essential for trustworthy deployment. Existing methods either rely on training-free confidence estimation (e.g., token probabilities, self-consistency) or training-based calibration with correctness annotations. While effective, achieving universal honesty alignment with training-based calibration requires costly, large-scale labeling. To support annotation-efficient training, we introduce Elicitation-Then-Calibration (EliCal), a two-stage framework that first elicits internal confidence using inexpensive self-consistency supervision, then calibrates this confidence with a small set of correctness annotations. To support a large-scale study, we release HonestyBench, a benchmark covering ten free-form QA datasets with 560k training and 70k evaluation instances annotated with correctness and self-consistency signals. Experiments show that EliCal achieves near-optimal alignment with only 1k correctness annotations (0.18% of full supervision) and better alignment performance on unseen MMLU tasks than the calibration-only baseline, offering a scalable solution toward universal honesty alignment in LLMs.

## Summary

Ni et al. propose Elicitation-Then-Calibration (EliCal), a two-stage annotation-efficient framework for honesty alignment in LLMs. The core insight is that correctness annotations serve two separable purposes: teaching a model to express confidence at all, and calibrating that expressed confidence against actual accuracy. EliCal separates these by first training a LoRA-augmented confidence head on large-scale self-consistency supervision (Stage 1: Confidence Elicitation), then fine-tuning on a small set of correctness-labeled examples (Stage 2: Confidence Calibration). The architecture freezes the backbone, attaches LoRA modules to all linear layers, and adds a linear head mapping the final-layer hidden state of the last question token to a scalar confidence score trained with MSE loss. Stage 1 uses k=20 sampled responses (temperature=1) checked for semantic consistency against the greedy response by Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct; Stage 2 requires only a small correctness-labeled set. To support large-scale study, the authors release HonestyBench: 567,647 training instances from five factual QA datasets (NQ, TriviaQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, ParaRel) plus 37,904 in-domain and 32,805 OOD evaluation instances, each annotated with both correctness and self-consistency confidence for three LLMs. Experiments on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct show EliCal with only 1,000 correctness annotations (0.18% of full supervision) achieves AUROC 84.36 in-domain and 84.47 OOD, reaching roughly 98% of the upper-bound performance obtained by training on all 560k+ annotations. The elicitation-only ablation (Eli-Only) matches the best training-free baseline (Consis-Sem) at a fraction of its inference cost. EliCal generalizes better to MMLU than Cal-Only even when Cal-Only has access to all 560k annotations, because internalizing self-consistency signals at scale produces representations that transfer across task formats rather than overfit to domain-specific correctness labels.

## Relevance to experiment

EliCal bears on the locked training-regimen SFT/DPO/KTO experiment in two concrete ways, with one important caveat about the structural analogy. First, the two-stage decomposition provides an existence proof that self-consistency signals alone can bring a confidence head to within a few AUROC points of an oracle trained on 560k correctness labels. The locked training-regimen arms train on output-quality preferences rather than self-consistency targets, so they do not replicate EliCal Stage 1 directly. But Stage 1 is evidence that an LLM's internal state at the final question token already encodes enough about self-consistency to support downstream calibration at minimal annotation cost, which converges with the mechinterp hypothesis: the last-layer hidden state is a candidate activation-probe site for the mechanism program inventory. Second, the MMLU generalization finding (EliCal beats a 560k-label Cal-Only baseline on a format-shifted benchmark) argues that internalizing consistency signals at scale reduces overfitting to QA-domain labels. This maps onto the locked training-regimen concern about alignment tax: SFT arms may acquire domain-label patterns that degrade cross-task calibration. The caveat: HonestyBench annotates free-form factual QA, which differs from the locked training-regimen preference-comparison format. The 98% upper-bound efficiency claim does not transfer directly to a claim that 1k DPO or KTO preference pairs would suffice for honesty calibration, because elicitation and calibration objectives differ from ranking objectives in preference learning. Use the result as an annotation-efficiency benchmark, not as a guarantee about locked training-regimen sample requirements.
