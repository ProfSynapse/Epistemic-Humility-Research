## Abstract

When language models (LMs) are trained via reinforcement learning (RL) to generate natural language "reasoning chains", their performance improves on a variety of difficult question answering tasks. Today, almost all successful applications of RL for reasoning use binary reward functions that evaluate the correctness of LM outputs. Because such reward functions do not penalize guessing or low-confidence outputs, they often have the unintended side-effect of degrading calibration and increasing the rate at which LMs generate incorrect responses (or "hallucinate") in other problem domains. This paper describes RLCR (Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards), an approach to training reasoning models that jointly improves accuracy and calibrated confidence estimation. During RLCR, LMs generate both predictions and numerical confidence estimates after reasoning. They are trained to optimize a reward function that augments a binary correctness score with a Brier score -- a scoring rule for confidence estimates that incentivizes calibrated prediction. We first prove that this reward function (or any reward function that uses a bounded, proper scoring rule) yields models whose predictions are both accurate and well-calibrated. We next show that across diverse datasets, RLCR substantially improves calibration with no loss in accuracy, on both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations -- outperforming both ordinary RL training and classifiers trained to assign post-hoc confidence scores. While ordinary RL hurts calibration, RLCR improves it. Finally, we demonstrate that verbalized confidence can be leveraged at test time to improve accuracy and calibration via confidence-weighted scaling methods. Our results show that explicitly optimizing for calibration can produce more generally reliable reasoning models. Code, models, and further info is available at https://rl-calibration.github.io/.

## Summary

Damani et al. introduce RLCR (Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards), a training method that augments the standard binary correctness reward used in reasoning-model RL with the Brier score: R_RLCR(y,q,y*) = 1[y=y*] - (q - 1[y=y*])^2. The model is prompted to produce a chain-of-thought reasoning trace, an answer, an optional uncertainty analysis, and a verbalized numerical confidence estimate. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that this combined reward, and any reward using a bounded proper scoring rule in place of the Brier term, has two properties: for any fixed prediction y the expected reward is maximized by a calibrated confidence q equal to the true success probability, and among all calibrated predictions the one with the highest success probability is preferred. Log loss is excluded because it is unbounded and the analogous result fails. Training runs on Qwen2.5-7B base using GRPO with no KL regularization. Across two training domains (HotpotQA multi-hop QA; Big-Math mathematical reasoning) and a combined set of six to seven OOD benchmarks, RLCR matches RLVR accuracy in-domain while reducing ECE from 0.37 to 0.03 (HotpotQA) and from 0.26 to 0.10 (math), and outperforms both ordinary RLVR and post-hoc confidence classifiers on OOD calibration. Ablations in Table 2 show that the reward signal, not just the uncertainty-analysis prompt format, drives the gains: RLCR without its analysis phase still drops ECE to 0.09 vs. RLVR's 0.37 at similar token cost. RLVR with the analysis prompt improves only modestly (OOD Brier 0.41 vs. RLCR's 0.21). A SFT warmup variant (SFT+RLCR) achieves the best in-domain AUROC (0.78) and ECE (0.08) on math but suffers OOD accuracy collapse (43.8% vs. 50.9% for RLCR), attributed to catastrophic forgetting. At test time, verbalized confidence enables confidence-weighted majority vote, which outperforms vanilla majority vote, max-confidence, and likelihood-based selection across seven datasets (Figure 3a). Calibration also improves modestly as analysis-CoT ensemble size grows (Figure 3b), consistent with low uncertainty-about-uncertainty for most questions.

## Relevance to experiment

RLCR is the most direct precedent for the locked training-regimen calibration-reward arm. The paper adds a bounded proper scoring rule (Brier score) term alongside the binary correctness reward and proves this simultaneously incentivizes accuracy and calibrated confidence, which is exactly the objective the locked training-regimen hypothesis targets. Three implications stand out. First, the Theorem 1 result specifies a precise constraint -- boundedness -- that any proper scoring rule used as a calibration reward must satisfy; log loss fails this and should not be used. Second, the ablation in Table 2 isolates the reward signal from the CoT format: RLCR without its analysis phase still cuts ECE from 0.37 to 0.09 relative to RLVR, confirming that the reward itself, not prompt engineering, is the mechanism. This is a direct check on whether DPO/KTO arms in the locked training-regimen study should add a calibration signal or only modify the preference format. Third, RLCR is trained with no KL regularization (Section 4.1), while the DPO and KTO arms in the locked protocol include KL control; the interaction between calibration rewards and KL regularization is therefore an open experimental variable. The OOD calibration advantage of RLCR (ECE 0.21 vs. 0.46 for RLVR on HotpotQA OOD) suggests that the calibration-reward effect generalizes beyond the training distribution, which bears on whether locked training-regimen gains will transfer to the held-out evaluation suite. The SFT+RLCR catastrophic-forgetting result (OOD accuracy 43.8%) warns against SFT warmup on narrow datasets when OOD capability is a primary objective. OOD calibration ECE should be added to the locked training-regimen evaluation suite alongside in-domain ECE, given the paper's finding that RLVR worsens OOD calibration while RLCR improves it.
