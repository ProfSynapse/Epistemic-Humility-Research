## Abstract

One of the key technologies for the success of Large Language Models (LLMs) is preference alignment. However, a notable side effect of preference alignment is poor calibration: while the pre-trained models are typically well-calibrated, LLMs tend to become poorly calibrated after alignment with human preferences. In this paper, we investigate why preference alignment affects calibration and how to address this issue. For the first question, we observe that the preference collapse issue in alignment undesirably generalizes to the calibration scenario, causing LLMs to exhibit overconfidence and poor calibration. To address this, we demonstrate the importance of fine-tuning with domain-specific knowledge to alleviate the overconfidence issue. To further analyze whether this affects the model's performance, we categorize models into two regimes: calibratable and non-calibratable, defined by bounds of Expected Calibration Error (ECE). In the calibratable regime, we propose a calibration-aware fine-tuning approach to achieve proper calibration without compromising LLMs' performance. However, as models are further fine-tuned for better performance, they enter the non-calibratable regime. For this case, we develop an EM-algorithm-based ECE regularization for the fine-tuning loss to maintain low calibration error. Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed methods.

## Summary

Xiao et al. diagnose why preference alignment degrades calibration and propose two fine-tuning algorithms to fix it. The causal story is preference collapse: RLHF and DPO train the model to strongly prefer one response over others, and this tendency generalises to multiple-choice settings where the model assigns near-unit probability to one option regardless of whether it is correct. The result is systematic overconfidence and high ECE even when the base model was well-calibrated before alignment.

The paper introduces a theoretical calibration framework built on the idea of a target probabilistic generative model. It derives upper and lower bounds on ECE as a function of model accuracy, which produces a bipartite partition of the accuracy-ECE space. In the calibratable regime (accuracy below the critical threshold), a model can reach zero ECE without giving up accuracy, and SFT-based calibration-aware fine-tuning (CFT) suffices. In the non-calibratable regime (accuracy above the threshold), zero ECE is structurally impossible according to the target distribution, and the paper's regularized CFT (RCFT) uses an EM algorithm to minimise an ECE regularisation term jointly with the SFT loss, navigating the accuracy-calibration tradeoff rather than eliminating it.

Experiments span four aligned 7-8B models: Llama-3.1-8B-Tulu (DPO), Vicuna-7B (RLHF), Mistral-7B (DPO), and OLMo2-7B (DPO), evaluated in-domain (MMLU, MedMCQA, OpenBookQA) and out-of-domain (ARC-Challenge), with temperature scaling as the primary baseline. CFT consistently outperforms temperature scaling on calibration while also improving accuracy and win rate. RCFT trades some calibration quality for substantially higher accuracy and remains competitive with temperature scaling on ECE. Temperature scaling reliably reduces ECE but leaves accuracy and win rate unchanged, confirming it adjusts surface confidence without recovering capability lost to preference collapse.

## Extracted numbers

All nine numbers were confirmed against the source in adversarial verification on 2026-06-24. Numbers map onto the effects.csv intervention schema: the intervention is CFT or RCFT applied to a preference-aligned model, the metric is ECE (conf-ECE and cw-ECE) and accuracy, with in-domain and out-of-domain splits.

- CFT reduces overall ECE of preference-aligned models from the range 14.22%-20.10% down to 2.39%-6.51% across all four model architectures and alignment methods (§1 Introduction).
- Llama-3.1-8B-Tulu: DPO baseline conf-ECE 0.1953; CFT reduces it to 0.0239 (88% reduction); RCFT achieves conf-ECE 0.0897 while raising in-domain accuracy from DPO 0.6228 to RCFT 0.8341 (Table 2).
- Vicuna-7B (RLHF-aligned, not DPO): baseline conf-ECE 0.1422; CFT reduces it to 0.0379 (73% reduction); CFT out-domain accuracy 0.6172 vs RLHF baseline 0.5233 (Table 2, §6.2).
- Mistral-7B: DPO conf-ECE 0.2010; CFT reduces it to 0.0651 (68% reduction); CFT out-domain accuracy 0.7863 vs DPO 0.7567 (Table 2, §6.2).
- OLMo2-7B: DPO conf-ECE 0.1555; CFT in-domain conf-ECE 0.0544; CFT out-domain cw-ECE 0.0637 vs temperature scaling 0.1196; RCFT in-domain accuracy 0.851 vs DPO 0.621 (Table 2).
- Temperature scaling on Vicuna-7B lowers in-domain conf-ECE to 0.0598 (vs RLHF baseline 0.1422) but leaves accuracy 0.4344 and out-domain accuracy 0.5233 unchanged, identical to the RLHF baseline (Table 2, §6.2).
- CFT on Llama-3.1-8B-Tulu improves out-domain win rate to 0.7460 vs DPO 0.7295 while maintaining out-domain cw-ECE of 0.0375 (Table 2, §6.2).
- Ablation: accuracy loss alone achieves ~90% accuracy but poor calibration; ECE loss alone drives the model toward random guessing (~25% accuracy, near-zero ECE); combining both objectives achieves the CFT result (§6.3, Appendix C.2).
- Training configuration: QLoRA with rank=128, LoRA alpha=64, bfloat16 precision, 5 epochs, batch size 2, learning rate 5e-4, on 2 A100-40G GPUs; in-domain training set is 3,000 samples from MMLU plus MedMCQA plus OpenBookQA (§6.1).

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is directly relevant to the locked training-regimen SFT vs DPO comparison and to the calibration diagnostics used across all arms. Three contributions connect concretely to the experiment design.

First, the preference-collapse mechanism establishes a causal pathway from DPO training to overconfidence. If the locked training-regimen DPO arm produces worse calibration than the SFT arm on the same base model, preference collapse is the most parsimonious explanation, and this paper's theoretical framework (the calibratable/non-calibratable partition) tells the experimenter whether recalibration via domain-specific fine-tuning is structurally possible given the DPO model's achieved accuracy.

Second, the temperature-scaling result is a direct caution for experiment reporting: temperature scaling reduces ECE without improving accuracy or win rate, so an ECE improvement that does not come with accuracy or win-rate movement is a surface fix, not evidence of recovered epistemic grounding. This constrains what can be claimed from a calibration-only intervention in the locked training-regimen results.

Third, CFT and RCFT are concrete candidate remediation steps for any arm that shows alignment-induced overconfidence: CFT is appropriate if the model falls in the calibratable regime; RCFT is appropriate when accuracy gains have pushed it into the non-calibratable regime. The paper's QLoRA setup (rank=128, 3,000 samples, 5 epochs) is directly replicable with the locked training-regimen infrastructure.

One scoping note: the paper does not study KTO, does not use open-ended generation tasks, and its calibration diagnostics are multiple-choice ECE rather than verbalized or logit-based confidence on free-form responses. Findings on whether preference collapse degrades calibration in open-ended settings remain an open question this paper does not address.

## Claims

- Preference collapse, the tendency of RLHF and DPO to over-concentrate probability on one response option, generalises to multiple-choice calibration settings, causing aligned models to be overconfident regardless of option correctness. [[preference-collapse-causes-alignment-overconfidence]]
- In the calibratable regime (model accuracy below the critical threshold), CFT via SFT loss alone recovers near-zero ECE without any accuracy loss, reducing conf-ECE by 68-88% across four models compared to DPO/RLHF baselines. [[calibration-aware-fine-tuning]]
- Temperature scaling reliably reduces ECE (e.g., Vicuna-7B conf-ECE from 0.1422 to 0.0598) but leaves accuracy and win rate unchanged, confirming it is a post-hoc confidence adjustment rather than a capability recovery technique. [[expected-calibration-error]]
- In the non-calibratable regime (model accuracy above the critical threshold), achieving zero ECE is structurally impossible under the target generative model; RCFT uses EM-based ECE regularisation to navigate the accuracy-calibration tradeoff, reaching high accuracy (e.g., OLMo2-7B RCFT accuracy 0.851 vs DPO 0.621) with competitive calibration. [[regularized-calibration-aware-fine-tuning]]
- CFT generalises out-of-domain without any additional calibration signal: on ARC-Challenge (unseen during training), Llama-3.1-8B-Tulu CFT achieves cw-ECE 0.0375 and win rate 0.746, both better than the DPO baseline (cw-ECE higher, win rate 0.7295). [[calibration-aware-fine-tuning]]
