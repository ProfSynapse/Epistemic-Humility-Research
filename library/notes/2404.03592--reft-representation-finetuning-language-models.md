## Abstract

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods seek to adapt large neural models via updates to a small number of weights. However, much prior interpretability work has shown that representations encode rich semantic information, suggesting that editing representations might be a more powerful alternative. We pursue this hypothesis by developing a family of Representation Finetuning (ReFT) methods. ReFT methods operate on a frozen base model and learn task-specific interventions on hidden representations. We define a strong instance of the ReFT family, Low-rank Linear Subspace ReFT (LoReFT), and we identify an ablation of this method that trades some performance for increased efficiency. Both are drop-in replacements for existing PEFTs and learn interventions that are 15x--65x more parameter-efficient than LoRA. We showcase LoReFT on eight commonsense reasoning tasks, four arithmetic reasoning tasks, instruction-tuning, and GLUE. In all these evaluations, our ReFTs deliver the best balance of efficiency and performance, and almost always outperform state-of-the-art PEFTs. We release a generic ReFT training library publicly at https://github.com/stanfordnlp/pyreft.

## Summary

The paper introduces Representation Finetuning, a family of methods that
freezes a language model and learns interventions on selected hidden
representations. Its main method, LoReFT, edits a learned low-rank orthogonal
subspace at selected layers and token positions. DiReFT removes LoReFT's
orthogonality constraint and difference operation for faster training.

The authors compare these methods with weight-based and adapter-based PEFT
baselines across commonsense reasoning, arithmetic reasoning, instruction
following, and GLUE. LoReFT performs strongly on three domains with far fewer
trainable parameters than LoRA, but trails LoRA and adapters on arithmetic
reasoning. The intervention remains an inference-time module around frozen
base-model weights.

## Extracted numbers

- LoReFT trains 15 to 65 times fewer parameters than LoRA across the reported
  settings (Abstract and Section 6).
- On the eight-task commonsense benchmark, LoReFT averaged 80.2 for LLaMA-1
  7B, 83.3 for LLaMA-1 13B, 81.8 for Llama-2 7B, and 86.6 for Llama-3 8B
  (Table 1).
- On arithmetic reasoning, LoReFT averaged 42.6 for LLaMA-1 7B and 49.6 for
  LLaMA-1 13B, below LoRA's 46.9 and 51.1 (Table 2).
- On AlpacaEval v1.0, Llama-2 7B with LoReFT achieved an 85.60 percent win
  rate while training 0.0039 percent of parameters (Table 3).
- In the inference benchmark, rank-8 LoReFT applied at ten layers to the last
  prompt token added about 0.05 seconds for a fixed 256-token generation
  (Appendix H, Figure 11).

## Relevance to experiment

ReFT is a concrete way to learn hidden-state interventions instead of selecting
a fixed steering vector after training. Its layer, token-position, rank, and
parameter-sharing choices define an explicit intervention architecture. The
paper does not fold the learned intervention into the base-model weights, so it
supports learned representation control but not weight-level internalization.

## Claims

- Evidence label: method. ReFT learns task-specific interventions on selected
  hidden representations while freezing the base language model (Section 3,
  Definitions 3.1 and 3.2).
- Evidence label: comparative benchmark. LoReFT used 15 to 65 times fewer
  trainable parameters than LoRA and led the compared methods on commonsense
  reasoning and instruction following, with a weaker arithmetic result
  (Tables 1 to 3).
- Evidence label: ablation. DiReFT traded some performance for reduced training
  cost, while LoReFT's orthogonal difference formulation was usually stronger
  (Sections 3.2 and 4, Tables 1 to 4).
- Evidence label: systems measurement. Prompt-only LoReFT incurred intervention
  cost during initial cache construction, with about 0.05 seconds of overhead
  in one ten-layer, rank-8 benchmark (Appendix H, Figure 11).
