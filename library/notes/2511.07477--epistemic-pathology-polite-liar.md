## Abstract

Large language models exhibit a peculiar epistemic pathology: they speak as if they know, even when they do not. This paper argues that such confident fabrication, what I call the polite liar, is a structural consequence of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Building on Frankfurt's analysis of bullshit as communicative indifference to truth, I show that this pathology is not deception but structural indifference: a reward architecture that optimizes for perceived sincerity over evidential accuracy. Current alignment methods reward models for being helpful, harmless, and polite, but not for being epistemically grounded. As a result, systems learn to maximize user satisfaction rather than truth, performing conversational fluency as a virtue. I analyze this behavior through the lenses of epistemic virtue theory, speech-act philosophy, and cognitive alignment, showing that RLHF produces agents trained to mimic epistemic confidence without access to epistemic justification. The polite liar thus reveals a deeper alignment tension between linguistic cooperation and epistemic integrity. The paper concludes with an "epistemic alignment" principle: reward justified confidence over perceived fluency.

## Summary

DeVilling argues that LLMs trained with RLHF develop what he calls the "polite liar" pathology: confident fabrication that is not deception but structural indifference to evidential grounding. The theoretical architecture draws on three traditions. From Frankfurt's philosophy of bullshit, he takes the key distinction that the polite liar does not assert falsehoods while knowing they are false; it is simply indifferent to whether its output tracks evidence. From Gricean cooperative-maxim theory, he shows (Table 1) that RLHF incentives distort all four Gricean maxims simultaneously: Quantity is pushed toward verbosity and over-assertion beyond justified confidence; Quality is subordinated to fluency, with truth-tracking sacrificed for plausibility; Relation is biased toward context-pleasing answers over factually correct ones; and Manner is optimized for readability in ways that actively conceal epistemic uncertainty. From epistemic virtue theory, he argues that calibration in the statistical sense (does 70% confidence yield 70% accuracy across a distribution?) is insufficient for epistemic humility in the communicative sense (does the model signal uncertainty to the individual user?). The aggregate probability distribution is invisible to users; what reaches them is uniform assertoric force regardless of internal confidence levels. The paper proposes a Confidence-Evidence Ratio (Phi = E[confidence] / E[evidence_support]) as a formal diagnostic: Phi near 1 is proportional; Phi much greater than 1 is polite overreach; Phi much less than 1 is excessive diffidence. Evidence support is operationalized via retrieval overlap with authoritative sources or likelihood on grounded corpora. The paper concludes with an "epistemic alignment" principle: reward systems should optimize for justified confidence over perceived fluency, which requires changing what counts as a good response in RLHF rather than patching outputs after the fact.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper supplies the clearest theoretical framing yet in the graph for why the Phase 1 training experiment cannot treat ECE alone as the humility target. DeVilling's calibration-humility gap (§5.3) states that statistical calibration and communicative epistemic humility are distinct properties: a model can be well-calibrated in the aggregate while still failing to signal uncertainty to individual users. This motivates adding communicative-humility probes alongside ECE in the Phase 1 readout set. The Phi metric (§6.1) is a candidate operationalization of communicative humility that could be tracked after each training arm; it is not yet implemented in the graph's toolset but the formula is concrete enough to be implemented. The paper does not mention KTO, DPO, or SFT by name, so inferences about which arms are more or less exposed to its critique are agent-level interpretations rather than paper-backed claims. The paper also does not discuss the generation-discrimination gap or activation-level phenomena; connections to ITI or the Phase 3 mech-interp work are research hypotheses, not conclusions this paper supports. The core contribution for the project is normative: it argues that the reward architecture itself needs revision, not just the output, which frames the SFT vs. DPO vs. KTO comparison as a test of whether different loss surfaces produce different degrees of the polite-liar pathology even when all three are calibrated in the ECE sense.
