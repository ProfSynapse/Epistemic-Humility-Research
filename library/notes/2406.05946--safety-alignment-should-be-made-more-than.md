## Abstract

The safety alignment of current Large Language Models (LLMs) is vulnerable. Relatively simple attacks, or even benign fine-tuning, can jailbreak aligned models. We argue that many of these vulnerabilities are related to a shared underlying issue: safety alignment can take shortcuts, wherein the alignment adapts a model's generative distribution primarily over only its very first few output tokens. We refer to this issue as shallow safety alignment. In this paper, we present case studies to explain why shallow safety alignment can exist and provide evidence that current aligned LLMs are subject to this issue. We also show how these findings help explain multiple recently discovered vulnerabilities in LLMs, including the susceptibility to adversarial suffix attacks, prefilling attacks, decoding parameter attacks, and fine-tuning attacks. Importantly, we discuss how this consolidated notion of shallow safety alignment sheds light on promising research directions for mitigating these vulnerabilities. For instance, we show that deepening the safety alignment beyond just the first few tokens can often meaningfully improve robustness against some common exploits. Finally, we design a regularized finetuning objective that makes the safety alignment more persistent against fine-tuning attacks by constraining updates on initial tokens. Overall, we advocate that future safety alignment should be made more than just a few tokens deep.

## Summary

Qi et al. (2024) argue that many known jailbreak vulnerabilities (adversarial suffixes, prefilling attacks, decoding-parameter manipulation, fine-tuning attacks) share a common root cause: safety alignment can take a shortcut, adapting a model's generative distribution primarily over its first few output tokens rather than the whole response. They call this shallow safety alignment. As evidence, on the HEx-PHI safety benchmark Llama-2-7B-Chat begins its response with "I cannot" or "I apologize" 96.1% of the time, and Gemma-7B-1.1-IT begins with "I am unable" 96.7% of the time; and when the unaligned base models are forced (via prefilling) to start their own generation with those same refusal tokens, their harmfulness rate collapses toward the aligned model's near-zero rate. As a counterfactual, the authors augment SFT data with safety-recovery examples that continue harmful-looking prefixes with a refusal, deepening the alignment past the first tokens; the augmented model shows sharply lower attack success rate across all tested inference-time exploits and better durability against downstream fine-tuning attacks. Finally, they design a token-wise regularized fine-tuning objective that constrains updates on the initial output tokens specifically, making safety alignment more persistent against fine-tuning attacks.

## Relevance to experiment

Shallow safety alignment is training-side evidence for the same "alignment concentrates on a small surface" pattern that URIAL (arXiv:2312.01552) documents from the prompting side, but here the surface is a temporal one (the first few output tokens) rather than a lexical one (stylistic tokens). It matters for reading trained-vs-prompted abstention gaps: if a training run only teaches a model to reliably emit an initial hedge/refusal token, but a prompt can be trivially bypassed or a training-time attack can strip that first-token behavior, the underlying capability gap between prompted and trained abstention may be smaller than raw benchmark numbers suggest, and durability against downstream perturbation (not just first-response accuracy) becomes a meaningful axis for evaluating whether SFT/DPO/KTO abstention training goes deeper than a first-token shortcut.
