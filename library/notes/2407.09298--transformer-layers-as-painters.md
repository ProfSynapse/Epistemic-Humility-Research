## Abstract

Despite their nearly universal adoption for large language models, the internal workings of transformers are not well understood. We aim to better understand the impact of removing or reorganizing information throughout the layers of a pretrained transformer. Such an understanding could both yield better usage of existing models as well as to make architectural improvements to produce new variants. We present a series of empirical studies on frozen models that show that the lower and final layers of pretrained transformers differ from middle layers, but that middle layers have a surprising amount of uniformity. We further show that some classes of problems have robustness to skipping layers, running the layers in an order different from how they were trained, or running the layers in parallel. Our observations suggest that even frozen pretrained models may gracefully trade accuracy for latency by skipping layers or running layers in parallel.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experiment. Middle layers of Llama2-7B and BERT-Large share a common representation space: skipping or switching the input source of a middle layer causes only graceful degradation while the same treatment applied to the first/last few layers is catastrophic, and this matches a high-cosine-similarity block spanning the middle layers in the all-layers similarity matrix (Figure 2, Figure 3, Section 3.1).
- Evidence label: experiment. Several middle layers can be skipped entirely with only graceful benchmark degradation for both Llama2-7B and BERT-Large, and this beginning/middle/ending block structure and its skip-tolerance pattern hold uniformly across Llama2-7B, 13B, and 70B (Figure 4, Section 3.2; Figure 12, Appendix A.1).
- Evidence label: experiment. Middle layers are not functionally redundant despite sharing a representation space: replacing a span of middle layers with repeated copies of the single center layer's weights ('Middle Repeat') is the single most catastrophic intervention tested, degrading far faster to random-baseline performance than simply skipping the same layers (Figure 5, Figure 11, Section 3.3; Figure 15, Appendix A.4).
- Evidence label: experiment. Layer execution order matters more for mathematical/reasoning benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K) than for semantic benchmarks (HellaSwag, WinoGrande) under reversed, random, and parallel execution, and looping a parallel-executed block of middle layers for a task-scaled number of iterations substantially recovers performance lost to a single parallel pass, making Looped Parallel the least-harmful variant overall for both Llama2-7B and BERT-Large (Section 3.6; Figure 9, Figure 10, Figure 11, Section 3.7-3.8).
