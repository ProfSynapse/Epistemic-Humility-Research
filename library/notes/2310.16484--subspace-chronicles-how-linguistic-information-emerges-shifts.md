## Abstract

Representational spaces learned via language modeling are fundamental to Natural Language Processing (NLP), however there has been limited understanding regarding how and when during training various types of linguistic information emerge and interact. Leveraging a novel information theoretic probing suite, which enables direct comparisons of not just task performance, but their representational subspaces, we analyze nine tasks covering syntax, semantics and reasoning, across 2M pre-training steps and five seeds. We identify critical learning phases across tasks and time, during which subspaces emerge, share information, and later disentangle to specialize. Across these phases, syntactic knowledge is acquired rapidly after 0.5% of full training. Continued performance improvements primarily stem from the acquisition of open-domain knowledge, while semantics and reasoning tasks benefit from later boosts to long-range contextualization and higher specialization. Measuring cross-task similarity further reveals that linguistically related tasks share information throughout training, and do so more during the critical phase of learning than before or after. Our findings have implications for model interpretability, multi-task learning, and learning from limited data.

## Claims

- Evidence label: representational dynamics. A critical learning phase
  between 1k and 10k pretraining steps (about 0.5% of full training) accounts
  for most syntactic knowledge acquisition and the steepest gains in
  subspace-level task information (Section 5.1, Figure 2-3).
- Evidence label: subspace instability. Principal Subspace Angles (SSA)
  between the same task's probe subspace at successive checkpoints keep
  shifting substantially (around 45 degrees per step during the critical
  phase, narrowing to only 5-20 degrees by the end of training) well after
  probing F1 has converged, and SSA between different random seeds at a
  matched checkpoint consistently exceeds 80 degrees despite near-identical
  F1 across those seeds (Section 5.2, Figure 4 and Appendix C.1).
- Evidence label: cross-task structure. Cross-task subspace overlap, measured
  by SSA rather than shared data or labels, groups tasks along linguistically
  intuitive lines (syntax-related POS and dependency parsing overlap
  strongly; sentiment, QA, and NLI share more with each other) and this
  sharing is highest during the critical learning phase (Section 5.3, Figure
  6).

## Summary

Müller-Eberstein, van der Goot, Plank and Titov build an information-theoretic
probing suite to study not just how well a linear probe performs on a
linguistic task at a given LM checkpoint, but how the probe's own fitted
subspace changes across training time and across tasks, using 290 MultiBERTs
models (Sellam et al. 2022) spanning 2M pretraining steps and five random
seeds. Rather than measuring probe accuracy alone, each probe's quality is
scored by codelength (the minimum-description-length bits-back compression
cost of transmitting the probe, following Voita and Titov 2020), and the
resulting task-specific subspace is compared across checkpoints or tasks
using Principal Subspace Angles (SSA), computed from the SVD of the
transformation between the two subspaces' orthonormal bases. SSA needs only
the probe weight matrices, so it can compare subspaces without requiring
matched input data or labels across tasks.

Section 5.1 finds a critical learning phase between 1k and 10k pretraining
steps during which most tasks reach most of their performance and codelength
gains, with syntax-adjacent tasks (POS, dependency) converging earliest and
open-domain, reasoning-heavy tasks (QA, NLI) continuing to improve past 100k
steps. Section 5.2 is the paper's central instability finding: SSA between
consecutive checkpoints of the same run shifts at a steady ~45 degree rate
during the critical phase and only narrows to 5-20 degrees near the end of
the 2M-step run, meaning the fitted subspace keeps rotating well after F1 has
plateaued; and separately, SSA between different random seeds at the same
checkpoint consistently measures above 80 degrees, near-orthogonal, even
though those same seeds show only minor F1 differences. Section 5.3 uses SSA
cross-task to show tasks share subspace information along linguistically
intuitive lines (POS and dependency parsing overlap heavily; NER shares with
POS/semantic tags but less with dependency; sentiment/QA/NLI cluster
together), and this sharing peaks during the critical learning phase before
tasks specialize into distinct layers. Section 6 confirms these dynamics
carry over to full fine-tuning and out-of-domain generalization: starting
fine-tuning from the steepest-improvement checkpoint (5k steps) is not
sufficient to reach 90-95% of final fine-tuned performance, so pretraining
past the critical phase still matters for downstream use even though probing
performance looks nearly converged. The paper explicitly cautions that
single-checkpoint probing studies risk missing these dynamics, since a
checkpoint's probed subspace is not representative of where that subspace
ends up, especially for an undertrained model.

- Critical learning phase (Section 5.1, Figure 2): steep F1 increase between
  1k and 10k update steps across all nine tasks; POS and TOPIC converge
  (>90% of final F1) within the 10k range; NER continues improving past 100k
  steps.
- Step-wise SSA (Figure 4, Section 5.2): particularly large subspace updates
  in the first 100 steps, smaller changes until ~1k steps, then a steady
  ~45 degree shift per step through the 1k-10k critical phase, decreasing to
  5-20 degrees by the end of the 2M-step run.
- Cross-seed SSA at matched checkpoints (Appendix C, Figure 13): SSA between
  models sharing the same initialization but different training seeds is
  consistently greater than 80 degrees, indicating high dissimilarity despite
  comparable performance.
- Reproduction check (Section 4.1): SSAs between the paper's own reproduced
  early checkpoints and the official MultiBERTs checkpoints at the matching
  step are around 60 degrees, within the range observed for same-run,
  different-seed comparisons.
- Full fine-tuning transfer (Section 6.3): starting full fine-tuning from
  the 5k-step checkpoint (near the steepest information-gain point) does not
  suffice to reach the ~90-95% of final performance achieved when fine-tuning
  from the 20k-step checkpoint.

## Relevance to experiment

Subspace Chronicles is direct precedent for cross-checkpoint subspace
comparison using only fitted probe bases, exactly the operation a
correctness-subspace-overlap estimator needs when comparing correctness
subspaces across training stages or seeds. Its comparison instrument,
Principal Subspace Angles (new atom [[principal-subspace-angles]]), needs
only the two probes' weight matrices and no matched inputs or labels, which
generalizes better across training stages (different data distributions per
stage) than a instance-matched measure would.

More importantly, its central finding is an independent replication, at LM
pretraining scale and with a different instrument, of the exact dissociation
the correctness-direction-rotation cell ran into: SSA shows that a task
probe's own fitted subspace keeps rotating substantially even after that
probe's F1 has converged (45 degrees per step during the critical phase,
still 5-20 degrees apart at the very end of training), and that subspaces
fit on different random seeds at the same checkpoint are consistently more
than 80 degrees apart despite near-identical performance. This is the same
shape of result as
[[correctness-direction-weakly-identified-defeats-cosine-rotation-probe]]'s
split-half noise floor (0.174 cosine) sitting close to the observed
cross-stage cosines: a stable readout (AUROC there, F1 here) does not license
treating the underlying fitted direction or subspace as stable. The new
mechanism
[[subspace-identity-rotates-across-seeds-and-checkpoints-despite-stable-probe-performance]]
records this parallel. Practically, it means any correctness-subspace-overlap
design should expect a nonzero, possibly large, cross-seed or cross-checkpoint
angle even under a null hypothesis of "the true subspace did not change,"
and should calibrate against that baseline (e.g. via multiple seeds at a
fixed checkpoint) rather than treating a large angle alone as evidence of
genuine rotation.
