## Abstract

Recent papers show LLMs achieve near-random accuracy in causal relation classification, raising questions about whether such failures arise from limited pretraining exposure or deeper representational gaps. We investigate this under uncertainty-based evaluation, testing whether pretraining exposure to causal examples improves causal understanding >18K PubMed sentences -- half from The Pile corpus, half post-2024 -- across seven models (Pythia-1.4B/7B/12B, GPT-J-6B, Dolly-7B/12B, Qwen-7B). We analyze model behavior through: (i) causal classification, where the model identifies causal relationships in text, and (ii) verbatim memorization probing, where we assess whether the model prefers previously seen causal statements over their paraphrases. Models perform four-way classification (direct/conditional/correlational/no-relationship) and select between originals and their generated paraphrases. Results show almost identical accuracy on seen/unseen sentences (p > 0.05), no memorization bias (24.8% original selection), and output distribution over the possible options is almost flat, with entropic values near the maximum (1.35/1.39), confirming random guessing. Instruction-tuned models show severe miscalibration (Qwen: > 95% confidence, 32.8% accuracy, ECE=0.49). Conditional relations induce highest entropy (+11% vs. direct). These findings suggest that failures in causal understanding arise from the lack of structured causal representation, rather than insufficient exposure to causal examples during pretraining.

## Summary

Lithgow-Serrano, Kanjirangat, and Antonucci ask whether LLM failures on causal relation classification stem from insufficient pretraining exposure or a deeper representational deficit. They build two PubMed-sentence datasets: MCQA (1,434 sentences from The Pile, filtered from Yu et al. 2019 with total variation distance 0.026 from the original label distribution) and MCQA-newer (post-2024 sentences labeled by a BERT classifier with F1 = 0.97, subsampled to match MCQA class distribution). Together with five paraphrases and one negation per sentence the corpus reaches 18,366 items. Seven models spanning base pretrained (Pythia-1.4B/7B/12B, GPT-J-6B, all Pile-trained) and instruction-tuned (Dolly-v2-7B/12B built from Pythia, Qwen-7B-base not Pile-trained) are evaluated on two tasks: four-way causal classification (direct, conditional, correlational, no relationship; 25% random baseline) and verbatim memorization probing (select among paraphrases of an original sentence). The central finding is null: Pile-trained base models show near-identical accuracy on seen and unseen sentences (delta below 1.5 percentage points, Cohen's d below 0.2; dolly-v2-12b is the single exception with a significant MCQA-vs-MCQA-newer accuracy shift at p = 0.001), and no selection preference for original verbatim text (24.8% selection rate, statistically indistinguishable from 25% random). Entropy hovers near the four-class maximum of 1.39, confirming random guessing. Instruction tuning collapses calibration without improving accuracy: Dolly-v2-7B ECE rises from Pythia-7B's 0.131 to 0.363, and Qwen-7B-base reaches ECE 0.493 while assigning greater than 95% confidence at only 32.8% accuracy. Conditional causal relations are systematically hardest, carrying 11% higher entropy than direct causal relations. Paraphrase consistency analysis shows 60-75% of questions receive consistently wrong predictions across paraphrases, interpreted as evidence of aleatoric ambiguity in the task rather than model-specific confusion. Larger instruction-tuned models (Qwen, Dolly) show high KL divergence across paraphrases (greater than 0.5 for Qwen, greater than 1.0 for Dolly), while small base models stay stable (KL below 0.15). The authors conclude that causal understanding failures reflect a structural representational gap, not a pretraining data volume problem, and that instruction tuning teaches overconfidence as a surface behavior while leaving the underlying gap intact.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper has three direct connections to the Synaptic Tuner Phase 1 experiment. First, it provides a clean null result on data exposure: Pile-trained base models show no accuracy advantage on seen sentences, which supports treating epistemic uncertainty in the models under study as representational rather than recall-based. That baseline assumption underlies the Phase 1 hypothesis that SFT, DPO, and KTO interventions change how uncertainty is represented, not just what the model has memorized. Second, the calibration collapse induced by instruction tuning (ECE nearly tripling from Pythia to Dolly at matched size) is directly relevant to the SFT arm: the paper is evidence that naive instruction tuning suppresses appropriate uncertainty expression, and the mechanism it proposes (confidence imitation as a surface behavior) is already captured in the graph under verbalized-confidence-imitation-overconfidence. The Synaptic Tuner SFT arm should measure ECE and ACE pre- and post-training to determine whether honesty-oriented SFT avoids this collapse or merely shifts where it appears. Third, the finding that conditional causal relations induce maximum entropy points to a structural difficulty that calibration training alone cannot fix: if the model lacks compositional causal representations, better-calibrated uncertainty over the wrong distribution does not add understanding. This is a boundary condition for interpreting any accuracy gains in the Phase 1 task mix and connects to the known-unknowns taxonomy in the library. The verbatim memorization null result also strengthens the case that uncertainty probes in the mechinterp sweep are reading representational state, not familiarity with specific training tokens.
