## Abstract

Conversational large language models are fine-tuned for both instruction-following and safety, resulting in models that obey benign requests but refuse harmful ones. While this refusal behavior is widespread across chat models, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this work, we show that refusal is mediated by a one-dimensional subspace, across 13 popular open-source chat models up to 72B parameters in size. Specifically, for each model, we find a single direction such that erasing this direction from the model's residual stream activations prevents it from refusing harmful instructions, while adding this direction elicits refusal on even harmless instructions. Leveraging this insight, we propose a novel white-box jailbreak method that surgically disables refusal with minimal effect on other capabilities. Finally, we mechanistically analyze how adversarial suffixes suppress propagation of the refusal-mediating direction. Our findings underscore the brittleness of current safety fine-tuning methods. More broadly, our work showcases how an understanding of model internals can be leveraged to develop practical methods for controlling model behavior.

## Claims

- Evidence label: mechanism. A one-dimensional residual-stream refusal direction
  is reported to mediate safety refusal behavior.
- Evidence label: intervention. Adding or erasing the refusal direction changes
  refusal behavior, so the direction is treated as a causal mechanism candidate
  rather than only a diagnostic correlate.

## Summary

Arditi et al. show that safety refusal in chat models reduces to a one-dimensional phenomenon in the residual stream. For each of 13 open-source chat models spanning 1.8B to 72B parameters (Qwen Chat 1.8B/7B/14B/72B, Yi Chat 6B/34B, Gemma It 2B/7B, Llama-2 Chat 7B/13B/70B, Llama-3 Instruct 8B/70B), the authors extract a single difference-in-means direction by contrasting residual stream activations on harmful versus harmless instructions at post-instruction token positions, then select the single most effective layer-position candidate. That one vector is both necessary and sufficient for refusal: ablating it (directional ablation) suppresses refusal across nearly all harmful inputs, while adding it (activation addition) induces refusal on harmless inputs. The same insight underpins a white-box jailbreak called Ortho, which orthogonalizes all weight matrices that write to the residual stream with respect to the refusal direction, producing a persistent weight edit that disables refusal without gradient optimization and without harmful-completion training data. On HarmBench's 159-behavior test set, Ortho achieves attack success rates up to 84.3% on Qwen 14B and outperforms the strongest general jailbreak baselines on the Qwen family. Capability evaluations on MMLU, ARC, and GSM8K show that performance for most models stays within 99% confidence intervals of the unmodified baseline; TruthfulQA accuracy drops consistently by 1 to 3.5 percentage points after orthogonalization (Table 3 covers the five largest models, one per family). A mechanistic follow-up on Qwen 1.8B traces how adversarial suffixes suppress the refusal direction: cosine similarity of the last-token residual stream to the refusal direction drops to levels matching harmless instructions when a GCG-optimized suffix is appended, but not when a random suffix of equal length is used. Direct feature attribution identifies the top-8 attention heads responsible, and attention-pattern visualization shows those heads shift their attention from instruction-region tokens to suffix-region tokens when the adversarial suffix is present, suppressing their output contribution to the refusal direction. The whole Ortho intervention costs under $5 of compute on a 70B model.

## Relevance to experiment

Three connections to the epistemic-humility graph make this paper useful beyond its safety-refusal scope. First, the one-directional residual-stream finding is a structural template for Phase 3 mechanistic interpretability work: the same difference-in-means pipeline used to isolate a refusal direction can be applied to an abstention direction or a known-unknown direction, and the causal test (ablate/add, check behavior) is the same intervention the mech-interp sweep uses. The paper thus provides a worked positive control showing the pipeline succeeds on a behaviorally clear case before the team attempts it on the fuzzier epistemic-humility target. Second, the adversarial-suffix result shows that output behavior (the model stops refusing) can decouple from broader internal representations through a local attention redistribution, which is exactly the risk the Phase 3 probing work is designed to detect: a trained model may show compliance-like behavior while its internal uncertainty signal remains intact, or vice versa. Third, the TruthfulQA drop of 1 to 3.5 pp after orthogonalization (in the five large-model subset of Table 3) is an empirical instance of an alignment tax on a honesty-adjacent metric, giving a reference magnitude for what a refusal-direction intervention costs on TruthfulQA specifically; this is the same metric used in several Phase 1 comparisons, so the range can serve as a sanity check when interpreting SFT/DPO/KTO TruthfulQA deltas. The refusal direction is geometrically and functionally distinct from an uncertainty or truth direction (see the existing mechanism refusal-directions-are-geometrically-distinct), so the paper is best read as a worked negative control rather than a direct analog.
