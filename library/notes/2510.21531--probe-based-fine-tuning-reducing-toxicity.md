## Abstract

Probes trained on model activations can detect undesirable behaviors like deception or biases that are difficult to identify from outputs alone. This makes them useful detectors to identify misbehavior. Furthermore, they are also valuable training signals, since they not only reward outputs, but also good internal processes for arriving at that output. However, training against interpretability tools raises a fundamental concern: when a monitor becomes a training target, it may cease to be reliable (Goodhart's Law). We propose two methods for training against probes based on Supervised Fine-tuning and Direct Preference Optimization. We conduct an initial exploration of these methods in a testbed for reducing toxicity and evaluate the amount by which probe accuracy drops when training against them. To retain the accuracy of probe-detectors after training, we attempt (1) to train against an ensemble of probes, (2) retain held-out probes that aren't used for training, and (3) retrain new probes after training. First, probe-based preference optimization unexpectedly preserves probe detectability better than classifier-based methods, suggesting the preference learning objective incentivizes maintaining rather than obfuscating relevant representations. Second, probe diversity provides minimal practical benefit - simply retraining probes after optimization recovers high detection accuracy. Our findings suggest probe-based training can be viable for certain alignment methods, though probe ensembles are largely unnecessary when retraining is feasible.

## Summary

The paper tests two ways to use a frozen linear toxicity probe as a training
signal for Gemma-3-1B. Probe-based DPO uses probe scores to select chosen and
rejected responses before standard DPO training. Probe-regularized SFT adds a
probe toxicity penalty directly to the language-modeling loss and
backpropagates through the frozen probe into model activations.

Probe-scored DPO reduced toxicity while preserving probe detectability better
than classifier-scored DPO in the reported run. Direct SFT regularization only
slightly reduced the toxicity increase caused by training on toxic comments
and modestly lowered the original probes' AUC. Held-out and newly retrained
probes remained accurate, suggesting that the toxicity signal moved to a
different linear direction rather than disappearing.

## Extracted numbers

- Classifier-scored DPO reduced toxicity rate from 0.23 to 0.11. One-probe and
  ten-probe DPO reached 0.14 and 0.15 (Section 5.1, Table 1).
- Under classifier-scored DPO, held-out and retrained probe AUC fell to 0.866
  and 0.770. One-probe DPO retained 0.938 and 0.926, while ten-probe DPO
  retained 0.994 and 0.992 (Section 5.1, Table 1).
- Standard SFT increased toxicity from 0.22 to 0.38. One-probe and ten-probe
  regularization both reached 0.35 (Section 5.2, Table 2).
- Retrained-probe AUC after SFT was 0.958 for classifier SFT, 0.973 for
  one-probe SFT, and 0.957 for ten-probe SFT (Section 5.2, Table 2).
- Ten probes improved DPO held-out AUC from 0.938 to 0.994 but required ten
  times the probe-scoring computation (Section 5.1).
- The reported experiments used one training seed and 100 held-out prompts for
  toxicity-rate evaluation (Sections 4.2 and 5).

## Relevance to experiment

Probe-regularized SFT is a direct weights-level training objective that reads a
hidden-state scalar and backpropagates it into the model. Probe-based DPO is
less direct: the probe constructs static preference pairs, and the later DPO
objective does not consult hidden states during its weight updates.

Neither method makes generation consult the probe at inference time. The
results are limited to one seed, one 1B base model, and toxicity, which is
readily measurable from outputs. The paper reports substantial metric noise
and cannot determine whether probe-based DPO removes toxic processing or only
produces non-toxic text while retaining detectable toxicity representations.

## Claims

- Evidence label: method. Probe-based DPO ranks sampled responses with frozen
  hidden-state probes, while probe-regularized SFT differentiates a frozen
  probe penalty through layer-20 activations (Sections 3.2 and 3.3).
- Evidence label: comparative benchmark. Probe-scored DPO retained higher
  held-out and retrained probe AUC than classifier-scored DPO, but produced a
  slightly higher toxicity rate (Section 5.1, Table 1).
- Evidence label: SFT comparison. Probe regularization reduced the toxicity
  increase from 0.38 to 0.35 and did not prevent a modest reduction in the
  original training probes' AUC (Section 5.2, Table 2).
- Evidence label: probe refit. Newly trained probes recovered high AUC after
  optimization, limiting the demonstrated benefit of maintaining a probe
  ensemble when refitting is feasible (Sections 5.1 and 5.2).
