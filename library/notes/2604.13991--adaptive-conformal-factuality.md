## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are prone to generating factually incorrect outputs. Recent work has applied conformal prediction to provide uncertainty estimates and statistical guarantees for the factuality of LLM generations. However, existing approaches are typically not prompt-adaptive, limiting their ability to capture input-dependent variability. As a result, they may filter out too few items (leading to over-coverage) or too many (under-coverage) for a given task or prompt. We propose an adaptive conformal prediction approach that extends conformal score transformation methods to LLMs, with applications to long-form generation and multiple-choice question answering. This enables prompt-dependent calibration, retaining marginal coverage guarantees while improving conditional coverage. In addition, the approach naturally supports selective prediction, allowing unreliable claims or answer choices to be filtered out in downstream applications. We evaluate our approach on multiple white-box models across diverse domains and show that it significantly outperforms existing baselines in terms of conditional coverage.

## Summary

Rubashevskii et al. address a core weakness in applying conformal prediction to LLMs: existing methods use a single globally calibrated threshold and therefore only guarantee marginal coverage (correct on average), while performing poorly within specific prompt categories, yielding over-coverage for some domains and under-coverage for others. Figure 1 in the paper illustrates this directly: when a conformal procedure is calibrated jointly on Biographies and Inventions, global thresholding produces over-coverage for Inventions (the harder category) and under-coverage for Biographies (the easier category), while the adaptive method achieves target coverage for both.

The proposed fix is a two-stage adaptive conformal prediction pipeline. In the first stage, a conditional quantile estimator is trained on prompt embeddings (768-dimensional sentence embeddings PCA-reduced to 32 dimensions) using the pinball loss. In the second stage, each nonconformity score is divided by its predicted conditional quantile before the usual conformal calibration step, producing a normalized score whose quantiles are approximately prompt-invariant. The result is a prompt-dependent threshold that preserves finite-sample marginal guarantees (proven in Appendix A) while improving conditional coverage across heterogeneous categories.

The method applies to two settings: long-form QA (where atomic claims are filtered by uncertainty score) and multiple-choice QA (where candidate answer classes are filtered). In both settings the paper benchmarks the adaptive approach against the non-adaptive Conformal Factuality baseline.

A parallel contribution is a systematic comparison of claim-level uncertainty scorers for long-form generation, measured by PR-AUC. Claim Conditioned Probability (CCP) outperforms all other scorers (Max Token Entropy, Perplexity, Maximum Probability, TokenSAR, Pointwise Mutual Information) across all three models, and the paper adopts it as the claim scorer for subsequent conformal experiments.

Experiments run on Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Gemma-3-12B-Instruct. Long-form QA uses 8 categories from the HEAD-QA-derived benchmark (300 samples per category, 2400 total), and MCQA uses 16 MMLU categories. Each experiment is repeated 10 times with random data shuffles.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is a post-hoc evaluation harness, not a training intervention, so it does not populate effects.csv directly. Its relevance to the Synaptic Tuner experiment is two-fold.

First, the CCP scorer is the strongest available claim-level uncertainty signal for long-form generation across all three tested LLM families (PR-AUC 0.360-0.367 for 7-8B models). After locked training-regimen arms, CCP could serve as the claim-filtering signal in a downstream factuality evaluation, giving a principled way to measure how each training arm changes the model's within-claim uncertainty discrimination before applying conformal thresholds.

Second, the adaptive conformal pipeline provides a ready-made framework for estimating whether a trained model's factuality guarantees are stable across knowledge categories. If locked training-regimen training improves factuality on Biographies but hurts it on Landmarks, a global conformal threshold would mask that; the adaptive method would expose it. This makes the paper relevant as a conditional evaluation tool for any experiment that reports factuality across heterogeneous domains.

The key caveat is that the paper evaluates white-box models at inference time and assumes a calibration set with ground-truth factuality labels. Generating those labels requires GPT-4o annotation (as done in the paper), which adds an evaluation cost not present in simpler accuracy metrics.
