## Abstract

Modern language models often need to optimize a primary accuracy objective while also accommodating secondary behavioral preferences, such as verbosity, agreeableness, or the level of technical expertise in its response. In practice, a base model may exhibit a desired behavior very rarely or not at all. Thus, endowing the model with a target behavior creates a sparse behavioral reward bottleneck. To address such multi-objective problems, we introduce Vector-Steered Policy Optimization (VSPO) which employs a steering vector associated with the target behavior to control the behavior intensity of the generated rollouts. VSPO is obtained by modifying GRPO to sample rollouts with varying steering intensities. This process can be interpreted as an on-policy latent self-distillation procedure where the model internalizes its steering vector. By varying steering intensities, VSPO upsamples rare behaviors and enriches rollout diversity, which alleviates the sparse reward issue and provably accelerates the policy optimization. Through comprehensive theory and experiments, we establish that VSPO has favorable properties compared to vanilla reward shaping and other alternative approaches. Specifically, under a bandit abstraction, VSPO provably achieves better iteration complexity than reward-shaped GRPO when the steering-induced distributions are sufficiently aligned with the target behavior. We evaluate VSPO across multiple reasoning benchmarks, including MATH and MMLU-Pro, for four target behaviors: explanation expertise, confidence expression, robustness to misleading context, and response verbosity. Our results show that VSPO consistently improves the control along target behavior while maintaining or improving task accuracy compared with reward shaping, teacher-trace distillation, and guidance-based baselines.

## Summary

The paper introduces Vector-Steered Policy Optimization. A teacher helps create
contrastive response pairs for a target behavior, and target-model activation
differences define a steering vector. During GRPO training, the current policy
generates one rollout at each of several steering intensities. The unsteered
policy is then updated from group-relative advantages over those rollouts.

VSPO treats activation steering as structured exploration. It exposes rare
behavioral variants before reward computation and distills useful variants into
the policy weights. Tests cover explanation expertise, expressed confidence,
resistance to misleading context, and response length.

## Extracted numbers

- Steering vectors were built from 200 positive-negative rewrite pairs per
  target behavior (Section 4.1.1).
- On MMLU-Pro expert control, VSPO reached a style score of 64.3 and accuracy
  of 75.4, compared with the original model's 26.3 and 44.0 (Table 1).
- Under misleading context, the original model scored 23.6 percent accuracy and
  VSPO reached 64.2 percent (Section 4.2, Figure 4).
- On MMLU-Pro concise reasoning, VSPO reached 60.8 percent accuracy with
  2,345.26 tokens, versus 59.1 percent and 2,616.70 tokens for reward shaping
  (Section 4.2, Figure 5).
- Most steering-vector construction benefit appeared within the first 20 to 50
  contrastive pairs (Appendix H, Figure 9).

## Relevance to experiment

VSPO is a direct steering-to-weights internalization method. The activation
intervention is used only to diversify training rollouts. The final policy is
updated and evaluated without steering. This design is relevant to persistent
behavior control, but its confidence task controls wording style rather than
calibration or internal answerability.

## Claims

- Evidence label: method. VSPO samples current-policy rollouts at multiple
  steering intensities, then updates the unsteered policy with a GRPO objective
  (Section 3.1 and Appendix D.2).
- Evidence label: theory. Under the paper's bandit abstraction and alignment
  condition, VSPO has a better iteration-complexity bound than reward-shaped
  GRPO (Section 3.2, Propositions 1 and 2, Corollary 3).
- Evidence label: comparative benchmark. VSPO improved target behavior while
  preserving or improving task accuracy across four behavior-control settings
  (Section 4.2, Table 1 and Figures 3 to 5).
- Evidence label: training dynamics. A fixed vector remained effective across
  checkpoints and exposed progressively stronger target behavior during
  training (Section 4.3, Figure 7).
