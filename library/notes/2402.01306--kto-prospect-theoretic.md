## Abstract

Kahneman & Tversky's $\textit{prospect theory}$ tells us that humans perceive random variables in a biased but well-defined manner (1992); for example, humans are famously loss-averse. We show that objectives for aligning LLMs with human feedback implicitly incorporate many of these biases -- the success of these objectives (e.g., DPO) over cross-entropy minimization can partly be ascribed to them belonging to a family of loss functions that we call $\textit{human-aware losses}$ (HALOs). However, the utility functions these methods attribute to humans still differ from those in the prospect theory literature. Using a Kahneman-Tversky model of human utility, we propose a HALO that directly maximizes the utility of generations instead of maximizing the log-likelihood of preferences, as current methods do. We call this approach KTO, and it matches or exceeds the performance of preference-based methods at scales from 1B to 30B, despite only learning from a binary signal of whether an output is desirable. More broadly, our work suggests that there is no one HALO that is universally superior; the best loss depends on the inductive biases most appropriate for a given setting, an oft-overlooked consideration.

## Summary

Ethayarajh et al. frame existing LLM alignment objectives through the lens of Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory and introduce the concept of human-aware loss functions (HALOs): objectives that implicitly encode human cognitive biases such as loss aversion and reference-point sensitivity. They prove that DPO, SLiC (calibration loss only), and PPO-Clip all belong to this family (Proposition 3.5), and show empirically across Pythia and Llama model families (1B to 30B parameters) that HALO methods outperform non-HALOs (CSFT, full SLiC), with the gap becoming statistically significant (p < 0.05) only at 13B+ scale. A striking control result shows that offline PPO with dummy +1/-1 rewards performs on par with DPO at all scales except Llama-30B, suggesting the inductive bias of the loss function matters more than the quality of reward signal at moderate scale. Building on this analysis, the paper derives a new HALO called Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO) that maximizes the prospect-theoretic utility of individual outputs directly, using only a binary desirability label per response rather than contrastive preference pairs. KTO matches or exceeds DPO at all tested scales (1B to 30B), can discard up to 90% of desirable training examples while still outperforming DPO on Llama-7B, and is the only tested method to match SFT+DPO performance when applied without any prior supervised finetuning stage. Theoretical results explain these properties: Proposition 4.1 shows KTO ignores examples too noisy to learn from; Theorem 4.2 shows maximizing preference likelihood does not imply maximizing human utility; and Theorem 4.3 shows KTO is robust to intransitive preferences where DPO degrades in the worst case. The conclusion is explicit that no single HALO is universally superior: the best loss depends on the inductive biases most appropriate for a given setting.

## Relevance to experiment

KTO is the third training arm in the Phase 1 experiment (SFT vs DPO vs KTO). Three properties are directly operationally relevant. First, the loss-aversion asymmetry encoded in KTO's objective (penalizing undesirable outputs more heavily than it rewards desirable ones) could produce a distinct abstention profile compared to DPO: the asymmetric gradient signal may make the model more conservative about generating outputs it cannot confidently mark as desirable, which is precisely the calibrated abstention behavior the experiment is designed to elicit and measure. Second, the finding that KTO alone (without SFT) is competitive with SFT+KTO for Llama models suggests the Phase 1 design could test a KTO-skip-SFT cell without a quality penalty, compressing the run matrix if SFT compute is a bottleneck. Third, KTO's demonstrated robustness to extreme data imbalance (90% desirable-data discard, 1:10 ratio) is directly relevant to abstention-labeled corpora, where undesirable (abstain) examples are typically rarer than positive examples. None of the confirmed numbers map onto the effects.csv intervention schema because they are win-rate and capability scores, not abstention-rate or calibration deltas; those deltas will be generated by the Phase 1 runs themselves.
