## Abstract

Chat-based language models are designed to be helpful, yet they should not comply with every user request. While most existing work primarily focuses on refusal of "unsafe" queries, we posit that the scope of noncompliance should be broadened. We introduce a comprehensive taxonomy of contextual noncompliance describing when and how models should not comply with user requests. Our taxonomy spans a wide range of categories including incomplete, unsupported, indeterminate, and humanizing requests (in addition to unsafe requests). To test noncompliance capabilities of language models, we use this taxonomy to develop a new evaluation suite of 1000 noncompliance prompts. We find that most existing models show significantly high compliance rates in certain previously understudied categories with models like GPT-4 incorrectly complying with as many as 30% of requests. To address these gaps, we explore different training strategies using a synthetically-generated training set of requests and expected noncompliant responses. Our experiments demonstrate that while direct finetuning of instruction-tuned models can lead to both over-refusal and a decline in general capabilities, using parameter efficient methods like low rank adapters helps to strike a good balance between appropriate noncompliance and other capabilities.

## Summary

Brahman et al. introduce CoCoNot (Contextually, Comply Not), a benchmark and training resource for contextual noncompliance in language models. The paper starts from the observation that prior work on model refusal has focused almost exclusively on safety: harmful, toxic, or illegal content. CoCoNot broadens this to four additional categories where direct compliance is also wrong: incomplete requests (underspecified, false presupposition, incomprehensible), unsupported requests (modality, length, or temporal limitations), indeterminate requests (unknowns and subjective matters), and humanizing requests (anthropomorphizing the model). Each category defines its own noncompliant-but-not-refusal response standard, ranging from asking for clarification to providing multi-perspective answers to acknowledging capability limits. The evaluation shows that frontier models have substantial compliance gaps in these non-safety categories: GPT-4 incorrectly complies with 29.8% of incomplete requests (highest among the frontier models tested: GPT-4, GPT-4o, Claude-3 Sonnet, Llama-3-70B) and 14.1% of indeterminate requests (the highest for that category across all tested models). Llama-2-70B reaches 40.8% compliance on unsupported requests, the single highest per-category compliance score in the benchmark. Safety requests show lower compliance overall, though some models like Mistral 7B reach 28.1%. The training experiments compare four strategies starting from Llama-2 7B: (1) full SFT from scratch on Tulu2Mix combined with CoCoNot, (2) continued SFT of already-instruction-tuned Tulu-2, (3) continued LoRA on Tulu-2, and (4) DPO on a contrastive preference set (CoCoNot-Pref). The key finding is that full continued SFT collapses general capability (AlpacaEval win-rate drops from 73.9% to 18.7%) and leads to severe over-refusal on the contrast set (compliance drops to 31.4%). LoRA finetuning retains both general capability and appropriate noncompliance. DPO on the contrast preference set then corrects residual over-refusal without undermining the noncompliance gains. The paper does not report calibration metrics such as ECE or AUROC.

- Continued LoRA on Tulu-2 7B (CoCoNot): MMLU-0 50.0, AlpacaEval win-rate 74.2%, closely matching SFT baseline (50.4 / 73.9); full continued SFT on CoCoNot alone drops AlpacaEval win-rate to 18.7% (Table 3)
- SFT from scratch on T2M(all)+CoCoNot (Llama-2 7B): Incomplete compliance 5.3%, Safety compliance 1.0%, Humanizing compliance 0.0%; contrast-set compliance drops to 74.9%, indicating over-refusal of benign requests (Table 3)

- DPO on CoCoNot-Pref applied on top of Tulu-2-no-refusal LoRA: Incomplete compliance 17.3%, Safety compliance 12.3%, contrast-set compliance 89.1% (Table 3)

## Relevance to experiment

CoCoNot is directly relevant to Phase 1 of the experiment in three ways. First, it provides empirical baselines for the noncompliance compliance-rate problem across five categories, showing that frontier models fail on incomplete and indeterminate requests at rates (up to 30%) that the Phase 1 SFT vs DPO study aims to address. Second, the training comparison maps onto the Phase 1 design space: the paper tests SFT, continued SFT, LoRA, and DPO in sequence, finding that LoRA is the preferred first stage and DPO on a contrast preference set is the preferred corrective, which matches the SFT-then-DPO architecture the experiment is built around. Third, the over-refusal finding quantifies the alignment tax incurred by noncompliance training: contrast-set compliance drops from 92.4% (SFT baseline) to 31.4% under full continued SFT, but recovers to 90.8% under LoRA, a concrete benchmark for evaluating whether Phase 1 training arms preserve appropriate compliance on benign inputs. The paper does not report calibration metrics (ECE, selective-classification AUC, AUROC) and does not populate effects.csv directly. It also uses compliance rate (proportion of prompts answered directly) rather than a calibration-theoretic measure, so it addresses behavioral abstention rather than confidence calibration per se.
