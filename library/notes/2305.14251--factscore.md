## Abstract

Evaluating the factuality of long-form text generated by large language models (LMs) is non-trivial because (1) generations often contain a mixture of supported and unsupported pieces of information, making binary judgments of quality inadequate, and (2) human evaluation is time-consuming and costly. In this paper, we introduce FACTSCORE, a new evaluation that breaks a generation into a series of atomic facts and computes the percentage of atomic facts supported by a reliable knowledge source. We conduct an extensive human evaluation to obtain FACTSCOREs of people biographies generated by several state-of-the-art commercial LMs -- InstructGPT, ChatGPT, and the retrieval-augmented PerplexityAI -- and report new analysis demonstrating the need for such a fine-grained score (e.g., ChatGPT only achieves 58%). Since human evaluation is costly, we also introduce an automated model that estimates FACTSCORE using retrieval and a strong language model, with less than a 2% error rate. Finally, we use this automated metric to evaluate 6,500 generations from a new set of 13 recent LMs that would have cost $26K if evaluated by humans, with various findings: GPT-4 and ChatGPT are more factual than public models, and Vicuna and Alpaca are some of the best public models. FACTSCORE is available for public use via `pip install factscore`.

## Summary

Min et al. introduce FActScore, a fine-grained factuality evaluation that decomposes a long-form generation into atomic facts (short, single-claim sentences) and measures the fraction supported by a reference knowledge source, typically Wikipedia. The motivation is that binary holistic judgments are inadequate because even a single sentence from ChatGPT contains a mix of supported and unsupported claims in roughly 40% of cases. Human annotators evaluated biographies generated by three commercial systems across 183 people entities: InstructGPT scores 42.5%, ChatGPT 58.3%, and retrieval-augmented PerplexityAI 71.5%. Despite having search access, PerplexityAI still fails: its FActScore drops by a relative 50% at the atomic level and 64% at the sentence level for rare entities versus frequent ones, and its inline citations are nearly uncorrelated with factual correctness (36.0% of supported vs. 37.6% of unsupported sentences carry citations). The paper also shows that ChatGPT and PerplexityAI raise precision partly by abstaining (14.2% and 9.3% of prompts), which FActScore by design excludes from its denominator, creating a precision-recall tension. To enable large-scale evaluation without human cost, the authors develop automated estimators combining Wikipedia retrieval with a verifying language model. The best configuration (Retrieve then LM plus a no-premise filter using Inst-LLAMA as evaluator) achieves error rates of 1.4% on InstructGPT-subject generations and 0.4% on ChatGPT-subject generations, meeting the paper's stated threshold of under 2% for those conditions. A 13-model evaluation of 6,500 generations that would have cost $26K in human annotation reveals GPT-4 and ChatGPT outperform all public models, with Vicuna 13B and Alpaca among the best open alternatives.

## Relevance to experiment

FActScore is the primary automated factuality metric used across most downstream fine-tuning work in this vault, including the DPO-on-factuality-pairs arm and the conservative-reward-model results. Three implications for the locked training-regimen and mechinterp work are worth holding. First, FActScore measures precision only, not recall: a model that abstains more will appear more factual under FActScore even if its factual output is unchanged. Any locked training-regimen arm comparison must report abstention rates alongside FActScore to avoid confounding an abstention effect with a factual-precision effect. Second, entity rarity is a systematic confounder: if training or evaluation data over-represents frequent entities, FActScore will be inflated relative to deployment conditions where users ask about rarer subjects. Evaluation set entity-frequency distributions should be documented. Third, the paper shows single-retrieval automated estimators can overestimate FActScore by up to 17 percentage points in conditions where the model is confidently wrong; the no-premise filter materially reduces this overestimation and should be used in any automated FActScore pipeline in this vault.

## Claims

- Decomposing generated text into atomic facts and measuring the fraction supported by a reference knowledge source gives a more discriminating factuality signal than holistic binary judgments, because 40% of ChatGPT sentences mix supported and unsupported claims within a single sentence. (Section 3.1, Figure 1) [[atomic-fact-decomposition]]
- All three commercial LMs have substantially below-perfect factuality on biography generation (InstructGPT 42.5%, ChatGPT 58.3%, PerplexityAI 71.5%), and partial abstention by ChatGPT and PerplexityAI inflates their precision scores relative to InstructGPT which abstains only 0.5% of the time. (Table 1, Section 3.4) [[factscore]]
- PerplexityAI FActScore drops by a relative 50% at the atomic level and 64% at the sentence level for rare versus frequent entities even with retrieval access, contradicting the assumption that search engines make factual precision robust to entity rarity. (Figure 2 top, Section 3.4) [[entity-rarity-degrades-factual-precision]]
- PerplexityAI inline citations are nearly uncorrelated with factual correctness (36.0% of supported vs. 37.6% of unsupported sentences carry citations), showing that citation presence is not a reliable signal of atomic-fact accuracy. (Section 3.4) [[factscore-biography-benchmark]]
- A retrieval-augmented automated estimator with a no-premise filter achieves error rates of 1.4% and 0.4% on InstructGPT-subject and ChatGPT-subject conditions respectively; without the filter, single-retrieval estimators can overestimate FActScore by up to 17 percentage points. (Table 3, Section 4.2) [[factscore]]
