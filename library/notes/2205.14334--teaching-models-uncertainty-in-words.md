## Abstract

We show that a GPT-3 model can learn to express uncertainty about its own answers in natural language -- without use of model logits. When given a question, the model generates both an answer and a level of confidence (e.g. "90% confidence" or "high confidence"). These levels map to probabilities that are well calibrated. The model also remains moderately calibrated under distribution shift, and is sensitive to uncertainty in its own answers, rather than imitating human examples. To our knowledge, this is the first time a model has been shown to express calibrated uncertainty about its own answers in natural language. For testing calibration, we introduce the CalibratedMath suite of tasks. We compare the calibration of uncertainty expressed in words ("verbalized probability") to uncertainty extracted from model logits. Both kinds of uncertainty are capable of generalizing calibration under distribution shift. We also provide evidence that GPT-3's ability to generalize calibration depends on pre-trained latent representations that correlate with epistemic uncertainty over its answers.

## Summary

Lin, Hilton, and Evans show that GPT-3 can be finetuned to generate calibrated confidence estimates as natural language text, a technique they call verbalized probability, without any access to internal logits. The key claim is that this is the first demonstrated case of a model expressing calibrated uncertainty about its own answers in natural language. To test this, the authors introduce CalibratedMath, a suite of 21 programmatically generated arithmetic sub-tasks divided into an Add-subtract training split and two held-out evaluation splits: Multi-answer (with multiple valid answers and a label-distribution shift, where GPT-3's median accuracy rises to 65% vs. 21% on Add-subtract) and Multiply-divide (with unique correct answers but content that is more difficult for GPT-3 than Add-subtract). Training uses 100 questions per sub-task, roughly 10k inputs total, with labels derived from GPT-3's empirical per-sub-task accuracy. In the verbalized-words setup, the paper tried ordered terms ("lowest" through "highest") but found that random meaningless names ("john", "sam", "matt", "dan", "tom") mapped to the same five probability intervals performed slightly better, and those names are what the final implementation trains with. Finetuned verbalized-numbers achieves MSE 22.0 on Multi-answer, where both logit methods fail: answer logit scores 37.4 and indirect logit scores 33.7. On Multiply-divide, logit methods actually outperform verbalized numbers in MSE (best: answer logit 10.4), so the OOD advantage for verbalized probability is specific to the label-distribution-shift split, not universal. The paper rules out two simple explanations for verbalized calibration: it cannot be fully explained by learning to read out logit information, and a logistic regression with hand-crafted difficulty heuristics (digit count, operator, number format) performs worse than verbalized probability on both evaluation sets. The most theoretically relevant finding for mechanistic work is that a linear probe on GPT-3 embeddings achieves MSE 14.0 and MAD 14.2 on Multiply-divide without any calibration-specific training, and that 50-shot in-context learning reaches calibration performance close to the finetuned model trained on over 2.5k examples. Both results are interpreted as evidence that GPT-3 pretraining encodes latent epistemic structure that finetuning activates rather than creates.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is the primary empirical anchor for verbalized calibration as a trainable, generalizable signal in the Epistemic-Humility project. Three connections matter for the experiment design. First, the data-efficiency floor: 50-shot ICL reaches calibration performance close to an SFT model trained on 2.5k examples, which sets a realistic lower bound for how much labeled data the locked training-regimen SFT arm needs to move the needle beyond what few-shot prompting already achieves. If a cheap prompting baseline already approaches finetuned performance, the study needs to justify the SFT annotation cost against that bar. Second, the distribution-shift methodology: CalibratedMath's Add-subtract-to-Multi-answer design is the template for OOD calibration evaluation in the locked training-regimen study. The key insight is that the hard test for verbalized calibration is a label-distribution shift (where model accuracy changes substantially between train and eval), not merely a content shift. locked training-regimen eval should include an analogous held-out split where model accuracy on the target domain differs from the training distribution. Third, the mechinterp connection: the linear-probe result on GPT-3 embeddings (MSE 14.0 OOD without calibration training) is the upstream mechanistic evidence that pretrained representations already encode epistemic structure. This backs the hypothesis driving mechanism program that activation-space probes should find calibration-relevant directions before and after training, and that finetuning changes how those directions are used rather than creating them. One nuance to preserve from the verifier: the statement that "logit methods collapse under distribution shift" overstates the evidence. Logits underperform on Multi-answer (the label-distribution-shift split) but outperform verbalized numbers on Multiply-divide. The failure mode is specific to the label-distribution shift, which is the operationally relevant case for the locked training-regimen experiment where training and deployment distributions will diverge.
