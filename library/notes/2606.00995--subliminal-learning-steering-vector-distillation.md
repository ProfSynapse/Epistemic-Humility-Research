## Abstract

Subliminal learning refers to a student language model acquiring a teacher's traits (e.g. a system-prompted preference for owls) when fine-tuned on the teacher's outputs, despite the outputs being semantically unrelated to those traits. It remains poorly understood how data without semantic meaning can transfer specific semantic traits. In this work, we show that subliminal learning is mediated by a single steering vector, i.e. a vector added to the model's activations. Across two open-source models, we find that the teacher's system prompt is well approximated by a steering vector, and that the student's behavior is driven by learning an aligned vector over fine-tuning. System prompts that are not well approximated by steering vectors are not subliminally learned. This is a special case of steering vector distillation, in which a student trained on the outputs of a steered teacher learns to imitate that steering. We demonstrate steering vector distillation on a range of semantic and random vectors. Adding a semantic vector to a model's activations can have both model-independent and model-specific (i.e. non-semantic) effects on its behavior, so generated data that is non-semantic can transmit a vector with semantic effects, enabling subliminal learning. This also explains why subliminal learning does not transfer between models. We find that adaptive optimizers are necessary for subliminal learning in language models: activation gradients on steered data carry a small but consistent component along the steering direction, and non-adaptive optimizers impede this by allowing outlier gradients to dominate.

## Summary

The paper argues that subliminal learning in language models is a special case
of steering vector distillation. A teacher's trait-inducing system prompt is
approximated by a residual-stream direction. Fine-tuning a student on filtered,
semantically unrelated teacher outputs installs an aligned direction in the
student. Interventions show that the teacher and student directions are partly
necessary and sufficient for trait transfer.

The authors generalize the process by generating training data from models
steered with semantic, random, and sparse-autoencoder vectors. Students learn
the vectors by activation and loss metrics, while overt trait transfer occurs
only for semantically effective vectors. Low-rank training and adaptive
optimizers produce the strongest and most reliable effect.

## Extracted numbers

- Main students used rank-8 LoRA with alpha 32 on 10,000 samples for two epochs
  (Section 2.1 and Appendix A.3).
- Replacing the student's learned direction with the reference-model component
  removed more than 50 percent of trait-aligned behavior (Section 3.2,
  Figure 3).
- Across four traits whose inference-time steering rate was 0.0 percent,
  subliminal learning also failed (Section 4.1, Figure 5a).
- Steering-vector distillation runs reached empirical activation similarity
  above 0.5 within the first 1,000 training steps for all tested traits
  (Appendix J, Figure 17).
- Adding the teacher vector reduced number-completion NLL per token from 0.7273
  to 0.6823, closing about 32 percent of the gap to the prompted teacher
  (Appendix M, Figure 20).

## Relevance to experiment

This paper directly demonstrates weight-level internalization of an
inference-time steering direction. The strongest result uses LoRA rather than
full fine-tuning. Full fine-tuning still distills vectors by activation metrics,
but more weakly and less consistently. Its necessity and sufficiency tests also
provide controls for separating a learned internal direction from behavioral
correlation.

## Claims

- Evidence label: causal intervention. Teacher and student residual-stream
  directions are partly necessary and sufficient for subliminal trait transfer
  (Sections 3.2 and 3.3, Figures 3 and 4).
- Evidence label: generalization experiment. Students trained on outputs from
  steered teachers learned semantic, random, and sparse-autoencoder directions,
  while behavioral transfer required semantically effective vectors
  (Section 5, Figure 6).
- Evidence label: optimizer intervention. Adaptive per-parameter scaling
  enabled the steering-aligned signal to survive outlier gradients, while the
  tested SGD settings failed despite matched loss (Section 6 and Appendix L,
  Figures 7 and 19).
- Evidence label: cross-model analysis. Steering directions reduced loss most
  reliably on the source model's outputs, while cross-variant OLMo-3 transfer
  remained positive (Section 4.2 and Appendix H, Figures 5b and 16).
