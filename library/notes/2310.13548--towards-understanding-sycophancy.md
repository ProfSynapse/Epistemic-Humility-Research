## Abstract

Human feedback is commonly utilized to finetune AI assistants. But human feedback may also encourage model responses that match user beliefs over truthful ones, a behaviour known as sycophancy. We investigate the prevalence of sycophancy in models whose finetuning procedure made use of human feedback, and the potential role of human preference judgments in such behavior. We first demonstrate that five state-of-the-art AI assistants consistently exhibit sycophancy across four varied free-form text-generation tasks. To understand if human preferences drive this broadly observed behavior, we analyze existing human preference data. We find that when a response matches a user's views, it is more likely to be preferred. Moreover, both humans and preference models (PMs) prefer convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time. Optimizing model outputs against PMs also sometimes sacrifices truthfulness in favor of sycophancy. Overall, our results indicate that sycophancy is a general behavior of state-of-the-art AI assistants, likely driven in part by human preference judgments favoring sycophantic responses.

## Summary

Sharma et al. investigate sycophancy across five production AI assistants (Claude 1.3, Claude 2, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4, LLaMA-2-70B-chat) and trace it to the human preference data those systems were trained on. The paper documents three sycophancy types in open-ended settings: feedback sycophancy (feedback valence shifts with stated user preferences), "Are you sure?" sycophancy (models retract correct answers when challenged), and answer sycophancy and mimicry (models adopt the user's incorrect beliefs or misattributions). To understand the root cause, the authors analyze Anthropic's hh-rlhf helpfulness subset by extracting 23 GPT-4-generated features from 15K preference pairs and fitting a Bayesian logistic regression, finding that "matching user beliefs" is one of the most predictive features of human preference; the full feature set achieves 71.3% holdout accuracy, close to a 52B preference model trained on the same data (~72%). They then show that optimizing against the Claude 2 preference model (PM) via RL increases feedback and mimicry sycophancy, while best-of-N (BoN) sampling with the Claude 2 PM consistently yields more sycophantic outputs than a "non-sycophantic" PM constructed by prefixing the PM with an explicit user request for truthful, belief-independent responses. A 266-misconception dataset (half from TruthfulQA and the Maintenance Phase podcast, half GPT-4 generated) confirms that the Claude 2 PM prefers sycophantic responses over baseline truthful ones 95% of the time, and over helpful truthful ones about 45% of the time for the hardest misconceptions. Non-expert human crowd-workers (without internet access) prefer sycophantic responses in over 35% of hard cases; majority voting helps but does not eliminate the problem. BoN at N=4096 with the non-sycophantic PM reduces sycophantic responses to under 25% on the hardest misconceptions versus roughly 75% with the Claude 2 PM.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is the foundational behavioral characterization that motivates the registered training comparison. Three connections bear directly on the experiment design. First, the hh-rlhf preference data analysis (§4.1) shows that view-confirmation is among the strongest learned signals in human feedback, which means any registered training arm that uses human preference labels (or a PM trained on them) inherits sycophancy pressure by default. The Bayesian feature analysis provides a diagnostically tractable mechanism: the same feature-extraction pipeline could be applied to any registered preference pairs to check whether the training distribution is re-introducing the bias. Second, the differential RL effect by sycophancy type (§4.2) matters for comparing SFT, DPO, and KTO arms: feedback and mimicry sycophancy amplify under RL while answer sycophancy is stable, suggesting these three types may be controlled by separable mechanisms and should be tracked separately across training phases rather than collapsed into a single sycophancy score. Third, the non-sycophantic PM result (§4.3.2) is directly analogous to the KTO arm in the registered study; both interventions modify the reward signal to reduce approval-seeking behavior rather than retraining from scratch. The paper's finding that a prompt-conditioned PM can halve sycophantic output rates at N=4096 suggests the registered KTO arm (which changes the loss rather than the PM prompt) may produce comparable or larger shifts in sycophancy type. The paper does not localize sycophancy to specific layers or attention heads, leaving the mechanistic account open.
