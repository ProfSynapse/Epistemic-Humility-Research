## Abstract

Optimization-based latent reasoning improves large language model outputs by optimizing instance-specific continuous states at test time while keeping model parameters frozen. Existing methods, however, typically connect these states to the reasoning trajectory through decoded tokens, making sequence-level credit assignment indirect and obscuring how latent updates shape subsequent reasoning. We introduce GradCuit (gradient through circuit), which inserts optimizable latent states at a selected Transformer layer between the hidden representations of the prompt and the generated continuation. Causal self-attention provides every continuation-token log-probability with a differentiable path to every preceding latent state through the remaining Transformer blocks, enabling reward-weighted gradients from the entire continuation to be assigned directly to the latents. Across five instruction-tuned backbones, three reasoning benchmarks, and two answer formats, GradCuit achieves an average accuracy of 64.5%, outperforming chain-of-thought prompting by 6.6 percentage points and the strongest competing method by 2.4 points. GradCuit also demonstrates greater robustness: across seven learning-rate settings, it consistently outperforms LatentSeek while reducing the standard deviation of accuracy from 1.53 to 0.82, and even its random-walk variant remains competitive with LatentSeek. For interpretability, token-level gradient attribution reveals that latent influence concentrates on reasoning-connector tokens, while layer analysis identifies early-to-middle Transformer layers as the most effective optimization space. By directly optimizing internal reasoning from outcome feedback, GradCuit opens a new axis of robust and interpretable test-time scaling, where LLMs adapt how they reason rather than merely regenerate, sample, or rerank outputs.

## Summary

GradCuit is a test-time optimization method that freezes the language model and
optimizes only an instance-specific offset to a short prefix's hidden states at
an intermediate Transformer layer. Unlike output-side latent optimization, its
inserted states remain inside the causal self-attention graph, so the
log-probability of every continuation token is differentiable with respect to
every preceding latent state. A binary self-verifier supplies a sequence-level
reward, and Adam updates the latent offset for at most ten iterations while the
model greedily regenerates after each update.

The evaluation spans five instruction-tuned Llama and Qwen backbones,
GPQA-Diamond, GSM8K, and MATH-500, and both Boxed and JSON answer formats. The
method improves mean answer accuracy over chain-of-thought and several
sampling, reflection, and latent-optimization baselines. Its gains split into
two components: direct exploration of a selected-layer latent space already
helps under random directions, while reward-derived gradients add a further
benefit. Gradient attribution indicates that optimized latents have their
largest first-order influence on reasoning-connector tokens, and layer analysis
places the strongest optimization sites between 25% and 50% of network depth.

The paper does not evaluate abstention, calibration, hallucination, or
unanswerable questions. Its reward is the backbone's own binary final-answer
verdict, with no confidence term. Correct answers receive 0; incorrect answers
and outputs from which no valid final answer can be extracted both receive -1.
Its relevance to epistemic humility is therefore methodological: it exposes an
inference-time write interface and a token-to-latent credit pathway that can be
tested with humility-aware rewards and readouts.

## Relevance to experiment

### What the paper establishes

GradCuit establishes that intermediate hidden-state prefixes are a workable
test-time optimization interface for final-answer reasoning under a binary
self-verifier. It also establishes that reward gradients contribute beyond the
inserted prefix and random exploration, that the interface is less sensitive to
learning-rate choice than the tested output-side baseline, and that
continuation-token sensitivity is largest for rule-defined reasoning
connectors. These claims are bounded to three answerable reasoning benchmarks,
one fixed seed per configuration, and final-answer accuracy.

### Candidate epistemic-humility tests

1. **Humility-aware latent reward, high value and medium feasibility.** Replace
   the correctness-only reward with a preregistered selective-prediction reward
   that distinguishes correct answers, warranted abstention, confident error,
   and unnecessary abstention. Evaluate on mixed answerable and unanswerable
   questions with selective risk, coverage, calibration, and accuracy. This is
   an inference from GradCuit's write interface, not a result in the paper. It
   directly tests whether credit-assigned latent updates can improve when to
   answer rather than only how to reach an answer. The need follows from
   Appendix A.1, where the verifier sees only the question and extracted final
   answer, maps accepted/rejected answers to 0/-1, and also assigns -1 when no
   valid final answer can be extracted.

2. **Readout trajectory audit, high value and medium feasibility.** At every
   latent update, record independent answerability and correctness readouts,
   generated confidence, verifier verdict, and final truth. Test whether a
   successful update moves an internal correctness signal before the output
   changes, or merely finds a trajectory the self-verifier accepts. This is an
   inference that combines the paper's differentiable latent trajectory with
   the research program's readout vocabulary. The paper itself reports only
   accuracy and token-gradient norms, not epistemic-state trajectories
   (Sections 3.2 and 3.4).

3. **Epistemic token credit map, medium-high value and high feasibility.** Extend
   the Figure 4 token categories with abstention and uncertainty markers such as
   `I do not know`, `uncertain`, `cannot determine`, and confidence numerals.
   Compare token-to-latent gradient norms across answerable, unanswerable,
   correct, and incorrect strata. This is an inference from the attribution
   instrument in Section 3.4, not evidence that GradCuit already targets
   humility-related tokens.

4. **Verifier perturbation and exploit audit, high value and medium-low
   feasibility.** Compare oracle, self-verifier, noisy, label-permuted, random
   direction, and no-update arms, scoring every output against held-out truth.
   This tests whether accuracy and humility gains survive reward corruption or
   expose verifier exploitation. It is motivated by the paper's strong
   random-direction result (Section 3.3, Figure 3) and by the fact that the main
   experiments use one self-verifier and one fixed seed (Appendix A.1), but the
   proposed corruption controls are new.

The first two tests are the best next step. Together they ask whether this new
write interface can improve selective behavior while preserving an independent
account of what the model internally registers as answerable and correct.
