## Abstract

Humans use introspection to evaluate their understanding through private internal states inaccessible to external observers. We investigate whether large language models possess similar privileged knowledge about answer correctness, information unavailable through external observation. We train correctness classifiers on question representations from both a model's own hidden states and external models, testing whether self-representations provide a performance advantage. On standard evaluation, we find no advantage: self-probes perform comparably to peer-model probes. We hypothesize this is due to high inter-model agreement of answer correctness. To isolate genuine privileged knowledge, we evaluate on disagreement subsets, where models produce conflicting predictions. Here, we discover domain-specific privileged knowledge: self-representations consistently outperform peer representations in factual knowledge tasks, but show no advantage in math reasoning. We further localize this domain asymmetry across model layers, finding that the factual advantage emerges progressively from early-to-mid layers onward, consistent with model-specific memory retrieval, while math reasoning shows no consistent advantage at any depth.

## Summary

The paper asks whether a model's own question representations predict its answer correctness better than representations from peer models processing the same question. It defines the premium gap as self-probe AUC minus the strongest external-probe AUC. Full-set comparisons show little reliable self advantage because models usually succeed and fail on the same questions. The paper therefore evaluates, without retraining, on test items where the target and source models have opposite correctness labels. On these disagreement subsets, a modest self advantage appears consistently for factual recall but not for mathematical reasoning. Layerwise analysis places the factual advantage from early-to-middle layers onward, with the gap generally growing at greater depth.

## Extracted numbers

- The three main decoder models agreed on correctness for approximately 72 to 83 percent of questions across the five datasets, leaving disagreement subsets near 20 percent of the original data (Figure 4; Appendix B).
- On full factual test sets, only Gemma-2-9B had positive premium gaps across all three datasets: 0.017 on Mintaka, 0.018 on TriviaQA, and 0.021 on HotpotQA. Other target models had mixed or negative gaps (Figure 3).
- On factual disagreement subsets, all nine main linear-probe configurations had significant positive gaps. Gemma-2-9B scored 0.023, 0.034, and 0.025; Llama-3.1-8B scored 0.032, 0.018, and 0.020; Qwen-2.5-7B scored 0.016, 0.015, and 0.015 across Mintaka, TriviaQA, and HotpotQA (Figure 3).
- MLP probes reproduced significant positive gaps in all nine factual disagreement configurations, while mathematical disagreement gaps remained absent under both probe types (Section 4.3; Appendix A.2, Figure 7).
- In factual tasks, the layerwise premium gap becomes reliably positive around layers 10 to 15, or normalized depth 0.25 to 0.40. MATH remains near zero and GSM1K is predominantly negative across depth (Section 5, Figure 5).

## Relevance to experiment

The peer-model baseline tests whether an internal correctness readout contains model-specific information or only question difficulty shared across models. The disagreement-subset design isolates cases where peer correctness cannot serve as a positive proxy. The findings also warn that a readout advantage can be domain-specific. This is a correlational probe study. It does not test whether the target model uses the factual signal to select, revise, answer, or abstain, and it performs no weights-level training.

## Claims

- Evidence label: confound analysis. High inter-model correctness agreement lets a peer probe predict target correctness through shared difficulty and peer-specific success signals, so full-set parity does not establish the absence of private target information (Section 4.2).
- Evidence label: held-out subset comparison. Probes trained on full data are evaluated on target-source disagreement items, avoiding the perfect negative-label shortcut that disagreement-only training would create (Section 3.3).
- Evidence label: cross-model result. Self-probes beat all external probes on every main factual disagreement configuration, but show no consistent advantage on MATH or GSM1K (Section 4.3, Figure 3).
- Evidence label: depth localization. The factual premium gap grows from early-to-middle layers toward later layers, while no layer provides a consistent math advantage (Section 5, Figure 5).
- Evidence label: robustness. A one-hidden-layer MLP probe and a Qwen-3-32B extension preserve the main domain-specific pattern (Appendix A).
- Evidence label: limitation. The study identifies predictive information, not its causal role. The authors propose activation steering as future work (Sections 6 and 7).
