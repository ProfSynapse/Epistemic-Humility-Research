## Abstract

Empowering large language models to accurately express confidence in their answers is essential for trustworthy decision-making. Previous confidence elicitation methods, which primarily rely on white-box access to internal model information or model fine-tuning, have become less suitable for LLMs, especially closed-source commercial APIs. This leads to a growing need to explore the untapped area of black-box approaches for LLM uncertainty estimation. To better break down the problem, we define a systematic framework with three components: prompting strategies for eliciting verbalized confidence, sampling methods for generating multiple responses, and aggregation techniques for computing consistency. We then benchmark these methods on two key tasks-confidence calibration and failure prediction-across five types of datasets (e.g., commonsense and arithmetic reasoning) and five widely-used LLMs including GPT-4 and LLaMA 2 Chat. Our analysis uncovers several key insights: 1) LLMs, when verbalizing their confidence, tend to be overconfident, potentially imitating human patterns of expressing confidence. 2) As model capability scales up, both calibration and failure prediction performance improve. 3) Employing our proposed strategies, such as human-inspired prompts, consistency among multiple responses, and better aggregation strategies can help mitigate this overconfidence from various perspectives. 4) Comparisons with white-box methods indicate that while white-box methods perform better, the gap is narrow, e.g., 0.522 to 0.605 in AUROC. Despite these advancements, none of these techniques consistently outperform others, and all investigated methods struggle in challenging tasks, such as those requiring professional knowledge, indicating significant scope for improvement. We believe this study can serve as a strong baseline and provide insights for eliciting confidence in black-box LLMs.

## Summary

Xiong et al. establish the first systematic benchmark for black-box confidence elicitation in large language models, covering three method families (verbalized, consistency-based, and hybrid), four models (GPT-3, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Vicuna), and eight datasets spanning five task types (commonsense, arithmetic, symbolic, ethics, professional knowledge). The core finding is that vanilla verbalized confidence is severely miscalibrated across all tested models: ECE averages range from 37.7 to 52.0 (x100) for GPT-3, GPT-3.5, and Vicuna, with GPT-4 improving to 18.0 but still achieving only 62.7% average AUROC, far below useful discrimination. The paper traces this to a corpus imitation effect: LLMs learn to express confidence in the 80-100% range because human training text concentrates confidence expressions at high, round values regardless of accuracy. Replacing verbalized confidence with self-consistency AUROC jumps from 54.8% to 92.7% on GSM8K arithmetic, a roughly 38-point gain driven by the convergence property of correct arithmetic answers across samples. The paper's central methodological proposal is the hybrid approach (verbalized-consistency confidence), which adjusts each consistency score upward when a sampled answer agrees with the original and penalizes it when the sampled answer switches to a different response, using the candidate's verbalized probability as the adjustment weight. Hybrid (self-consistency) achieves the best average AUROC of 74.5% across five datasets on GPT-3.5, and hybrid methods win 13 of 20 evaluated metric-dataset comparisons across all four metrics. Separately, hybrid methods dominate on AUPRC-Positive across all five datasets, two independent claims the paper makes in §1 and §5.4. Model scale correlates positively with calibration: from GPT-3 to GPT-4 there is an approximately 22.2% relative improvement in AUROC under vanilla verbalized confidence (absolute gap 11.4 percentage points, from 51.3% to 62.7%). Professional-knowledge tasks remain a hard ceiling: even the best method on Professional Law reaches only 65.6% AUROC (self-consistency) and ECE of 12.8 (x100, hybrid/induced), both well below ideal.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides the foundational black-box confidence elicitation baseline for the Phase 1 experiment. Three connections are direct. First, the verbalized and consistency signals benchmarked here are exactly the readout candidates for the SFT vs DPO vs KTO arms: after each training run, ECE and AUROC on the same method families can characterize whether abstention training sharpens or dulls calibration relative to these baselines. The overconfidence finding (vanilla verbalized ECE averaging above 37% on models comparable to study targets) motivates including calibration metrics alongside abstention rate in the effects.csv schema. Second, the professional-knowledge ceiling finding gives the Phase 1 study a hard upper-bound expectation: even optimal black-box methods plateau near 65% AUROC on Professional Law, so any gains from abstention training in that regime need to be interpreted against that ceiling. Third, the relative scale improvement (roughly 22% AUROC gain from GPT-3 to GPT-4 under vanilla verbalization) supports the hypothesis that model capability is a lever for confidence quality, which means that comparing Qwen3 4B and 8B arms in Phase 1 is methodologically grounded. The hybrid method (verbalized-consistency confidence) is a candidate add-on probe for Phase 3: after mech-interp identifies where epistemic signal concentrates in hidden states, a hybrid confidence readout could test whether post-training shifts that readout. One caution: all evaluations here are in-distribution closed-book QA on GPT-era models; generalization to Qwen3-class instruction-tuned models after SFT or preference optimization is an open question the Phase 1 arms are positioned to test.
