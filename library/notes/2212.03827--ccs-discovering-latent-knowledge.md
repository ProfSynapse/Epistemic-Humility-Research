## Abstract

Existing techniques for training language models can be misaligned with the truth: if we train models with imitation learning, they may reproduce errors that humans make; if we train them to generate text that humans rate highly, they may output errors that human evaluators can't detect. We propose circumventing this issue by directly finding latent knowledge inside the internal activations of a language model in a purely unsupervised way. Specifically, we introduce a method for accurately answering yes-no questions given only unlabeled model activations. It works by finding a direction in activation space that satisfies logical consistency properties, such as that a statement and its negation have opposite truth values. We show that despite using no supervision and no model outputs, our method can recover diverse knowledge represented in large language models: across 6 models and 10 question-answering datasets, it outperforms zero-shot accuracy by 4\% on average. We also find that it cuts prompt sensitivity in half and continues to maintain high accuracy even when models are prompted to generate incorrect answers. Our results provide an initial step toward discovering what language models know, distinct from what they say, even when we don't have access to explicit ground truth labels.

## Summary

Burns et al. introduce Contrast-Consistent Search (CCS), an unsupervised method that recovers a model's latent knowledge directly from its hidden-state activations, bypassing what the model says in its outputs. The core insight is that truth satisfies a structural property that few other internal features share: a statement and its negation must carry complementary probabilities. CCS exploits this by forming contrast pairs (a question answered both as "Yes" and "No"), normalizing their representations to remove surface differences, and then learning a linear probe whose output satisfies two consistency constraints simultaneously: the negation-consistency loss (probabilities for a statement and its negation must sum to one) and the confidence loss (predictions must be decisive rather than hedging at 0.5). No labels, no model outputs, and no weight updates are used.

Evaluation spans 6 models (RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-J, T5, UnifiedQA, T0) and 10 classification and QA datasets (IMDB, Amazon, AG-News, DBpedia-14, RTE, QNLI, COPA, Story-Cloze, BoolQ, PIQA). Averaged across the 5 non-T0 models and 10 datasets, CCS reaches 71.2% accuracy versus 67.2% for calibrated zero-shot, while cutting prompt-sensitivity standard deviation roughly in half (3.2 vs 6.1). A misleading-prefix stress test shows calibrated zero-shot on UnifiedQA drops 9.5 percentage points, while CCS maintains accuracy. Cross-task transfer experiments demonstrate that a CCS probe trained on Amazon sentiment transfers with an average accuracy of 71.8%, slightly above the no-transfer baseline, indicating a task-agnostic truth representation in the activations. The supervised logistic regression ceiling sits at 83.7%, quantifying the remaining gap between unsupervised latent-knowledge extraction and a labeled upper bound. The companion Contrastive Representation Clustering (CRC) variants, which cluster the differences in normalized hidden states rather than optimizing a loss, reach 69.2% (TPC) and 69.8% (BSS), confirming that truth is a salient, easily-discoverable feature of the representation space even without optimization.

## Relevance to experiment

CCS is the founding paper for latent-knowledge reading from activations, which is the conceptual anchor for the Phase 3 mechanistic-interpretability sweeps in this project. Three connections matter directly.

First, CCS demonstrates that a linear probe constrained by negation consistency can recover truth-relevant signal that the model's own outputs fail to express. That is the empirical grounding for the Phase 3 hypothesis: after SFT or DPO abstention training in Phase 1, the model's hidden states may encode uncertainty information that the trained output behavior does not fully reflect. Running CCS probes on Qwen3 before and after each training arm is a concrete diagnostic that the method supports.

Second, CCS is the direction estimator that ITI (2306.03341) uses as one ablation baseline, where it scores 33.4% True*Informative on TruthfulQA versus mass-mean probing at 42.3%. This positions CCS in the probe-method comparison: it is a zero-label method that occupies the slot between naive zero-shot and supervised probing, making it a natural comparison point for any mech-interp probe fitted on Phase 1 arms.

Third, the generation-discrimination gap that CCS quantifies (model activations encode what is true while model outputs do not reliably express it) is the same gap that motivates this project's epistemic-humility framing. CCS does not model abstention as a behavioral output, so it does not directly populate `effects.csv`, but it establishes that the internal signal exists, which is what makes measuring activation-output divergence after training a meaningful research question rather than a speculative one.
