## Abstract

Comparing learned neural representations in neural networks is a challenging but important problem, which has been approached in different ways. The Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) similarity metric, particularly its linear variant, has recently become a popular approach and has been widely used to compare representations of a network's different layers, of architecturally similar networks trained differently, or of models with different architectures trained on the same data. A wide variety of conclusions about similarity and dissimilarity of these various representations have been made using CKA. In this work we present analysis that formally characterizes CKA sensitivity to a large class of simple transformations, which can naturally occur in the context of modern machine learning. This provides a concrete explanation of CKA sensitivity to outliers, which has been observed in past works, and to transformations that preserve the linear separability of the data, an important generalization attribute. We empirically investigate several weaknesses of the CKA similarity metric, demonstrating situations in which it gives unexpected or counter-intuitive results. Finally we study approaches for modifying representations to maintain functional behaviour while changing the CKA value. Our results illustrate that, in many cases, the CKA value can be easily manipulated without substantial changes to the functional behaviour of the models, and call for caution when leveraging activation alignment metrics.

## Claims

- Evidence label: theoretical. Translating a subset of a set of
  representations along an arbitrary direction (subset translation) provably
  drives linear CKA toward a value determined only by the translated
  fraction and the representations' effective dimensionality, independent of
  whether the translation preserves the data's local structure (Theorem 1).
- Evidence label: theoretical. This subset-translation result specializes to
  explain two previously only-empirical weaknesses: sensitivity to a single
  outlier point (Corollary 3) and sensitivity to translations that preserve
  linear separability and margin size (Corollary 4).
- Evidence label: empirical manipulation. A trained network's CKA map (its
  matrix of pairwise inter-layer CKA values) can be optimized toward almost
  any target map, including forcing high similarity between its first and
  last layer or minimizing similarity between all layers, while degrading
  test accuracy by at most about 1.5 percentage points (Section 4.3, Figures
  5-7).
- Evidence label: counter-intuitive baseline. Early layers of a
  task-generalizing network, a label-memorizing network, and a randomly
  initialized network report high mutual CKA, despite having visibly
  different and unequally useful learned convolution filters (Section 4.1,
  Figures 2-3).

## Summary

Davari, Horoi, Natik et al. formally characterize why linear Centered Kernel
Alignment (CKA), the dominant representation-similarity measure in recent
deep learning work, can be actively misleading. CKA is provably invariant
only to orthogonal transformations (rotation, permutation, reflection) and
isotropic scaling; the paper's Theorem 1 shows it is highly sensitive to a
much broader and more mundane class of transformations, subset translations,
where a subset of the representations is shifted a distance c along an
arbitrary direction while the rest stay fixed. As c grows, the linear CKA
value between the original and translated representations converges to a
closed form depending only on the untranslated fraction and the
representations' effective (participation-ratio) dimensionality, regardless
of whether the translation preserves the representations' local geometry.
Corollary 3 specializes this to a single translated point (an outlier),
explaining previously only-empirically-observed CKA sensitivity to outliers
(Ding et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2021, 2022); Corollary 4 specializes it to a
translation that preserves the exact separating hyperplane and margins
between two linearly separable classes, showing CKA can report near-zero
similarity between representations a linear classifier would treat as
essentially unchanged.

Empirically, Section 4.1 first shows CKA can also mislead in the opposite
direction: early layers of a generalizing CIFAR-10 network, a network trained
to memorize random labels, and a randomly initialized network all report
high mutual CKA, yet their learned convolution filters are visibly different
and, by a linear-probe usefulness check, not equally informative. Section 4.2
runs the theoretical subset-translation result on both synthetic cube data
and real CIFAR-10 CNN representations: CKA values drop below 0.2 for both
linear and RBF kernels well before the translation distance is large, even
though the translation exactly preserves the two classes' linear
separability and margins. Section 4.3 goes further and directly optimizes a
network's layer-by-layer CKA map toward an arbitrary target via a joint
distillation-plus-CKA-map loss, with a dynamically adjusted weighting that
prioritizes preserving accuracy: the resulting networks can match the target
CKA map (forcing high similarity between the first and last layer, or across
all layers, or minimizing similarity across all layers, or even matching
deliberately "comical" target maps) while test accuracy moves by roughly 0.2
to 1.6 points from a baseline near 85.9-93.9% depending on the architecture.
The paper explicitly does not conclude CKA is useless, but argues any
similarity-measure conclusion needs to be checked against these known failure
modes, especially when the process that produced the compared models is not
known or controlled (e.g. comparing open-sourced pretrained checkpoints).

  linear and RBF CKA between two sets of points sampled from linearly
  separable unit cubes drop below 0.2 well before the translation distance
  needed to disrupt separability, for a translation that preserves local
  structure and separability by construction.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper grounds why a correctness-subspace-overlap estimator cannot treat
a single similarity score (CKA or any other off-the-shelf measure) as
self-certifying. Theorem 1 and its corollaries show that plausible,
functionality-preserving changes to a set of representations (a translated
subset, a single moved outlier, a margin-preserving class shift) can drive
linear CKA toward zero even though nothing about what the network computes
has changed, and Section 4.3 shows the reverse failure mode too: a network's
CKA map can be optimized to match nearly any target, including forcing high
similarity between clearly different layers, while barely moving accuracy.
Both directions of failure mean a bare CKA value between two fitted
correctness subspaces (e.g. across training stages, the same comparison the
correctness-direction-rotation cell needed for cosine) cannot be trusted
without a reference point for what the measure reports under conditions
where the true overlap is known.

The direct methodological takeaway for the correctness-subspace-overlap
design is to never report a raw similarity number alone: pair every observed
statistic with a disjoint-split reliability estimate (what does the same
estimator report when comparing two independently fitted subspaces on
disjoint data splits of the same distribution, which upper-bounds how
reproducible the "true" overlap could look) and a permutation-null baseline
(what does the estimator report for subspaces that share no real structure,
e.g. fit on shuffled labels). This paper's own within-condition experiments
are effectively hand-built versions of exactly that comparison, showing how
far a bare similarity score can drift from ground truth even when the
comparison is constructed to be innocuous. The new mechanism
[[cka-similarity-manipulable-without-functional-change]] records this
finding, and the new atom [[centered-kernel-alignment]] anchors the measure
itself for reuse if a future ingest needs to invoke CKA directly rather than
by way of its known failure modes.
