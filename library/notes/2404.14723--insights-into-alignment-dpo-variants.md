## Abstract

This study evaluates Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and its variants for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences, testing three configurations: (1) with Supervised Fine Tuning (SFT), (2) without SFT, and (3) without SFT but using an instruction tuned model. We further investigate how training set size influences model performance. Our evaluation spans 13 benchmarks covering dialogue, reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, question answering, truthfulness, MT-Bench, Big Bench, and the Open LLM Leaderboard. We find that: (1) alignment methods often achieve near optimal performance even with smaller subsets of training data; (2) although they offer limited improvements on complex reasoning tasks, they enhance mathematical problem-solving; and (3) using an instruction tuned model improves truthfulness. These insights highlight the conditions under which alignment methods excel, as well as their limitations.

## Summary

Saeidi et al. (2024) run the most systematic cross-scenario evaluation of RL-free alignment variants to date, comparing DPO, KTO, IPO, and CPO on Mistral-7B across 13 benchmarks and three experimental configurations: (1) fine-tuning an SFT-warmed model (Scenario 1), (2) skipping SFT and aligning a raw pre-trained model (Scenario 2), and (3) skipping SFT and aligning an instruction-tuned model (Scenario 3). The three-way scenario design reveals effects that single-scenario studies miss. In Scenario 1, KTO leads across most academic benchmarks but none of the alignment methods surpass the SFT-only baseline on MMLU (alignment scores 59.14--59.88, SFT baseline 60.92 from Table 1). Data-volume experiments (Figure 3) show all four methods peak at 1K--10K training examples and degrade beyond that, contradicting the usual assumption that more preference data helps. In Scenario 2, CPO and KTO match Mistral+SFT on MT-Bench dialogue quality, while DPO and IPO fall short without the SFT warm-up (Figure 1). The most striking finding comes from Scenario 3: replacing the SFT warm-up with an instruction-tuned base (Mistral-Instruct-7B-v0.2) roughly doubles the TruthfulQA gain from alignment -- KTO improves TruthfulQA by approximately 9.5 percentage points over the SFT baseline from the pre-trained base (Table 9, Appendix B) but by approximately 17.5 percentage points over the SFT baseline when starting from the instruction-tuned base (Table 3). The instruction-tuned base does not, however, confer general advantage: reasoning and MMLU performance are comparable across scenarios. Training used AdamW without weight decay, no LoRA, 6 A100 GPUs, bfloat16, 1 epoch, peak LR 5e-7, 10% warmup, global batch size 8, and beta=0.1 (Appendix A prose). The paper does not measure calibration, ECE, or abstention rate.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides direct empirical scaffolding for the Phase 1 SFT vs DPO vs KTO experiment. Three implications stand out. First, the method-ordering result (KTO leads in Scenario 1, CPO and KTO match SFT in Scenario 2) gives a prior on what to expect from the abstention training arms: if KTO's inductive bias from prospect theory helps on academic benchmarks without SFT, the same loss-aversion asymmetry may help the model resist over-abstention or over-answering, though this paper does not measure abstention rate directly. Second, the base-model choice turns out to matter as much as the alignment method for truthfulness: the instruction-tuned base doubles the TruthfulQA gain (17.5 pp vs 9.5 pp). If the Phase 1 experiment uses a pre-trained or lightly SFT-warmed starting point, it should expect substantially smaller truthfulness effects than experiments that start from an instruction-tuned base. Third, the training-data saturation at 1K--10K points is a practical constraint: Phase 1 runs do not need large preference datasets, and may benefit from deliberately small data budgets. The paper reports no calibration metric, no ECE, and no abstention rate, so it does not populate effects.csv but it does constrain the design space and expectations for those metrics.
