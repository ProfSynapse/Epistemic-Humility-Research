## Abstract

Like students facing hard exam questions, large language models sometimes guess when uncertain, producing plausible yet incorrect statements instead of admitting uncertainty. Such "hallucinations" persist even in state-of-the-art systems and undermine trust. We argue that language models hallucinate because the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty, and we analyze the statistical causes of hallucinations in the modern training pipeline. Hallucinations need not be mysterious -- they originate simply as errors in binary classification. If incorrect statements cannot be distinguished from facts, then hallucinations in pretrained language models will arise through natural statistical pressures. We then argue that hallucinations persist due to the way most evaluations are graded -- language models are optimized to be good test-takers, and guessing when uncertain improves test performance. This "epidemic" of penalizing uncertain responses can only be addressed through a socio-technical mitigation: modifying the scoring of existing benchmarks that are misaligned but dominate leaderboards, rather than introducing additional hallucination evaluations. This change may steer the field toward more trustworthy AI systems.

## Summary

Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang (2025) offer a unified theoretical account of why language models hallucinate at both training stages. The core move is a reduction from generative language modeling to binary classification: any base model implicitly solves an Is-It-Valid (IIV) problem by thresholding its output probabilities, and Theorem 1 (Section 3.2) shows that the generation error rate is lower-bounded by twice the IIV misclassification rate, minus a ratio of max error-set size to min valid-set size, minus a calibration deviation term delta. The simpler prompt-free version (Corollary 1, Section 3.1) drops the ratio term. The delta term equals the magnitude of the cross-entropy loss gradient with respect to a probability rescaling, so any model near a local minimum of the cross-entropy objective will have small delta, meaning calibration and hallucination are structurally coupled for pretrained base models. Theorem 2 (Section 3.3.1) instantiates this bound for arbitrary facts: the hallucination floor equals the singleton rate (fraction of training prompts appearing exactly once) minus a term that shrinks with response-set size and sample count. Corollary 2 (Section 3.3.2) shows any trigram model on two symmetric prompts must err at least half the time. For post-training, Observation 1 proves formally that under any binary grading rubric IDK responses are strictly suboptimal, and a survey of ten popular benchmarks (Table 2, Section 4.1) finds nine award zero IDK credit and one (WildBench) gives only partial credit whose rubric may still score IDK below a hallucinated answer. The proposed fix is confidence-target scoring (Section 4.2): append a penalty of t/(1-t) per wrong answer to each question prompt and report across t = 0, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, converting existing leaderboard benchmarks rather than adding new hallucination evaluations. The paper is purely theoretical; no fine-tuning experiments, datasets, or detection systems are trained or evaluated.

- Arbitrary-facts lower bound (Theorem 2, Section 3.3.1): hallucination rate >= singleton rate - 1/min_c|valid response set_c| - C/sqrt(N) - delta, where singleton rate is the fraction of training prompts appearing exactly once (Theorem 2, Section 3.3.1)
- Trigram model lower bound: any trigram model on two symmetric prompts has generation error rate >= 1/2 (Corollary 2, Section 3.3.2)
- Benchmark survey: 9 of 10 evaluated popular benchmarks use binary grading and award zero IDK credit; WildBench is the sole exception with partial credit, and its rubric may score IDK (3-4 out of 10) lower than a response with hallucinations (5-6 out of 10) (Table 2, Section 4.1; Appendix F)
- Confidence-target scoring: penalty t/(1-t) per wrong answer; answering is optimal iff correctness probability > t; t=0 recovers binary grading, t=0.5 sets penalty 1, t=0.75 sets penalty 2, t=0.9 sets penalty 9 (Section 4.2)
- GPT-4 pretrained base model is well calibrated on multiple-choice queries; RL-finetuned version shows calibration degradation (reprinted from OpenAI 2023a Figure 8, Figure 2, Section 3.1)

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides the theoretical scaffolding for the locked training-regimen experiment's central claim: that training procedures, not just knowledge gaps, drive hallucination. Three connections are direct. First, Theorem 2 establishes a non-trivial theoretical floor on hallucination equal to the singleton rate in the pretraining corpus, which means that any cross-entropy-trained base model enters post-training already constrained by a data-coverage lower bound the experiment cannot escape. This sets a principled baseline against which locked training-regimen effect sizes should be interpreted. Second, the calibration-coupling argument (delta shrinks at a cross-entropy optimum, but post-training objectives such as RLHF degrade calibration as shown in Figure 2) implies that training arms which modify the cross-entropy objective may produce models whose calibration deviates from the base model; the paper does not analyze DPO or KTO directly, but the inference that RL-style post-training introduces calibration deviation is citable from Figure 2 and Section 3.1. Third, the benchmark-survey finding (Table 2) explains why leaderboard pressure suppresses abstention behavior: models trained under standard evaluation incentives will learn that IDK is strictly suboptimal, which is precisely the misalignment the locked training-regimen IDK-SFT and preference-optimization arms attempt to correct. The confidence-target scoring proposal in Section 4.2 could serve as an evaluation protocol for the locked training-regimen study outputs: running the held-out test sets at t = 0.5, 0.75, and 0.9 would provide a richer characterization of each arm's epistemic behavior than binary accuracy alone. The paper does not produce intervention effect sizes and does not populate effects.csv.

## Claims

- Any base model whose output probabilities are near a cross-entropy local minimum will have small calibration deviation delta; Corollary 1 then implies that hallucination rate is at least twice the IIV misclassification rate minus a ratio term, making errors structurally inevitable for well-trained base models. (Corollary 1, Section 3.1; calibration argument Section 3.1) [[cross-entropy-calibration-couples-to-hallucination]]
- In the Arbitrary Facts setting, any learning algorithm using N training samples produces a model with hallucination rate at least the singleton rate minus 1/min_c|valid response set_c| minus C/sqrt(N) minus delta; the singleton rate is therefore a theoretical floor on hallucination in the pretrained base model. (Theorem 2, Section 3.3.1) [[singleton-rate]]
- Under binary grading, IDK responses are strictly suboptimal for any belief distribution over correct answers; nine of ten surveyed popular benchmarks use binary grading, and WildBench's partial-credit rubric may still score IDK below a hallucinated response. (Observation 1, Section 4.1; Table 2, Section 4.1; Appendix F) [[binary-grading-reinforces-hallucination]]
- Confidence-target scoring -- stating a penalty of t/(1-t) per wrong answer in the prompt -- makes abstention strictly optimal when correctness probability falls below t, converting existing benchmarks into hallucination-sensitive evaluations without introducing new leaderboards. (Section 4.2) [[confidence-target-scoring]]
- GPT-4's pretrained base model is well calibrated on multiple-choice queries, but the RL-finetuned version shows calibration degradation, consistent with the theoretical prediction that post-training objectives deviating from cross-entropy will increase delta. (Figure 2, Section 3.1 (reprinted from OpenAI 2023a Figure 8)) [[rlhf-degrades-conditional-calibration]]
