## Abstract

Given the broad capabilities of large language models, it should be possible to work towards a general-purpose, text-based assistant that is aligned with human values, meaning that it is helpful, honest, and harmless. As an initial foray in this direction we study simple baseline techniques and evaluations, such as prompting. We find that the benefits from modest interventions increase with model size, generalize to a variety of alignment evaluations, and do not compromise the performance of large models. Next we investigate scaling trends for several training objectives relevant to alignment, comparing imitation learning, binary discrimination, and ranked preference modeling. We find that ranked preference modeling performs much better than imitation learning, and often scales more favorably with model size. In contrast, binary discrimination typically performs and scales very similarly to imitation learning. Finally we study a `preference model pre-training' stage of training, with the goal of improving sample efficiency when finetuning on human preferences.

## Summary

Askell et al. (Anthropic, 2021) define alignment operationally as Helpful, Honest, and Harmless (HHH) and study prompting as a baseline alignment technique before comparing it to training-based objectives. In Section 2, they show that a hand-written HHH prompt improves alignment-relevant evaluations (HHH preference evals, TruthfulQA, toxicity) with benefits that grow with model size and negligible performance cost ("alignment tax") on large models. They then introduce context distillation: given a prompt context C that conditions the model's output distribution to P(X | C), a fresh copy of the model is fine-tuned to match that conditional distribution directly, using a loss equal to the KL divergence between P(X | C) and the student's own predictions P(X), so the prompt's effect is baked into weights and no longer needs to occupy context at inference time. Comparing fully HHH-prompted 13B and 52B models against their context-distilled versions in a human Elo-style rating study, contractors preferred the prompted model over the distilled one only about 53% of the time, close to chance, indicating the distilled model reproduces nearly all of the prompted model's behavior with only weak evidence of degradation. A much shorter one-example prompt performed clearly worse than either the full prompt or its distillation. Beyond prompting, the paper compares imitation learning, binary preference discrimination, and ranked preference modeling as training objectives, finding ranked preference modeling scales more favorably with model size than imitation learning or binary discrimination, and introduces preference model pre-training (PMP) to improve finetuning sample efficiency on human preference data.

## Extracted numbers

Extracted from arXiv HTML render at library/fulltext/2112.00861.html; verified against the source 2026-08-15.

- Context distillation formulation: the prompt context C conditions the model's distribution to P(X | C); a fresh model is fine-tuned with a loss equal to the KL divergence between P(X | C) and the distilled model's own predicted distribution, so it directly models P(X | C) without the prompt present at inference time (Section 2, list item 2)
- Human preference comparison, 13B and 52B: contractors preferred the fully HHH-prompted model over its context-distilled counterpart about 53% of the time (near chance), with 1-sigma error bars reported; this is described as weak evidence that context distillation degrades performance somewhat relative to the full prompt (Section 2.2, following Figures 9-10)
- A much shorter prompt (a single example conversation) performed noticeably worse than either the full HHH prompt or its context-distilled version at the same model size (Section 2.2)
- Prompting effect on capability: small models' capabilities (NLP/coding evaluations) are diminished by the presence of the prompt, but larger models perform at roughly the same level with or without the prompt (Section 2, list item 3)
- On the adversarial TruthfulQA MC1 evaluation, the context-distilled prompt slightly improves performance for the largest models relative to no prompt (Section 2.2)

## Relevance to experiment

This is the earliest weights-vs-prompt equivalence experiment relevant to the trained-vs-prompted abstention question: context distillation directly tests whether training a model to match a prompt's conditional distribution reproduces that prompt's behavior, and the near-53%-vs-47% human preference result shows the answer is "almost, with a small measurable gap." That gap-not-zero result is a useful base rate for how much residual difference to expect between prompted and trained behavior in general, against which any abstention-specific residual (see [[prompt-cannot-override-rlvr-abstention-deficit]]) can be compared: is the abstention-training gap larger than this generic context-distillation gap, or of the same order? The KL-divergence-to-a-prompted-teacher formulation is also a directly reusable objective if a future locked training-regimen cell wants to test training a model to internalize an abstention-inducing prompt rather than compare it against SFT/DPO/KTO trained from scratch.
