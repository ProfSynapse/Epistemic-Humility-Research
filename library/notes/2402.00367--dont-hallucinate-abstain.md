## Abstract

Despite efforts to expand the knowledge of large language models (LLMs), knowledge gaps -- missing or outdated information in LLMs -- might always persist given the evolving nature of knowledge. In this work, we study approaches to identify LLM knowledge gaps and abstain from answering questions when knowledge gaps are present. We first adapt existing approaches to model calibration or adaptation through fine-tuning/prompting and analyze their ability to abstain from generating low-confidence outputs. Motivated by their failures in self-reflection and over-reliance on held-out sets, we propose two novel approaches that are based on model collaboration, i.e., LLMs probing other LLMs for knowledge gaps, either cooperatively or competitively. Extensive experiments with three LLMs on four QA tasks featuring diverse knowledge domains demonstrate that both cooperative and competitive approaches to unveiling LLM knowledge gaps achieve up to 19.3% improvements on abstain accuracy against the strongest baseline. Further analysis reveals that our proposed mechanisms could help identify failure cases in retrieval augmentation and pinpoint knowledge gaps in multi-hop reasoning.

## Summary

Feng et al. introduce the AbstainQA framework and a systematic benchmark for LLM abstention from factually questionable answers. The paper adapts 11 existing approaches from four families (calibration-based, training-based, prompting-based, self-consistency-based) and evaluates them alongside two new multi-LLM collaboration methods across three LLMs (Mistral-7B, LLaMA2-70B, ChatGPT) and four QA datasets (MMLU, Knowledge Crosswords, Hellaswag, Propaganda). The evaluation uses four metrics: Reliable Accuracy (R-Acc), Effective Reliability (ER), Abstain Accuracy (A-Acc), and Abstain F1 (A-F1). The central finding is that single-LLM approaches suffer from confirmation bias and cannot reliably self-reflect on their own errors, motivating two collaboration-based alternatives. Cooperate solicits natural-language feedback from peer LLMs and synthesizes it into an abstain decision; Compete pits the target model against adversarial peers generating conflicting answers and triggers abstention when the model changes its answer under social pressure. Together, these two methods outperform all baselines in 9 of 12 evaluation settings and achieve up to 19.3% improvements in abstain accuracy against the strongest baseline. Among the adapted baselines, calibration-based approaches outperform training-based, which outperform consistency-based, which outperform prompting-based approaches. Instruction tuning achieves competitive in-domain abstain accuracy but collapses entirely when transferred to different datasets or LLMs (up to 33.8% drop). Further analysis shows that Compete identifies retrieval-augmentation failure cases and that the multi-LLM approaches substantially reduce the incorrect-answer rate in multi-hop reasoning.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is the direct conceptual ancestor of the Phase 1 abstention study. The AbstainQA framework and its four metrics (R-Acc, ER, A-Acc, A-F1) provide a ready-made evaluation scaffold for comparing SFT, DPO, and KTO abstention training arms: A-Acc and R-Acc together reveal whether a training method improves the decision to abstain rather than merely raising answer accuracy on attempted items.

The instruction-tuning collapse finding is an active caution for the Phase 1 SFT arm. Training an SFT model to abstain on one dataset may produce A-Acc drops as large as 33.8% when evaluated on a different domain or a different base model. This is the primary risk vector for the Phase 1 SFT arm and motivates including cross-dataset generalization checks in the evaluation protocol.

Compete is operationally attractive as a post-training probe. Because it requires no labeled uncertainty data and no held-out set, it can be applied to any of the three Phase 1 training arms after fine-tuning to generate an abstention signal for comparison against the trained model's own abstention behavior. The 56.6% retrieval error-rate reduction also suggests a downstream integration experiment combining the Phase 1 fine-tuned models with a retrieval step gated by the Compete abstention signal.

The Phase 3 mechanistic interpretation work is informed by the paper's finding that single-LLM self-reflection fails because the model cannot exit its own representation space to evaluate its own answer. This is a functional framing of the same failure mode the Phase 3 sweeps investigate at the activation level: does the model's internal state actually encode the right abstention decision, even when the output does not express it? The contrast between calibration-based methods (which read internal probability signals) and prompting-based methods (which ask the model to self-report) maps directly onto the probe-versus-output-signal distinction in the Phase 3 design.
