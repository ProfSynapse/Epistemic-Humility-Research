## Summary

Kalai and Vempala (2023) prove a statistical lower bound showing that pretrained language models satisfying a natural generative calibration condition must hallucinate at a rate close to the monofact estimate: the fraction of facts appearing exactly once in training data. The argument does not depend on architecture, training objective, or data quality. Under a sparsity and regularity assumption on the factoid distribution (all unobserved factoids are, on average, equally likely to be true), Corollary 1 gives: for any algorithm, with probability at least 1-delta, the LM hallucination rate is at least the monofact estimate minus the miscalibration rate minus 3e^{-s} (where s is the sparsity parameter) minus sqrt(6 ln(2/delta)/n). The bound is tight in the sense that Lemma 3 exhibits a calibrated algorithm whose hallucination rate exceeds the monofact estimate by at most 3*sqrt(ln(2/delta)/n) (coefficient 3 is outside the root). The Good-Turing connection is central: Corollary 6 of the paper (proved by the authors, building on prior concentration results of McAllester and Ortiz 2003) confirms the monofact estimate is within O(sqrt(1/n)) of the true missing mass for any distribution. A key corollary of the theory is a distinction between fact types: for arbitrary facts (5W-style, whose truth is statistically undetermined from training data) the monofact rate can be high, whereas for reference-type facts that recur many times in training (p >> 1/n) the missing mass is low and there is no statistical necessity to hallucinate. The paper illustrates via Figure 1, which shows GPT-4 calibration curves on a multiple-choice exam before and after RLHF (reproduced from the GPT-4 Technical Report), that post-training alignment visibly increases calibration error relative to the pre-RLHF model; the authors argue this is consistent with the model trading calibration for reduced hallucination, although the figure directly measures calibration on a classification task rather than generative hallucination rates.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper supplies a formal lower bound on pretrained LM hallucination rates that is directly relevant to the Phase 1 SFT vs DPO vs KTO study. Three concrete implications follow. First, the bound frames ECE and Brier score as mandatory co-metrics alongside abstention rate: the bound's miscalibration term epsilon_inf means a model can reduce apparent hallucination by becoming miscalibrated, so tracking calibration alongside abstention is essential for correctly interpreting training-arm differences rather than crediting lower hallucination to the training objective rather than to miscalibration drift. Second, the arbitrary-vs-systematic fact distinction (Section 5.1, Corollary 4) motivates stratifying evaluation by fact type: 5W-style one-shot facts and reference-type multi-occurrence facts should be reported separately, because the monofact lower bound applies only to the former and training arms may differ in how they handle each type. Third, for mechinterp work, the paper reframes the probe hypothesis: rather than asking whether a probe reads truth in the abstract, the right question is whether a probe distinguishes facts that are in-training-support (p > 0) from those that are not (p = 0, the unobserved factoid set), because it is the latter that generate the monofact-rate hallucinations the bound governs. The bound also establishes that a calibrated pretrained model that hallucinates on arbitrary facts is not failing at calibration specifically; it is behaving consistently with statistical necessity, so post-training interventions that reduce hallucination may do so by sacrificing calibration rather than by learning new facts.

## Claims

- For any algorithm on an s-sparse regular factoid distribution, the LM hallucination rate is at least the monofact estimate minus the miscalibration rate minus 3e^{-s} minus sqrt(6 ln(2/delta)/n), with probability at least 1-delta over training sets. (Corollary 1, Section 5) [[monofact-estimator]]
- The lower bound is tight: a calibrated algorithm that memorizes observed facts and assigns uniform mass to unobserved factoids hallucinates at a rate exceeding the monofact estimate by at most 3*sqrt(ln(2/delta)/n) (coefficient 3 outside the root). (Lemma 3, Section 7) [[monofact-estimator]]
- The Good-Turing estimator of missing mass is within 3*sqrt(ln(2/delta)/n) of the true missing mass with probability at least 1-delta for any distribution, a result proved in this paper as Corollary 6 building on prior concentration inequalities. (Corollary 6, Appendix A) [[monofact-estimator]]
- For reference-type facts that appear many times in training (p >> 1/n), the missing mass is low and there is no statistical necessity to hallucinate them; observed hallucinations on such facts must arise from other causes such as model capacity. (Section 9, Conclusions) [[arbitrary-vs-systematic-facts]]
- Post-training alignment reduces the calibration error increase visible in GPT-4 calibration curves on a multiple-choice exam (Figure 1), consistent with the paper's model that post-training trades calibration for reduced hallucination on arbitrary facts.
