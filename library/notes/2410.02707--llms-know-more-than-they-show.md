## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) often produce errors, including factual inaccuracies, biases, and reasoning failures, collectively referred to as "hallucinations". Recent studies have demonstrated that LLMs' internal states encode information regarding the truthfulness of their outputs, and that this information can be utilized to detect errors. In this work, we show that the internal representations of LLMs encode much more information about truthfulness than previously recognized. We first discover that the truthfulness information is concentrated in specific tokens, and leveraging this property significantly enhances error detection performance. Yet, we show that such error detectors fail to generalize across datasets, implying that -- contrary to prior claims -- truthfulness encoding is not universal but rather multifaceted. Next, we show that internal representations can also be used for predicting the types of errors the model is likely to make, facilitating the development of tailored mitigation strategies. Lastly, we reveal a discrepancy between LLMs' internal encoding and external behavior: they may encode the correct answer, yet consistently generate an incorrect one. Taken together, these insights deepen our understanding of LLM errors from the model's internal perspective, which can guide future research on enhancing error analysis and mitigation.

## Summary

Orgad et al. (2024) probe the internal representations of Mistral-7B and Llama3-8B (base and instruction-tuned variants) across ten datasets to ask three questions: where is truthfulness information stored, does it generalize across tasks, and how does what the model internally represents relate to what it actually generates? The central empirical finding is that truthfulness signal concentrates sharply at the exact answer tokens (e.g., "Hartford" in "The capital of Connecticut is Hartford, an iconic city...") rather than at the last generated token or the end-of-question token that prior work typically probed. Focusing a linear probing classifier on those exact answer tokens lifts AUC reliably for probing (0.85 to 0.95 across datasets and models), though the same focus does not uniformly help logit-based methods and can sometimes hurt them on specific dataset-model pairs (Table 1). Cross-task generalization experiments (Section 4) show that after subtracting the performance of the best logit-based baseline, the probe's apparent advantage over cross-task pairs collapses to near zero for most dataset pairs, with meaningful generalization only within clusters requiring similar underlying skills (factual retrieval, commonsense reasoning). This challenges prior claims of universal truthfulness encoding. Within a single task (TriviaQA), the paper defines a five-type error taxonomy covering 96% of Mistral-7B-instruct errors based on the distribution of answers across 30 resampled responses (Section 5.1). Probes trained on exact-answer-token representations predict which error type a given input belongs to at AUC 0.67-0.90 depending on type and model (Table 2). Finally, using the error-detection probe to select from 30 candidate answers reveals a striking internal-external discrepancy: on error types where greedy decoding never or rarely produces the correct answer (C2, D, E1), the probe-guided selection recovers 30-40 points of accuracy in some cases on TriviaQA and Math (Section 6, Figure 5), indicating that the model's representations encode the correct answer even when its generation does not express it.

## Claims

- A linear probe trained on exact-answer-token activations achieves AUC 0.85-0.95 for error detection across datasets and models, consistently outperforming probes at the last generated token, the end-of-question token, and logit-based baselines for probing. (Table 1, §3.4) [[exact-answer-token-probing]]
- After subtracting the logit-min-exact baseline, cross-task probe generalization reduces to near zero for most cross-skill dataset pairs, contradicting claims of a universal truthfulness encoding and indicating that apparent generalization reflects output-logit correlation rather than an internal representation that transcends task type. (Figure 3b, §4) [[skill-specific-truthfulness-encoding]]
- Internal representations encode fine-grained error-type information: a five-category taxonomy (refuse, consistently correct, consistently incorrect, two competing answers, many answers) covers 96% of TriviaQA errors for Mistral-7B-instruct, and per-type probes reach AUC 0.64-0.90 across models. (Table 2, §5.1, §5.2) [[error-type-taxonomy-llm]]
- Probe-guided selection from 30 resampled answers recovers 30-40 accuracy points in some cases over greedy decoding for error types where greedy decoding never or rarely produces the correct answer (C2, D, E1), revealing a significant discrepancy between internal encoding and external generation behavior. (Figure 5, §6) [[generation-discrimination-gap]]
- Models can self-extract their own exact answer with 0.95-0.99 success rate on 1000 TriviaQA samples, validating the exact-answer-token extraction pipeline used throughout the paper. (Table 3, Appendix A.2) [[exact-answer-token-probing]]
