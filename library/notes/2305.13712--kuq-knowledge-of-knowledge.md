## Abstract

This paper investigates the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) in the context of understanding their knowledge and uncertainty over questions. Specifically, we focus on addressing known-unknown questions, characterized by high uncertainty due to the absence of definitive answers. To facilitate our study, we collect a new dataset with Known-Unknown Questions (KUQ) and establish a categorization framework to clarify the origins of uncertainty in such queries. Subsequently, we examine the performance of open-source LLMs, fine-tuned using this dataset, in distinguishing between known and unknown queries within open-ended question-answering scenarios. The fine-tuned models demonstrated a significant improvement, achieving a considerable increase in F1-score relative to their pre-fine-tuning state. Through a comprehensive analysis, we reveal insights into the models' improved uncertainty articulation and their consequent efficacy in multi-agent debates. These findings help us understand how LLMs can be trained to identify and express uncertainty, improving our knowledge of how they understand and express complex or unclear information.

## Summary

Amayuelas et al. introduce the Known-Unknown Questions (KUQ) benchmark to measure whether LLMs can distinguish questions that have definitive answers from questions that are inherently unanswerable. The dataset contains 12,726 paired questions: 6,363 unknown questions drawn from three sources (229 web-crawled, 2,695 GPT-4-generated, 3,439 crowd-sourced) and 6,363 known questions from ELI5. A six-category taxonomy (Future Unknown, Unsolved Problem, Controversial, False Assumption, Counterfactual, Underspecified) captures why each unknown question lacks a definitive answer.

Three tasks are evaluated. First, binary known-vs-unknown classification reveals that frontier models (GPT-3.5-turbo zero-shot F1 0.702, GPT-4 zero-shot F1 0.652) substantially outperform open-source models (Vicuna-13B 0.407, Alpaca-7B 0.437), while a BERT model fine-tuned on the training split reaches near-perfect accuracy. Few-shot prompting produces mixed effects across models. Self-Ask prompting, where the model first generates an answer and then decides, lowers F1 for GPT-3.5-turbo and davinci-003 relative to zero-shot, but raises it for GPT-4 (from 0.652 to 0.720) and Alpaca-7B (from 0.437 to 0.659). The paper attributes the general tendency toward overconfidence under Self-Ask to the model's compulsion to produce an answer, which anchors its subsequent classification; this characterization is partially supported by the data.

Second, multi-class category classification (1-shot) shows GPT-4 at F1 0.660 with smaller models well below 0.4. Future Unknown questions are the easiest category because their linguistic markers (temporal cues) are distinctive.

Third, the paper introduces Answer Uncertainty Disparity (AUD), a normalized aggregate of three semantic features (subjectivity, hedge-phrase frequency, text-uncertainty aspect), to measure whether a model's generated language is more hedged for unknown questions than for known ones. AUD of 0.5 represents chance. Across all five models, AUD without label provision is 0.543--0.579, indicating only a marginal above-chance signal. When the prompt explicitly tells the model whether the question is known or unknown, AUD rises by 0.024--0.060 points but still remains low.

A human-in-the-loop study using GPT-3.5-turbo rationales shows that human binary accuracy starts at 0.74, drops to 0.705 after exposure to the model's unprompted rationale (the first rationale condition), and rises to 0.87 after exposure to rationales generated with the correct label given to the model. Category classification accuracy is 0.67 for humans and remains 0.67 after reading model explanations, rising to 0.83 when users judge the explanation as useful or correct.

## Relevance to experiment

KUQ bears on the Phase 1 experiment in three concrete ways.

First, it supplies the only existing benchmark purpose-built around paired known/unknown questions. After each Phase 1 training arm (SFT, DPO, KTO), KUQ's binary and multi-class tasks can be run to check whether the trained model abstains differentially on the unknown split without blanket over-refusal on the known split. The BERT fine-tuning result (near-100% accuracy) sets an upper ceiling: the task is solvable with sufficiently targeted training data, so any gap in Phase 1 arms is attributable to the training procedure rather than the intrinsic difficulty of the signal.

Second, the AUD metric operationalizes a surface-level output signal for uncertainty expression that is complementary to abstention-rate and calibration metrics. AUD is cheap to compute (TextBlob, hedge-phrase counts, a sciBERT certainty model) and could be logged per Phase 1 arm as a secondary diagnostic alongside ECE and verbalized-confidence scores. The baseline range of 0.543--0.579 across frontier models without label provision establishes what untrained behavior looks like; a training arm that genuinely installs epistemic humility should push AUD upward.

Third, the human-in-the-loop study documents a propagation risk: LLM-generated rationales that lack genuine uncertainty signal do not help humans and may slightly harm them (0.74 to 0.705 drop). This matters if Phase 1 outputs are used as rationale providers in downstream human workflows. A model with high binary classification accuracy but low AUD is producing confident-sounding rationales even for unknown questions, which the human study suggests can mislead users. Measuring AUD alongside binary F1 gives a fuller picture of whether training is installing genuine epistemic humility or just changing classification behavior while leaving output register untouched.

## Claims

- Smaller and open-source LLMs achieve near-random performance on binary known-vs-unknown classification (zero-shot F1: Alpaca-7B 0.437, Vicuna-13B 0.407), while a BERT-based model fine-tuned on the KUQ dataset achieves nearly 100% accuracy. (Table 4, Section 5.1) [[known-unknown-questions]]
- All tested LLMs exhibit minimal Answer Uncertainty Disparity (AUD ~0.55-0.58) between known and unknown questions, indicating models fail to express noticeably higher uncertainty when answering inherently unanswerable questions. (Table 6, Section 5.4) [[answer-uncertainty-disparity]]
- Future Unknown questions are the easiest category for LLMs to classify correctly because they contain distinctive linguistic features (temporal cues), while other categories prove much harder. (Section 5.2, Figure 1) [[known-unknowns-taxonomy]]
- Providing the model with explicit known/unknown label in the prompt increases AUD by approximately 0.03-0.06 points across all models, but uncertainty expression still falls far short of what would be expected for highly uncertain questions. (Table 6, Section 5.4) [[answer-uncertainty-disparity]]
