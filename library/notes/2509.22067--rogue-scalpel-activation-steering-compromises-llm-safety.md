## Abstract

Activation steering is a promising technique for controlling LLM behavior by adding semantically meaningful vectors directly into a model's hidden states during inference. It is often framed as a precise, interpretable, and potentially safer alternative to fine-tuning. We demonstrate the opposite: steering systematically breaks model alignment safeguards, making it comply with harmful requests. Through extensive experiments on different model families, we show that even steering in a random direction can increase the probability of harmful compliance from 0% to 1-13%. Alarmingly, steering benign features from a sparse autoencoder (SAE), a common source of interpretable directions, demonstrates a comparable harmful potential. Finally, we show that combining 20 randomly sampled vectors that jailbreak a single prompt creates a universal attack, significantly increasing harmful compliance on unseen requests. These results challenge the paradigm of safety through interpretability, showing that precise control over model internals does not guarantee precise control over model behavior.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experiment. Steering in a random, semantically empty direction reliably breaks refusal: harmful compliance on JailbreakBench rises from a 0% unsteered baseline to 1-13% across model families (up to 18% for Llama3.1-8B at c=2.0), with vulnerability concentrated in middle and early layers (Fig. 2, Sec 4.1).
- Evidence label: experiment. Benign SAE features are comparably or more dangerous than random directions: SAE-feature steering yields 1-4% higher compliance than matched random steering, and 817/1000 tested SAE features jailbreak at least one prompt, with the most potent features (e.g. "modal verbs", "brand identity") being semantically benign and generalizing poorly across harm categories (Fig. 5, Sec 4.2-4.3).
- Evidence label: experiment. Averaging just 20 randomly sampled vectors that jailbreak a single fixed prompt yields a universal attack vector that generalizes to the 99 unseen JailbreakBench prompts, boosting compliance roughly 4x over matched random steering (e.g. 50.4% on Llama3.1-70B; 5.7% to 63.4% on Falcon3-7B), using only black-box access and no harmful training data (Fig. 7, Sec 4.4).
- Evidence label: analysis. Harmful SAE features show near-zero cosine similarity with the known refusal direction (Arditi et al., 2024), rejecting the hypothesis that steering-induced jailbreaks work by simply suppressing a pre-existing linear refusal direction (Appx. E).
