## Abstract

For Large Language Models (LLMs) to be reliably deployed in both everyday and high-stakes domains, knowing when not to answer is equally critical as answering correctly. Real-world user queries, which can be underspecified, ill-posed, or fundamentally unanswerable, require LLMs to reason about uncertainty and selectively abstain -- i.e., refuse to answer definitively. However, abstention remains understudied, without a systematic evaluation framework for modern LLMs. In this work, we introduce AbstentionBench, a large-scale benchmark for holistically evaluating abstention across 20 diverse datasets, including questions with unknown answers, underspecification, false premises, subjective interpretations, and outdated information. Evaluating 20 frontier LLMs reveals abstention is an unsolved problem, and one where scaling models is of little use. While recent reasoning LLMs have shown impressive results in complex problem solving, surprisingly, we find that reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention (by $24\%$ on average), even for math and science domains on which reasoning models are explicitly trained. We find that while a carefully crafted system prompt can boost abstention in practice, it does not resolve models' fundamental inability to reason about uncertainty. We release AbstentionBench to foster research into advancing LLM reliability.

## Summary

Kirichenko et al. introduce AbstentionBench, a large-scale benchmark aggregating over 35,000 unanswerable questions from 20 datasets across six abstention scenarios: answer unknown, false premise, stale information, subjective, underspecified context, and underspecified intent. Scoring is done with a Llama 3.1 8B Instruct judge that reaches 88% accuracy on manually annotated responses, enabling consistent cross-dataset comparison at scale. The central finding is that abstention is an unsolved problem across all 20 frontier models tested, and that reasoning fine-tuning makes it worse: DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 70B and s1.1 32B each show an average 24% drop in abstention recall relative to their instruction-tuned base models, including on math and science datasets where reasoning models are explicitly optimized. Scaling Llama 3.1 Instruct from 8B to 405B produces almost no improvement in mean abstention recall, showing that parameter scale alone does not help. A staged post-training analysis using Tulu 3 checkpoints reveals that SFT and DPO together tend to improve abstention recall on most scenario types, but SFT introduces a sharp drop specifically on underspecified-context samples; adding an RLVR stage on top of DPO then causes a further degradation across the board. The authors hypothesize (stated explicitly as a hypothesis in Section 4.3) that RLVR degradation arises from reward model misspecification: a verifiable-correctness reward biases models toward definitive confident answers, leaving abstention unrewarded. When reasoning traces are examined, those traces contain increased uncertainty expressions on unanswerable questions, but models still commit to a definitive final answer; including the trace in judge evaluation degrades abstention precision. A carefully crafted system prompt can boost abstention recall for both standard and reasoning LLMs without significant precision loss, though the authors argue this does not resolve the models' underlying failure to reason about uncertainty.

## Relevance to experiment

AbstentionBench provides the most comprehensive existing evaluation landscape for the behavior locked training-regimen trains directly. The Tulu 3 staged ablation (SFT, DPO, RLVR) in Section 4.2 is a close structural parallel to the locked training-regimen arms and gives concrete baselines: SFT and DPO improve abstention on most scenarios but SFT specifically damages underspecified-context recall, and RLVR rolls back DPO gains. These are the patterns the locked training-regimen SFT vs DPO vs KTO comparison can be designed to detect and potentially avoid. The 24% reasoning-model degradation figure is a usable reference effect size for power calculations. The six-scenario taxonomy in Section 3.2 maps directly onto the scenario-stratified analysis the locked training-regimen evaluation plan requires, especially the underspecified-context scenario, which shows the sharpest SFT vulnerability and the most consistent cross-model failure. For mechanism work mechanistic work, the CoT finding in Section 4.3 is the key entry point: uncertainty representations are present in the reasoning trace but suppressed before the final token, which is exactly the internal-representation question the mech-interp sweeps target. The system-prompt result in Section 4.4 is a useful foil: it shows a prompt can partially recover abstention without altering internal representations, setting up the mechanistic contrast between surface-level and weight-level changes.

## Claims

- Reasoning fine-tuning hurts abstention: DeepSeek R1 (Distill Llama 70B) and s1.1 show an average 24% drop in abstention recall compared to their non-reasoning counterparts across AbstentionBench datasets. (Section 1, Fig. 6(a)) [[reasoning-fine-tuning]]
- Abstention does not improve with model scale: comparing Llama 3.1 Instruct at 8B, 70B, and 405B parameters, there is almost no effect of increasing scale on mean abstention recall over datasets. (Section 4.1, Fig. 3(b)) [[abstention]]
- A carefully crafted system prompt boosts abstention recall for both standard and reasoning LLMs without a significant degradation in abstention precision, but does not resolve the fundamental inability to reason about uncertainty. (Section 4.4, Fig. 8(b)) [[abstention]]
- Reasoning models express uncertainty in their chain-of-thought traces but still provide definitive final answers; incorporating the reasoning trace in evaluation does not improve abstention recall and degrades abstention precision. (Section 4.3, Fig. 8(a)) [[reasoning-fine-tuning]]
