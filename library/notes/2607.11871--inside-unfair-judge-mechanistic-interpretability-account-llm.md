## Abstract

Existing studies of LLM-as-judge scoring bias work predominantly at the input-output level: they perturb inputs, measure score deltas, and propose prompt-level mitigations. We argue that the same biases admit a representation-level account in the judge's hidden state, complementary to the input-output view and operationally useful in ways it does not afford. We report three findings, across seven judges, seven bias types, and nine benchmarks. Geometry: baseline judging inputs occupy a tight activation manifold while biased inputs are displaced along a low-dimensional, type-specific subspace that sharpens with depth and is recovered consistently by three families of estimators. Causal control: steering hidden states along this subspace drives scoring in both directions, forward shifts reproducing biased scoring on clean inputs and reverse shifts restoring baseline scoring on biased ones, while matched-norm random directions produce shifts an order of magnitude smaller. Operational: a simple linear projection onto the same bias-direction features anticipates judge failures on three entirely unseen benchmarks, substantially outperforming text-based alternatives. Reading bias as activation geometry, rather than as input-output noise, unifies geometric structure, causal control, and operational prediction within a single framework. The project page is available at https://xzx34.github.io/unfair-judge/

## Summary

Xu et al. study whether LLM-as-judge scoring bias has a stable internal representation rather than appearing only as input-output score noise. Across seven judges, seven bias types, and nine benchmarks, they construct matched baseline, positive-cue, and negative-cue inputs intended to preserve answer content while changing surface framing. Activation analyses on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Gemma-3-12B find that biased inputs leave a compact baseline manifold along low-dimensional, bias-type-specific directions that become clearer at deeper layers.

The paper then uses these directions as interventions. Adding a fitted bias direction to clean activations reproduces biased scoring, while subtracting it from biased activations restores scores toward baseline. Matched-norm random directions are much weaker, and swapping in another bias type's vector produces an intermediate effect. The authors therefore claim a sufficient interventional handle, not proof that the recovered direction is the judge's unique natural causal pathway.

Finally, per-layer projections onto the learned bias directions support a judge-conditional failure predictor. A linear model transfers better than a richer tree model to benchmarks excluded from direction fitting, suggesting that the compact geometric features generalize more reliably than a broader activation feature set. The activation intervention requires white-box access, and the main activation claims are limited to three mid-scale open-source judge families.

## Extracted numbers

Source: `library/fulltext/2607.11871.html`.

- Experimental scale: 4,500 baseline questions, 31,500 positive-cue variants, and 31,500 negative-cue variants, spanning seven bias types and nine benchmarks; seven judges are evaluated behaviorally and three white-box judges receive activation analysis. (Section 4.1; Appendix B.1-B.3)
- Behavioral asymmetry: aggregated negative perturbations yield Cohen's d from -0.35 to -0.65 across the seven judges, all reported at p < 0.001, while the aggregate positive response is near zero and type-dependent. (Section 4.2, Table 2)
- Bias-subspace dimensionality: variance-explained analysis estimates 3-5 effective directions per bias type at Llama-3.1-8B layer 25. Biased and baseline activation norms are not distinguishable at p > 0.1 except at the final score-readout head. (Section 4.3; Appendix L.3 and L.5, Table 24)
- Estimator agreement: six fitted vectors have cosine similarity at least 0.91 within directional or discriminative estimator families and 0.55-0.65 across families. (Section 4.4; Appendix J.3, Table 17)
- Steering controls: attack and defense retain output validity above 0.93. Matched-norm random directions produce Wasserstein-1 shifts at least one order of magnitude smaller at every tested layer; bias-type swaps are 2-2.5 times weaker than the matched within-type vector and 2.8-4.5 times stronger than random. (Section 4.4; Appendix J.1-J.2)
- Held-out defense: five-fold cross-validated defense retains at least 80% of the in-sample Wasserstein-1 reduction on held-out folds. (Section 4.4; Appendix K.1)
- Outcome prediction: the linear bias-direction projection reaches AUROC 0.82 on the three unseen benchmarks versus 0.85 in-domain and 0.63 for the zero-shot text-LLM baseline. LightGBM reaches 0.93 on the development set but only 0.75 cross-domain. (Section 4.5; Appendix M.5, Table 29)
- Sensitivity: five alternative held-out benchmark trios keep linear cross-domain AUROC within about 0.025 of the 0.82 headline value. (Appendix H; Appendix M.7)

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides a concrete evaluator-side analogue of causal activation steering: a behaviorally defined failure mode can be localized as a compact direction, challenged with matched-norm and type-swap controls, and then tested for held-out operational value. For epistemic-humility evaluations that rely on an LLM judge, the result motivates treating judge bias as a measurable confound rather than assuming the scalar score is neutral. It also supplies useful design habits for future tuner-backed cells: fit directions only on effective paired failures, separate correlational readout from bidirectional intervention, include random and wrong-type steering controls, preserve output validity and rank order, and reserve held-out domains for the operational test.

The paper does not establish that one direction is the unique natural mediator of judge bias, does not provide activation evidence for proprietary judges, and requires white-box or characterization-time activation access. Those boundaries should carry into any experiment that adapts its methods.

- Evidence label: mechanistic geometry. Biased judging inputs are displaced from a compact baseline manifold along low-dimensional, bias-type-specific directions that sharpen with depth, with an estimated 3-5 effective directions per type at layer 25. (Section 4.3, Figures 1-2; Appendix L.5, Table 24) [[judge-bias-forms-type-specific-low-dimensional-subspaces]]
- Evidence label: causal intervention. Adding and subtracting the same fitted bias direction moves judge scores toward biased and baseline behavior respectively, while matched-norm random directions are at least an order of magnitude weaker; this supports interventional sufficiency but not a unique natural pathway. (Section 4.4; Appendix J.1-J.2; Appendix K.1) [[bias-direction-steering-bidirectionally-controls-judge-scores]]
- Evidence label: cross-domain prediction. A linear projection onto per-layer bias-direction features predicts at least one-point judge degradation with AUROC 0.82 on SocialMaze, BBQ, and GPQA, which were excluded from vector estimation, compared with 0.63 for a zero-shot text-LLM baseline. (Section 4.5; Appendix M.1 and M.5, Table 29) [[bias-direction-features-predict-cross-domain-judge-degradation]]
- Evidence label: behavioral context. Negative surface cues consistently reduce judge scores across all seven tested judges, while positive cues aggregate near zero and vary by bias type. (Section 4.2, Table 2)
