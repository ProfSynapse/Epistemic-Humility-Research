## Abstract

Assessing reliably the confidence of a deep neural network and predicting its failures is of primary importance for the practical deployment of these models. In this paper, we propose a new target criterion for model confidence, corresponding to the True Class Probability (TCP). We show how using the TCP is more suited than relying on the classic Maximum Class Probability (MCP). We provide in addition theoretical guarantees for TCP in the context of failure prediction. Since the true class is by essence unknown at test time, we propose to learn TCP criterion on the training set, introducing a specific learning scheme adapted to this context. Extensive experiments are conducted for validating the relevance of the proposed approach. We study various network architectures, small and large scale datasets for image classification and semantic segmentation. We show that our approach consistently outperforms several strong methods, from MCP to Bayesian uncertainty, as well as recent approaches specifically designed for failure prediction.

## Summary

Corbiere et al. propose True Class Probability (TCP) as a theoretically grounded confidence criterion for failure prediction in deep classifiers, and introduce ConfidNet, a lightweight auxiliary head that learns to regress TCP from frozen encoder representations via MSE. The central problem is that the maximum class probability (MCP) from the softmax layer is high even on misclassifications because it always selects the largest output; TCP -- the softmax probability assigned to the ground-truth class -- is naturally low when the model is wrong. Two formal guarantees follow from the definition: TCP > 1/2 implies correct classification, and TCP < 1/K implies misclassification (K = number of classes), with minimal empirical overlap in the intermediate range for modern overconfident networks. Because the true class is unavailable at test time, ConfidNet is trained on the training set using a two-phase scheme: first the confidence head's layers are optimized with the base encoder frozen, then a separate duplicate of the encoder is fine-tuned at reduced learning rate with dropout disabled. Evaluated on MNIST MLP and SmallConvNet, SVHN SmallConvNet, CIFAR-10 VGG16, CIFAR-100 VGG16, and CamVid SegNet, ConfidNet improves AUPR-Error over MCP in every setting, with the largest gap on the simplest model (MNIST MLP, +19.7pp). Ablations confirm that MSE regression toward TCP outperforms binary cross-entropy (BCE) on failure discrimination, that encoder fine-tuning adds approximately 1-2pp AUPR-Error, and that training on the training split is generally preferable to a held-out validation set unless a large train/val accuracy gap exists. The paper also notes that TCP equals the exponential of the per-sample cross-entropy loss, making it a proper scoring rule for multi-class confidence estimation.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is the direct architectural ancestor of the aux_head confidence-readout design tested in Amendment Q and Amendment R. Three structural features transfer almost verbatim: (a) a frozen base network plus a trainable auxiliary scalar head reading its penultimate representations, (b) MSE regression toward a correctness-probability target (TCP here; binary answerability in the LLM setting), and (c) a two-phase training discipline that keeps the classifier's predictions stable by decoupling the confidence encoder from the classification encoder. The theoretical guarantee that TCP > 1/2 implies correctness maps directly onto the binary answerability framing from Amendment Q, supporting the use of a correctness-probability target rather than a calibration target for the head. The paper also motivates the leakage-guard constraint in the Phase 1 protocol: ConfidNet requires the ground-truth label at training time, and the same constraint applies to the aux_head -- the head must not have access to held-out test-set correctness labels. The paper does not test on language tasks, abstention behavior, or distribution shift, so transfer to the LLM confidence-head setting is a structural analogy rather than a confirmed empirical result. One caveat about direct comparison: ConfidNet's performance gains are largest on small, high-accuracy models where error examples are rare; LLM abstention targets involve different class-imbalance structure and much richer input representations, so the absolute magnitude of gains is not expected to transfer.
