## Abstract

Neuron- and path-level interventions offer the finest-grained route to defending large language models (LLMs) against jailbreak attacks, yet existing methods fall short of this promise, i.e., they often compromise model utility significantly. Specifically, one line of work suppresses toxic neurons to erase harmful semantics, but since such semantics are distributed across the network, blocking every pathway forces a large intervention footprint. An alternative line of research focus on identify safety neurons using external classifiers. While promising, the existing approaches suffer from compromising neurons that are important for the model utility as well. Moreover, both approaches remain always on and thus perturb every benign request even when no attack is present. To address these limitations, we present \ours{}, a training-free defense that first identifies safety-specific neurons through per-neuron hypothesis tests under false-discovery-rate control together with a utility-specificity filter. Based on this identification, a trigger-style clamp holds the selected neurons at their harmful-conditional mean activations, injecting an internal harmful-input signal that triggers the refusal behavior learned during alignment. The clamp is then realized by two provably equivalent deployment modes, namely a detector-gated inference-time intervention and an offline bias-patch weight edit. Extensive experiments across four safety-aligned LLMs and four representative attacks demonstrate that \ours{} reduces the average attack success rate to at most 2.0\% while incurring a utility drop of only 0.5\% to 5.3\% on MT-Bench, the smallest among all defenses. Code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Tripwire-65C4.

## Summary

TripWire is a training-free, neuron-level jailbreak defense built on two ideas. First, a statistically rigorous identification funnel finds a small set of "safety neurons" in the gated MLP sublayers by running, per neuron, a direction filter (mean harmful activation > mean benign activation), a Welch t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate control across all neurons, and a utility-specificity filter that drops neurons whose utility-task activation also sits close to the harmful end of the benign-to-harmful axis; survivors are ranked by AUROC and the top-N are kept. Second, a trigger-style clamp holds those neurons at their harmful-conditional mean activation throughout generation, injecting a stable internal "this input is harmful" signal that triggers the refusal behavior already learned during alignment, rather than trying to block every pathway that carries harmful semantics. The clamp is deployed in two provably equivalent modes: an inference-time mode where a lightweight logistic-regression detector over the sparse safety-neuron feature vector gates whether the clamp fires (Proposition 1 shows this is mathematically identical to an offline bias-patch weight edit that zeroes the neuron's weight row and sets its bias to the harmful-conditional mean). Across four safety-aligned LLMs and four jailbreak attacks, TripWire holds average attack success rate at or below 2.0% with the smallest utility drop (0.5%-5.3% on MT-Bench) among compared defenses, and an ablation isolates the identification step: the FDR-controlled funnel needs 3-5x fewer neurons than probe-weight z-score attribution to reach comparable safety, because probe weights are not identifiable in the p >> n regime and cannot separate safety-specific neurons from generally-important ones.

## Extracted numbers

- Average ASR at top-2500 neuron budget, inference mode: 1.1% (Llama-2-7B), 0.5% (Llama-3.1-8B), 2.0% (Qwen2.5-7B), 0.8% (Qwen2.5-32B), vs. 43.0%/33.1%/24.1%/17.2% undefended (Table 1).
- Utility drop at top-2500, inference mode: 5.3% (Llama-2), 4.7% (Llama-3.1), 0.5% (Qwen2.5-7B) on MT-Bench, the smallest among all compared defenses (Table 1, Defense Performance Analysis).
- Baseline comparison at matched setting: RepE 8.3%-12.6% average ASR with up to 45.0% utility drop; TraceRouter comparable ASR to TripWire but up to 20.0% utility drop; DELMAN/LED (edit-based) 2.8%-4.9% ASR (Table 1).
- Inference-time overhead: TripWire at most 1.15x generation time, vs. 1.16x (TraceRouter) and 1.27x (RepE) (Table 1).
- Ablation (Table 2): probe-based selection (z=3.0) needs 3,589-10,811 neurons vs. TripWire's 1,000-1,001 for a comparable safety level, and degrades MT-Bench by up to 52.3% (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B: 6.60 -> 3.15) with over-refusal up to 62.5%, vs. TripWire's over-refusal of at most 8.8% at the same neuron-count regime.
- Identification cost: 79-92 seconds per model, one-time offline analysis, not affecting inference latency (Ablation Study section).
- Safety-neuron budgets as a fraction of total neurons: 2,500/1,060,864 = 0.23% (Qwen2.5-7B), 5,000/3,538,944 = 0.14% (Qwen2.5-32B) (Experimental Setup).
- Layer concentration: safety neurons peak in layers 12-16 (Llama-2/Llama-3.1) and layers 15-21 (Qwen2.5-7B), nearly absent in early layers, under the top-2500 budget (Figure 3, Safety Neuron Distribution Analysis).

## Relevance to experiment

TripWire is a working instance of the "read a known-unknown-adjacent state, then gate a write" pattern the actuation program (paper 5) is designing toward, applied to safety refusal rather than epistemic abstention. Concretely transferable pieces: (1) the FDR-controlled, utility-specificity-filtered identification funnel as a principled alternative to probe-weight thresholding when selecting a small set of units to read a target state from, with an explicit statistical guarantee on the false-selection rate; (2) the trigger-style clamp itself, i.e. writing a fixed conditional-mean activation into a small unit set to force a target behavior, as opposed to ablating/erasing a signal; (3) the proven equivalence between an inference-time detector-gated intervention and a permanent offline weight-patch, which gives a route from a cheap prototype gate to a zero-overhead deployed checkpoint; (4) the paper's own metrics (ASR, MT-Bench utility drop, over-refusal rate, and overhead) as a template for reporting a gate's safety/utility/cost trade-off. See the METHODS-WORTH-CONSIDERING notes in the ingest report for where each piece plugs in.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experimental (Table 1, primary results). Across four safety-aligned LLMs (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B) and four jailbreak attacks (GCG, AmpleGCG, AutoDAN, Jailbreak-R1), TripWire at the top-2500 neuron budget holds average attack success rate at or below 2.0% in both its inference-gated and offline-edit deployment forms, with the smallest utility drop (0.5%-5.3% on MT-Bench) among compared defenses (RepE, TraceRouter, DELMAN, LED).
- Evidence label: ablation (Table 2). Isolating the identification step under identical always-on clamping, the FDR-controlled, specificity-filtered funnel reaches comparable or lower ASR than probe-weight z-score attribution using 3-5x fewer neurons, and costs far less utility (probe-based selection degrades MT-Bench by up to 52.3% and over-refuses up to 62.5% of benign queries), because probe weights are not identifiable in the p >> n regime and cannot separate safety-specific from generally-important neurons.
- Evidence label: theoretical + empirical confirmation (Proposition 1; Ablation Study). The inference-time clamp and the offline bias-patch weight edit are proven mathematically identical maps (zeroing a neuron's weight row and setting its bias to the harmful-conditional mean reproduces the clamp exactly), and the inference-without-detector and edit forms of TripWire are confirmed empirically to yield nearly identical ASR and MT-Bench scores.
- Evidence label: exploratory analysis (Figure 3, Safety Neuron Distribution Analysis). Identified safety neurons concentrate in middle-to-upper transformer layers (peaking around layers 12-16 for Llama-2/Llama-3.1, layers 15-21 for Qwen2.5-7B) and are nearly absent in early layers, consistent with harm detection and refusal triggering being mediated by mid-to-high-level semantic and behavioral representations rather than surface token features.
