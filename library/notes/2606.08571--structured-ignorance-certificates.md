## Abstract

Large language models frequently fail in a characteristic way: rather than acknowledging ignorance, they produce fluent but incorrect answers to questions that lie beyond their knowledge boundaries. We introduce \textbf{Structured Ignorance Certificates} (SICs), a JSON-formatted output schema that demands a model explicitly name the missing domain intersection, enumerate required concepts, and propose a productive retrieval query rather than hallucinating an answer. To train models to produce high-quality SICs we construct a 7,347-sample \emph{Unknown-Unknown} (UU) dataset by prompting Qwen3-14B to stitch together questions from seven domains (physics, biology, engineering, CS, economics, medical, legal) into novel cross-domain queries that no single-domain expert could answer. We fine-tune a 14B-parameter model with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a composite reward that combines retrieval utility, concept specificity, and output-format validity. A paraphrase-divergence probe trained on model responses confirms that SIC-tuned outputs systematically exhibit higher unknown-unknown probability scores. Evaluation on 735 held-out UU questions achieves a 99.46\% JSON validity rate, a mean Certificate Specificity Score of 0.967, and a 3.6\% ROUGE-L improvement over the base model on retrieval-grounded generation -- demonstrating that explicit epistemic structuring is a learnable and measurable capability.

## Summary

Sahoo (ICML 2026 Workshop: Epistemic Intelligence in Machine Learning) argues that unknown-unknown failure in LLMs is not merely a knowledge gap but a structural output problem: models produce fluent, confident answers even when the required knowledge lies outside any single domain they were trained on. The proposed fix is a JSON output schema called Structured Ignorance Certificates (SICs), which forces the model to name the missing domain intersection, enumerate the concepts it would need, and propose a productive retrieval query rather than hallucinate. To train this behavior, the paper constructs a 7,347-sample Unknown-Unknown (UU) dataset by prompting Qwen3-14B to stitch together questions from seven domains (physics, biology, engineering, CS, economics, medical, legal) into novel cross-domain queries that no single-domain expert could answer. A 14B-parameter model is then fine-tuned with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a composite reward combining retrieval utility, concept specificity, and output-format validity. A paraphrase-divergence probe trained on model responses confirms that SIC-tuned outputs systematically exhibit higher unknown-unknown probability scores. The central thesis is that explicit epistemic structuring is a learnable and measurable capability, not a pre-existing disposition that training only reveals.

## Relevance to experiment

SICs operationalize a specific failure mode the graph calls abstention-generalization-failure: the model does not abstain but fabricates, because it has no schema for expressing what it does not know. This paper proposes that structured output format, learned via GRPO reward shaping, can install that schema. Three connections to the experiment are direct. First, the GRPO composite-reward approach is closely related to the ternary-reward and rule-based-reward families already in the graph; comparing it to DPH-RL and TruthRL arms would test whether format constraints add anything beyond reward signal. Second, the paraphrase-divergence probe is a behavioral probe of unknown-unknown probability, sitting next to the activation-probe and gradient-probe families already documented; it would be informative to check whether SIC-tuned models also show sharper mech-interp signals in the Phase 3 sweep. Third, the evaluation is entirely in-distribution on the UU dataset the paper itself constructs; there is no out-of-domain transfer test, which is exactly the gap that the abstention-generalization-failure mechanism in the graph predicts and the Phase 1 cross-dataset transfer rows should probe.
