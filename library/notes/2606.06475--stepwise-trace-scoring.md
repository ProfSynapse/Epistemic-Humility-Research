## Abstract

Recent advancements in reasoning language models have been driven by Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning. Most often, these rely on the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm or modifications thereof to steer the models to produce Chain-of-Thought (CoT) traces. The final answer can only be verified, and the reward assigned, after the CoT trace is complete, making it a delayed reward problem. GRPO and its modifications correspond to Monte Carlo methods in standard RL, which are known to suffer from high variance. A possible solution to this problem is the redistribution of rewards through credit assignment, where segments of the CoT trace that are important for arriving at the desirable solution are emphasized by assigning a higher reward. While Monte Carlo sampling can be used to provide an unbiased estimate of intermediate state values, its computational overhead makes it unsuitable for train-time credit assignment in long contexts at high granularity. We introduce RREDCoT (Reward REDistribution for Chain of Thoughts), which utilizes the model itself to approximate the optimal reward redistribution without additional generation. We investigate the advantages of our method compared to MC sampling and several attribution methods. We further analyze several aspects relevant to the construction of the redistribution such as segmentation of CoT traces and state value estimation.

## Summary

Ielanskyi et al. introduce RREDCoT (Reward REDistribution for Chain of Thoughts), a method that addresses the delayed-reward credit-assignment problem in RL fine-tuning of reasoning language models. Standard GRPO treats the entire CoT trace as a single unit, assigning a terminal reward only after the answer is complete. This is equivalent to Monte Carlo RL and inherits its high-variance gradient estimates. RREDCoT instead segments the CoT trace, estimates an intermediate state value for each segment without any additional generation, and redistributes the terminal reward proportionally, so gradient updates emphasize the segments most responsible for a correct answer.

The key technical contribution is an importance-sampling estimator that uses the model's own log-probabilities to approximate segment-level state values. The estimator has a provable non-positive bias: it conservatively underestimates segment value, which the authors argue is preferable to overestimation that could reinforce spurious reasoning. Bias vanishes only if the proposal distribution over completions covers all non-zero-probability, non-zero-utility sequences (Equation 10, Appendix C.5). The authors analyze two approximations for the estimator, gradient-based attribution and leave-one-out (LOO) attribution, and show via Figure 4 that these two correlate with each other but diverge from Monte Carlo estimates, suggesting the estimator captures different signal than direct MC sampling.

A companion analysis of MC sampling (Figure 3) quantifies why MC is impractical at train time: computing estimates for 30 AIME-25 questions at up to 40 segments required 100 GPU-hours. By contrast, the bias-variance analysis in Section 4 shows that MC estimate standard deviation falls below 0.1 after roughly five samples per segment, which would still be prohibitively expensive at scale.

RREDCoT is evaluated on Qwen3-4B (large-scale, 25k-token budget, Numina-CoT training set) and Qwen2.5-1.5B (small-scale, 1024-token budget, open-rs training set). On Qwen3-4B, RREDCoT improves over GRPO on four of five benchmarks, with AIME24 accuracy rising from 0.850 (GRPO) to 0.908. AIME25 is the single exception where GRPO (0.600) edges RREDCoT (0.583). The small-model variant RREDCoT-Nano also surpasses the Open-RS baseline on AMC23 and MATH500. RREDCoT requires 1.5 to 2x the compute of vanilla GRPO runs (Section 5, Limitations), but is much faster than MC sampling-based credit assignment.

## Relevance to experiment

Indirect but structurally informative for Phase 1 and mechanism program. The paper does not study calibration, abstention, or hallucination, so it does not populate effects.csv under the humility-metric schema.

The segment-level credit assignment story connects to the `reasoning-training-decouples-decisiveness-from-confidence` mechanism already in the graph: if the gradient signal the model receives during RL training is redistributed to specific CoT steps, the shape of what gets reinforced changes. Whether that sharpens or further decouples internal confidence from verbal expression is an open empirical question the mechinterp sweeps could address.

The Figure 4 finding that gradient-based attribution and LOO attribution correlate with each other but diverge from MC estimates extends the `gradient-structure-encodes-output-correctness` mechanism from the Grad Detect paper. Both papers treat gradient-derived signals as carrying distinct information compared to sampling-derived signals, but neither establishes whether the gradient-based signal reads correctness or familiarity. The `probe-reads-recall-not-truth` caveat in the existing graph applies here too.

The non-positive bias of the IS estimator (conservative underestimation of segment value) is a structural analog to the asymmetric reward designs already documented in the graph (`ternary-reward-enables-abstention-over-hallucination`, `conservative-reward-model`): the design principle of erring toward under-reinforcement appears across epistemic-humility and reasoning-accuracy contexts, suggesting a unifying principle worth tracking.

## Claims

- RREDCoT redistributes terminal GRPO reward to CoT segments via an importance-sampling estimator computed from the model's own log-probabilities, raising Qwen3-4B AIME24 accuracy from 0.850 (GRPO) to 0.908 while requiring no additional generation. (Table 1) [[rredcot]]
- The importance-sampling estimator has a provable non-positive bias: it conservatively underestimates segment value under non-negative utility assumptions, and bias vanishes only when the proposal distribution covers all non-zero-probability, non-zero-utility completion sequences. (Equation 10, Appendix C.5) [[importance-sampling-underestimates-cot-value]]
- Gradient-based and LOO attribution scores correlate with each other but diverge from Monte Carlo estimates, indicating the two families of estimators capture different signal about which CoT segments drive correct outcomes. (Figure 4) [[gradient-structure-encodes-output-correctness]]
- Monte Carlo sampling of intermediate state values requires 100 GPU-hours for just 30 AIME-25 questions at 40 maximum segments, making it impractical for on-policy train-time credit assignment at this context length and granularity. (Figure 3) [[mc-sampling-credit-assignment]]
- MC estimate standard deviation falls below 0.1 after approximately 5 samples per segment, yet even this threshold is computationally prohibitive at the scale and context length required for AIME-class problems. (Section 4) [[mc-sampling-credit-assignment]]
