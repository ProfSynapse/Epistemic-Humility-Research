## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in decision-making contexts, but when they present answers without signaling low confidence, users may unknowingly act on erroneous outputs. Prior work shows that LLMs maintain internal uncertainty signals, yet their expressed confidence is often miscalibrated and poorly discriminates between correct and incorrect answers. We investigate whether supervised fine-tuning can improve models' ability to communicate uncertainty and whether such improvements generalize across tasks and domains. We fine-tune LLMs on datasets spanning general knowledge, mathematics, and open-ended trivia, and evaluate two metacognitive tasks: (1) single-question confidence estimation, where the model assigns a numeric certainty to its answer, and (2) pairwise confidence comparison, where the model selects which of two answers it is more likely to answer correctly. We assess generalization to unseen domains, including medical and legal reasoning. Results show that fine-tuning improves calibration (alignment between stated confidence and accuracy) and discrimination (higher confidence for correct vs. incorrect responses) within and across domains. However, gains are task-specific: training on single-question calibration does not transfer to pairwise comparison, and vice versa. Multitask fine-tuning yields broader gains, lowering calibration error and strengthening discrimination in out-of-domain evaluations. This suggests that uncertainty communication in LLMs is trainable but requires multitask training to generalize effectively.

## Summary

Steyvers, Belem, and Smyth ask whether supervised fine-tuning can teach LLMs to communicate their own uncertainty reliably, and whether that skill transfers across domains and across metacognitive task formats. They study two models (GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 and Llama3.1-70B-Instruct) and two metacognitive tasks: single-question confidence estimation, where the model outputs a numeric score between 0 and 1 alongside its answer, and pairwise confidence comparison, where the model selects which of two questions it is more likely to answer correctly. Training targets for the single-question task are derived from consistency scores computed over N=10 stochastic samples per question, remapped through empirical accuracy curves and perturbed with small uniform noise to discourage memorization of discrete score bins. Pairwise training targets simply label the question with the higher consistency score as the preferred choice. Three in-domain datasets (MMLU-PRO, GSM8K, TriviaQA) supply fine-tuning data; three held-out datasets (TruthfulQA, MetaMedQA, LegalBench) test cross-domain generalization.

Fine-tuning produces large within-domain gains in both calibration (ECE) and discrimination (AUC) on both tasks. Cross-domain transfer is also real: models trained on M+G+T improve AUC and reduce ECE on TruthfulQA, MetaMedQA, and LegalBench relative to baseline, though the LegalBench AUC gain (0.58 to 0.63) does not reach statistical significance and TruthfulQA ECE actually worsens slightly (0.10 to 0.18) under single-task single-question training. The most important structural finding is that cross-task transfer fails completely in both directions: a model fine-tuned only on pairwise comparison shows no improvement in single-question AUC or ECE, and a model fine-tuned only on single-question calibration shows no improvement in pairwise AUCc or AUCa. This bidirectional failure implies that the two metacognitive routines are learned as operationally distinct competencies rather than as different expressions of a shared latent uncertainty signal. Multitask fine-tuning (C+S) partially repairs this by producing broader improvements across both task formats and unseen domains, consistent with the interpretation that joint supervision encourages partially shared internal uncertainty representations. Fine-tuning never improves task accuracy, confirming that the discrimination gains reflect genuinely better uncertainty reporting rather than improved factual knowledge.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is directly relevant to the Epistemic Humility Phase 1 design in three ways.

First, the consistency-based target construction pipeline (N=10 stochastic samples, consistency score remapped through empirical accuracy curves) is a concrete implementation of the kind of self-supervised uncertainty signal the Phase 1 SFT arms could adopt. The paper shows this pipeline produces calibration-aware SFT targets without ground-truth correctness labels, which is important for domains where such labels are costly.

Second, the cross-task transfer failure result is a preregistration-relevant prediction: if Phase 1 trains abstention using a single behavioral format (e.g., verbalized refusal on SFT, preference pairs on DPO), the resulting model may not generalize its uncertainty signal to other output modes. The paper quantifies this failure precisely and shows multitask fine-tuning as the remedy, which the Phase 1 design does not currently include.

Third, MetaMedQA appears as an out-of-domain benchmark where prior work claimed LLMs lack reliable metacognitive abilities. The present paper shows fine-tuned models recover substantial AUC and ECE improvements on MetaMedQA, making it a useful held-out comparator for any Phase 1 generalization check.

One caveat applies throughout: the consistency score used as a training target measures how often the model agrees with itself across stochastic samples, not whether its answers are factually correct. The resulting calibration therefore reflects self-consistency rather than ground-truth accuracy alignment, which matters when interpreting discrimination gains and when deciding whether this pipeline would complement or substitute for ground-truth-labeled abstention training in Phase 1.

## Claims

- Single-task supervised fine-tuning on single-question confidence estimation fails to transfer to pairwise confidence comparison, and conversely, fine-tuning on pairwise comparison fails to improve single-question AUC or ECE, implying the two metacognitive routines are learned as distinct competencies rather than as expressions of a shared uncertainty signal. (Table 1 rows 10-12; Table 2 rows 10-12; Section: Generalization across confidence tasks and multitask training) [[metacognitive-task-transfer-failure]]
- Multitask fine-tuning on both single-question and pairwise comparison tasks (C+S) partially overcomes cross-task isolation, raising out-of-domain single-question AUC from 0.73 to 0.76 and out-of-domain pairwise AUCc from 0.67 to 0.73 (both p<0.05), consistent with partially shared internal uncertainty representations emerging under joint supervision. (Table 1 rows 13-15; Table 2 rows 13-15; Section: Generalization across confidence tasks and multitask training) [[metacognitive-task-transfer-failure]]
- Consistency-based fine-tuning with N=10 stochastic samples, empirical accuracy remapping, and noise regularization dramatically reduces within-domain ECE (e.g., GSM8K 0.75 to 0.04) and raises AUC (GSM8K 0.53 to 0.76) without improving task accuracy, confirming that gains reflect better uncertainty communication rather than factual improvement. (Table 1 rows 1-3; Section: Improved calibration and discrimination within domain; Methods: Generating Consistency-Based Confidence Targets) [[consistency-based-confidence]]
- Out-of-domain calibration improvement transfers to medical reasoning: MetaMedQA AUC rises from 0.71 to 0.75 and ECE falls from 0.23 to 0.18 under M+G+T single-question fine-tuning, directly challenging prior claims that LLMs lack metacognitive abilities for reliable medical reasoning. (Table 1 row 8; Section: Improved calibration and discrimination across domains; Discussion) [[metamedqa]]
- Both GPT-4.1-mini and Llama3.1-70B-Instruct baseline models cluster verbalized confidence in a narrow range (0.85, 0.90, 0.95), exhibiting systematic overconfidence; fine-tuning diversifies and calibrates this distribution without altering the narrow band of token-level probability predictions.
