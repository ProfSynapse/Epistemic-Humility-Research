## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are typically aligned to refuse harmful instructions through safety fine-tuning. A recent attack, termed abliteration, identifies and suppresses the single latent direction most responsible for refusal behavior, thereby enabling models to generate harmful content. We propose a defense that fundamentally alters how models express refusal. We construct an extended-refusal dataset in which responses to harmful prompts provide detailed justifications before refusing, distributing the refusal signal across multiple token positions. Fine-tuning Llama-2-7B-Chat and Qwen2.5-Instruct (1.5B and 3B parameters) on this dataset yields models that maintain high refusal rates under abliteration: refusal rates drop by at most 10%, compared to 70-80% drops in baseline models. Comprehensive evaluations of safety and utility demonstrate that extended-refusal fine-tuning effectively neutralizes abliteration attacks while preserving general model performance and enhancing robustness across multiple alignment scenarios.

## Summary

Abu Shairah et al. diagnose why abliteration attacks succeed so reliably against standard safety alignment, then exploit that diagnosis as a defense. The key observation is that conventional RLHF and SFT alignment trains models to produce brief, formulaic refusals: "I'm sorry, I can't help with that." Because every harmful prompt triggers nearly the same short output, the residual-stream activations are stereotyped enough that a single direction in weight space captures most of the refusal signal. Projecting that direction out (weight orthogonalization, Eq. 5) collapses refusal rates from roughly 100% to 13-21%, while leaving MMLU and perplexity nearly intact.

The defense the authors call extended-refusal fine-tuning (ERFT) changes the output form, not the weights, before abliteration. Each harmful-prompt response in their constructed dataset has three parts: a neutral topic overview, an explicit refusal, and an ethical rationale. This richer, multi-component output forces the safety signal to be distributed across many token positions rather than concentrated at the first refusal token. After fine-tuning Llama-2-7B-Chat, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on 4,289 such examples augmented with 5,711 benign Alpaca-GPT4-en pairs, the resulting models retain greater than 90% refusal rates even after abliteration, compared to 13-21% for the baselines. The MMLU cost before abliteration is small (0.6-1.3 pp); after abliteration the extended-refusal models pay a larger utility penalty (5 pp on Llama-2-7B), which the authors interpret as evidence that refusal has become more entangled with general computation: harder to excise without collateral damage.

A PCA-based feature-space analysis (Table 2) quantifies this entanglement: abliteration reduces the Euclidean centroid distance between harmful and benign hidden-state representations by 28-34 points in baseline models but only by 8-14 points in extended-refusal models, confirming that the richer output format disperses the refusal signature into a harder-to-locate subspace.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is about safety refusals, not epistemic abstention, so the fit is mechanistic rather than direct. The value for Phase 1 and mechanism program lies in the activation-geometry argument, not in any humility metric.

The core finding is that formulaic, concentrated outputs (brief refusals) create a single isolable direction; richer, multi-component outputs distribute the signal so no single direction can collapse the behavior. This generalizes: if epistemic abstention training in Phase 1 produces stereotyped "I don't know" tokens at fixed positions, it may create an analogous vulnerability to direction-based suppression (or probing). Designing abstention outputs with more semantic variety, as ERFT does for refusals, could make the abstention signal harder to edit out and easier For mechanism work probes to find in a distributed form. The centroid-distance analysis in Table 2 is a direct template for measuring how compressed vs. distributed the harmful/benign (or known/unknown) boundary is in hidden state space.

The CatQA evaluation dataset used here (550 harmful questions) is not relevant to epistemic abstention benchmarking and does not populate any Phase 1 evaluation slot.

The paper does not report calibration, ECE, or abstention-rate effects, so no effects.csv row is warranted.

(Table 1, Section 4.2) [[extended-refusal-fine-tuning-disperses-safety-signal]]

(Table 1) [[refusal-direction-mediates-refusal]]
- PCA centroid distances between harmful and benign hidden-state representations are preserved far better by extended-refusal models after abliteration: baseline Qwen2.5-3B centroid distance collapses 33.89 points (100.88 to 66.99) while the extended-refusal variant loses only 7.65 points (79.59 to 71.94), showing that richer refusal outputs diffuse the feature-space boundary. (Table 2, Section 4.4) [[extended-refusal-fine-tuning-disperses-safety-signal]]

(Table 1, Section 4.2) [[extended-refusal-fine-tuning-disperses-safety-signal]]
