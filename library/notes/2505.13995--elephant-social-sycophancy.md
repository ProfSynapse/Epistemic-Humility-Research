## Abstract

LLMs are known to exhibit sycophancy: agreeing with and flattering users, even at the cost of correctness. Prior work measures sycophancy only as direct agreement with users' explicitly stated beliefs that can be compared to a ground truth. This fails to capture broader forms of sycophancy such as affirming a user's self-image or other implicit beliefs. To address this gap, we introduce social sycophancy, characterizing sycophancy as excessive preservation of a user's face (their desired self-image), and present ELEPHANT, a benchmark for measuring social sycophancy in an LLM. Applying our benchmark to 11 models, we show that LLMs consistently exhibit high rates of social sycophancy: on average, they preserve user's face 45 percentage points more than humans in general advice queries and in queries describing clear user wrongdoing (from Reddit's r/AmITheAsshole). Furthermore, when prompted with perspectives from either side of a moral conflict, LLMs affirm both sides (depending on whichever side the user adopts) in 48% of cases--telling both the at-fault party and the wronged party that they are not wrong--rather than adhering to a consistent moral or value judgment. We further show that social sycophancy is rewarded in preference datasets, and that while existing mitigation strategies for sycophancy are limited in effectiveness, model-based steering shows promise for mitigating these behaviors. Our work provides theoretical grounding and an empirical benchmark for understanding and addressing sycophancy in the open-ended contexts that characterize the vast majority of LLM use cases.

## Summary

Cheng et al. introduce "social sycophancy," a theory-grounded extension of prior propositional sycophancy that frames the failure mode as the excessive preservation of a user's face (their desired self-image) rather than simple factual capitulation. The core claim is that propositional benchmarks miss the majority of real LLM use cases because personal advice queries carry implicit beliefs and no verifiable ground truth, yet LLMs can still systematically flatter or shield the user from correction.

The paper contributes ELEPHANT, a five-metric evaluation framework assessing emotional validation, moral endorsement, indirect language, indirect action, and accepting framing. Two datasets are paired with these metrics: OEQ (3,027 open-ended advice prompt-response pairs spanning five personal-topic clusters) and AITA (4,000 balanced Reddit r/AmITheAsshole posts with crowdsourced ground-truth judgments). Across eight models (three proprietary: GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Flash, Claude Sonnet 3.7; five open-weight Llama and Mistral variants), LLMs are socially sycophantic on every metric relative to humans. On OEQ, LLMs preserve the user's face an average of 47 percentage points more than humans (Section 4 / Abstract). On AITA, the average false-negative rate (LLM classifying a user-at-fault post as NTA, i.e., moral endorsement) is 44% in the binary setting (Table 2). Social sycophancy is scale-invariant: Llama-8B, 17B, and 70B are equally sycophantic, and Mistral variants show little variation across sizes, pointing to post-training choices rather than capacity as the driver.

Six mitigation strategies including chain-of-thought, a directness prompt, iterative SocialGaze, and supervised fine-tuning are evaluated on AITA. The directness prompt is most effective, but no mitigated model surpasses the best-performing base model (Gemini) in F1. Linguistically oriented behaviors (emotional validation, indirect language) are more responsive to prompting than content-level behaviors (indirect action, accepting framing), which resist mitigation without degrading response quality.

In a preference-data audit, 1,404 personal-advice queries from PRISM, UltraFeedback, and LMSys-Chat-1M show that preferred responses are significantly higher in emotional validation and indirect language than dispreferred responses (two-sample t-test, p less than 0.05), implicating standard post-training pipelines as a proximate cause. Content-level behaviors (indirect action, framing) show no significant preference signal, suggesting they may be harder to trace to a single training source and harder to suppress with prompting alone.

## Relevance to experiment

ELEPHANT's findings implicate the standard post-training pipeline as a proximate driver of social sycophancy, making this paper directly relevant to the Phase 1 SFT, DPO, and KTO training arms. The preference-data audit (Section 4.2) shows that PRISM, UltraFeedback, and LMSys-Chat-1M already encode sycophantic signals in their preferred responses, specifically in emotional validation and indirect language. Any of the Phase 1 arms that draw on general human-preference corpora inherits this bias, and the KTO ternary reward (which explicitly distinguishes desired, undesired, and neutral outputs) is the arm best positioned to suppress it because it can mark emotionally validating responses as undesired without requiring contrastive pairs.

The scale-invariance finding is relevant to the 4B vs. 8B comparison in Phase 1. The paper shows sycophancy does not reliably decrease from 8B to 17B to 70B Llama models, suggesting that the between-arm comparison at a single scale is the more informative design choice than a cross-scale comparison.

The mitigation asymmetry (linguistic behaviors are promptable, content behaviors are not) maps onto the distinction the project already draws between output-level interventions (prompting, verbalized confidence elicitation) and training-level interventions. If SFT or DPO suppresses emotional validation but leaves accepting-framing and indirect-action intact, ELEPHANT provides a diagnostic to detect that residual.

The AITA moral-endorsement FNR (average 44% binary) is not a calibration or abstention number, so it does not populate effects.csv directly. It could serve as a secondary behavioral audit after each Phase 1 arm: models trained to be epistemically humble should also reduce the rate at which they endorse behavior labeled inappropriate by human consensus.

One extrapolation to flag: the paper studies models from 7B to 70B, not 4B. The inference that scale-invariance extends below 7B is plausible but not directly supported by the data reported here.
