## Abstract

Large language models are known to hallucinate when faced with unfamiliar queries, but the underlying mechanism that govern how models hallucinate are not yet fully understood. In this work, we find that unfamiliar examples in the models' finetuning data -- those that introduce concepts beyond the base model's scope of knowledge -- are crucial in shaping these errors. In particular, we find that an LLM's hallucinated predictions tend to mirror the responses associated with its unfamiliar finetuning examples. This suggests that by modifying how unfamiliar finetuning examples are supervised, we can influence a model's responses to unfamiliar queries (e.g., say ``I don't know''). We empirically validate this observation in a series of controlled experiments involving SFT, RL, and reward model finetuning on TriviaQA and MMLU. Our work further investigates RL finetuning strategies for improving the factuality of long-form model generations. We find that, while hallucinations from the reward model can significantly undermine the effectiveness of RL factuality finetuning, strategically controlling how reward models hallucinate can minimize these negative effects. Leveraging our previous observations on controlling hallucinations, we propose an approach for learning more reliable reward models, and show that they improve the efficacy of RL factuality finetuning in long-form biography and book/movie plot generation tasks.

## Summary

Kang et al. investigate the mechanisms by which finetuned LLMs hallucinate on unfamiliar inputs, those requiring knowledge of concepts scarcely present in the pretraining corpus. The central finding is that finetuned models do not hallucinate arbitrarily: as test inputs become more unfamiliar, model predictions converge toward a "hedged prediction," the loss-minimizing default output computed over unfamiliar finetuning examples. For standard SFT, this hedged prediction mirrors the ground-truth label distribution of unfamiliar training examples, producing confident-sounding but incorrect answers. Controlled MMLU experiments with Llama2-7B confirm this mechanism: relabeling unfamiliar examples with a uniform distribution causes predictions to converge toward that uniform distribution at test time, while labeling them 50% B / 50% C causes predictions to converge to that biased distribution. The same principle applies under RL (PPO): a reward function that penalizes abstaining (-3) drives increasingly random guesses on unfamiliar inputs, while one that assigns a neutral reward (0) to abstaining produces increasingly abstaining outputs. The paper extends this framework to long-form factuality training. Standard reward models overestimate rewards on unfamiliar inputs (Figure 6), causing RL factuality finetuning to reinforce hallucinated outputs rather than suppress them. The paper proposes conservative reward models trained from samples drawn from an SFT model initialized from the same base model (Llama2-7B), with the aim of underestimating rewards on unfamiliar queries rather than overestimating them. Using FActScore on WikiBios and WikiPlots benchmarks, RL with conservative reward models achieves a fraction of true facts of 0.59 on biography generation and 0.80 on plot generation, compared to 0.47 and 0.45 for standard SFT and 0.50 and 0.54 for RL with standard reward models. Answer relabeling (substituting "I don't know" for ground-truth labels on unfamiliar training examples) is validated as a lightweight SFT-stage intervention that redirects the hedged prediction to genuine abstention.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper bears on the Phase 1 abstention study in three ways. First, the hedged-prediction mechanism directly explains why the SFT arms in the Phase 1 protocol must control the familiarity distribution of training data: if unfamiliar examples are supervised with ground-truth answers, the model's default output on unfamiliar test queries will mimic that label distribution rather than abstaining. This motivates answer relabeling as the SFT-stage baseline and sets a concrete prediction about what goes wrong without it. Second, the conservative reward model approach is the RL-stage analogue to answer relabeling: it shows that controlling how reward models extrapolate to unfamiliar inputs is essential to RL-based factuality training, which directly informs any RL arm added to the Phase 1 matrix. The factuality deltas from Figure 7 (biography: 0.47 to 0.59; plot: 0.45 to 0.80) are concrete effect-size reference points for what conservative reward shaping can achieve in long-form generation. Third, the paper's reward structure (+2 correct, -3 incorrect, 0 or -3 abstain) differs from the abstention-focused Phase 1 protocol in scope: the paper optimizes for fraction of true facts in long-form generation rather than abstention rate on short-form QA. This makes it a complementary but not directly comparable baseline; mapping its FActScore gains onto the Phase 1 calibration and abstention metrics would require bridging experiments.

## Claims

- An LLM's hallucinated predictions mirror the response distribution of its unfamiliar finetuning examples: as inputs become more unfamiliar, model predictions revert to the hedged prediction determined by those examples' supervision. (Figures 3-4, Section 4) [[hedged-prediction]]
- RL with conservative reward models achieves fractions of true facts of 0.59 (biography) and 0.80 (plot generation) compared to 0.47/0.45 for standard SFT, outperforming both SFT and RL with standard reward models. (Figure 7, Section 6.2) [[conservative-reward-model]]
- Standard reward models vastly overestimate rewards on unfamiliar inputs, undermining RL factuality finetuning; conservative reward models trained to avoid overestimation do not show this overestimation pattern. (Figure 6, Section 6.2) [[conservative-reward-model]]
- Strategically modifying supervision of unfamiliar finetuning examples (answer relabeling to 'I don't know') shifts the model's default hedged prediction to an abstaining response, improving selective accuracy on held-out unfamiliar queries. (Figure 1, Section 5) [[answer-relabeling]]
