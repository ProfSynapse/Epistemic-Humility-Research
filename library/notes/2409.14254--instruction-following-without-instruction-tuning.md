## Abstract

Instruction tuning commonly means finetuning a language model on instruction-response pairs. We discover two forms of adaptation (tuning) that are deficient compared to instruction tuning, yet still yield instruction following; we call this implicit instruction tuning. We first find that instruction-response pairs are not necessary: training solely on responses, without any corresponding instructions, yields instruction following. This suggests pretrained models have an instruction-response mapping which is revealed by teaching the model the desired distribution of responses. However, we then find it's not necessary to teach the desired distribution of responses: instruction-response training on narrow-domain data like poetry still leads to broad instruction-following behavior like recipe generation. In particular, when instructions are very different from those in the narrow finetuning domain, models' responses do not adhere to the style of the finetuning domain. To begin to explain implicit instruction tuning, we hypothesize that very simple changes to a language model's distribution yield instruction following. We support this by hand-writing a rule-based language model which yields instruction following in a product-of-experts with a pretrained model. The rules are to slowly increase the probability of ending the sequence, penalize repetition, and uniformly change 15 words' probabilities. In summary, adaptations made without being designed to yield instruction following can do so implicitly.

## Summary

Hewitt et al. define instruction tuning as fine-tuning on (instruction, response) pairs to learn p(y|x), and show two weaker forms of adaptation still yield instruction-following behavior, which they call implicit instruction tuning. First, response tuning: they strip every instruction to the empty string in the 1,030-example LIMA dataset and fine-tune Llama-2-7B and OLMo-7B-Feb2024 only on responses, learning just the marginal p(y). Despite never seeing an instruction paired with a response during training, response-tuned models win against their own instruction-tuned counterparts on AlpacaEval roughly 43% of the time (vs. single-digit win rates for the un-tuned base models), showing pretrained models already encode much of the instruction-response mapping, and merely nudging the marginal output distribution toward "desirable response" style reveals it. Second, single-task finetuning: they fine-tune the same base models separately on five narrow, instruction-following-unrelated domains (MBPP code generation, GSM8K math derivations, poetry, recipes, chess games in PGN notation) and find every one of them substantially raises AlpacaEval win rate against instruction-tuned models on broad, out-of-domain instructions, even though the narrow-domain data has no resemblance to general instruction following. When held-out instructions are stylistically distant from the finetuning domain, model responses do not adopt that domain's style, meaning the effect is genuine broad instruction-following capability rather than domain-specific imitation leaking outward. To begin to explain why such minimal or off-target adaptation produces this effect, the authors hand-write a simple rule-based response adapter (slowly increasing end-of-sequence probability, penalizing repetition, and uniformly reweighting 15 words) that, combined with a pretrained model in a product-of-experts, also yields instruction following, suggesting the mechanism may not require learning anything task-specific at all.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is direct evidence against attributing a trained model's broad behavioral shift specifically to what a fine-tuning objective was designed to teach: both response-only training and narrow single-task data produce general instruction-following gains their training signal never targeted. For the abstention-training comparison, this raises a live confound for interpreting any SFT/DPO/KTO arm that trains on abstention-labeled data: some of the resulting behavioral shift toward caution or hedging could be a generic "any sufficiently strong distributional nudge from a small dataset yields broad behavior change" effect (implicit instruction tuning) rather than the model specifically learning when to abstain, and a response-tuning-style ablation (training on abstention-appropriate responses with instructions stripped) would be a natural control to distinguish the two.
