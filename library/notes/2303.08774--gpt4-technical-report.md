## Abstract

We report the development of GPT-4, a large-scale, multimodal model which can accept image and text inputs and produce text outputs. While less capable than humans in many real-world scenarios, GPT-4 exhibits human-level performance on various professional and academic benchmarks, including passing a simulated bar exam with a score around the top 10% of test takers. GPT-4 is a Transformer-based model pre-trained to predict the next token in a document. The post-training alignment process results in improved performance on measures of factuality and adherence to desired behavior. A core component of this project was developing infrastructure and optimization methods that behave predictably across a wide range of scales. This allowed us to accurately predict some aspects of GPT-4's performance based on models trained with no more than 1/1,000th the compute of GPT-4.

## Summary

The GPT-4 Technical Report (OpenAI, 2023) documents the development, capabilities, limitations, and safety properties of GPT-4, a large-scale multimodal Transformer pre-trained on next-token prediction and then aligned with RLHF. The report contributes three categories of findings directly relevant to epistemic-humility research: (1) a primary-source demonstration that RLHF post-training degrades token-probability calibration relative to the pre-trained base model, visible in Figure 8's calibration plots on MMLU; (2) a documented tension between safety RLHF and appropriate confidence, where the same training that reduces harmful content can also cause the model to become overly cautious on safe inputs, refusing innocuous requests or excessively hedging; and (3) a benchmark comparison showing GPT-4 reduces hallucinations by 19 percentage points versus GPT-3.5 on internal adversarially-designed factuality evaluations. On capability benchmarks, GPT-4 scores 86.4% on MMLU (5-shot) against GPT-3.5's 70.0%, and 92.0% on GSM-8K (5-shot chain-of-thought) against GPT-3.5's 57.1%. The Appendix B analysis shows that RLHF changes capability negligibly (base 73.7% vs. post-training 74.0% on exam multiple-choice), confirming that calibration degradation is a post-training artifact rather than a capability shift. The paper also reports predictable scaling: loss was accurately predicted from models trained with up to 10,000x less compute, with the prediction made shortly after the run started. TruthfulQA results are reported only as a figure (no numeric values in text), and calibration results rely entirely on visual plots without numeric ECE values.

## Relevance to experiment

The GPT-4 Technical Report is the primary public source for three findings that anchor the Phase 1 SFT vs. DPO vs. KTO calibration study. First, Figure 8 is the direct empirical demonstration that RLHF post-training degrades token-probability calibration: the pre-trained base model aligns well on the calibration diagonal; the RLHF model does not. This supports the project's central hypothesis that alignment training imposes a calibration cost and gives a concrete reference point for what that degradation looks like in a well-resourced system. Second, §6's over-caution finding pins down the dual failure mode: the same safety RLHF that reduces harmful content can cause the model to refuse innocuous requests or excessively hedge, the exact over-abstention pattern Phase 1 trains and measures. Third, the 19-point factuality improvement over GPT-3.5 motivates the question of whether smaller-scale RLHF variants (the Phase 1 arms) can achieve factuality gains without the calibration cost. The paper cannot feed effects.csv directly because it provides no numeric ECE values and no matched pre/post abstention-rate deltas on the same model under controlled conditions. The TruthfulQA results are figure-only. The report's architectural opacity (no model-size disclosure) prevents direct scale transfer to Qwen3, but the directional findings — calibration degrades, over-abstention is a real risk, capability survives RLHF largely intact — are the motivating priors for the Phase 1 design.

## Claims

- RLHF post-training significantly degrades token-probability calibration relative to the pre-trained GPT-4 base model, visible in Figure 8's calibration plots on MMLU: the pre-trained model tracks the calibration diagonal; the post-trained model does not. No numeric ECE is reported; the finding is qualitative from visual plots. (§5 Limitations prose; Figure 8 caption) [[rlhf-degrades-conditional-calibration]]
- After RLHF, GPT-4 can become overly cautious on safe inputs — refusing innocuous requests or excessively hedging — even as it reduces harmful outputs. OpenAI uses rule-based reward models (RBRMs) to penalize both over-refusal and under-refusal during RLHF fine-tuning. (§6 Risks and Mitigations prose) [[rlhf-induces-over-caution-on-safe-inputs]]
- GPT-4 scores 86.4% on MMLU (5-shot), 16 percentage points above GPT-3.5 (70.0%) and well above prior LM SOTA (70.7%) and best overall SOTA (75.2%), establishing a strong capability ceiling against which calibration and abstention costs can be measured. (§4 Capabilities, Table 2) [[mmlu]]
- Base GPT-4 and RLHF GPT-4 score 73.7% vs. 74.0% averaged across exam MCQ benchmarks, confirming that post-training does not substantially alter base capability — the calibration degradation shown in Figure 8 is a pure alignment artifact, not a capability regression. (Appendix B; Table 8) [[reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback]]
- GPT-4 reduces toxic generation on RealToxicityPrompts to 0.73% from GPT-3.5's 6.48%, and reduces disallowed-content responses by 82% while improving sensitive-request policy compliance by 29% — showing RLHF safety mitigations sharply shift the output distribution, with the over-caution risk as the documented cost side. (§6 Risks and Mitigations prose; Table 6; Figure 9) [[realtoxicityprompts]]
