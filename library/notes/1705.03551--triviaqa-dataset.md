## Summary

Joshi et al. introduce TriviaQA, a reading comprehension dataset of 95,956 question-answer pairs sourced from trivia enthusiasts and paired with independently collected evidence documents from Wikipedia and Web search, yielding over 650K question-answer-evidence triples. The core design choice is decoupling question authoring from evidence collection: trivia enthusiasts wrote questions for their own purposes, and documents were retrieved retrospectively, creating natural syntactic and lexical mismatches between questions and answer-bearing sentences rather than the near-paraphrase alignment that crowdsourced datasets exhibit. The paper demonstrates this challenge quantitatively: 69% of Wikipedia examples require resolving syntactic variation, 41% require synonym resolution, and 40% require multi-sentence reasoning (more than three times the rate in SQuAD). BiDAF, then state-of-the-art on SQuAD, achieves only 40% exact match on TriviaQA distant supervision against a human baseline of 79.7%, and the entity classifier reaches only 23% EM, leaving a large gap that the paper argues reflects genuine compositional and cross-sentence difficulty. The dataset also anticipates abstention workloads explicitly: a footnote in Section 2 notes that because roughly 20-25% of evidence documents do not contain a sufficient answer (inferred from the complement of the human annotator accuracy figures), the data could support a task formulation where models learn when to abstain rather than always attempt an answer. The verified subsets provide clean evaluation separate from the noisy distant supervision split.

## Relevance to experiment

TriviaQA is the primary training and evaluation corpus used in the locked training-regimen SFT-vs-DPO-vs-KTO epistemic humility study. Its factoid structure and clean answer aliases make it tractable for measuring honesty-score, prudence-score, and over-conservativeness-score against known ground truth. Three features make it particularly useful for abstention research. First, the dataset explicitly flags the abstention use case: Section 2 footnote 2 notes that because a meaningful fraction of evidence documents do not contain sufficient information to answer the question, the data supports a task formulation where the model must learn when to abstain. Second, the human-model gap (79.7% human vs 40% BiDAF) provides a calibration anchor: a well-calibrated model trained on this data should know it does not know roughly as often as it would be wrong. Third, the parametric-knowledge interpretation is well-defined here because questions were written independently of documents, so question-answer pairs test knowledge the model may have internalized from pretraining separately from whether any given retrieved document supplies it. The 20-25% of Wikipedia questions whose provided documents lack sufficient evidence (inferred from the complement of the 79.7%/75.4% human accuracy figures, not stated as a single phrase in the text) constitute a natural abstention-positive set that downstream experiments can exploit. The verified subsets allow evaluation on a clean subset free from distant-supervision noise, which matters for distinguishing genuine abstention from evidence-gap confounds.

## Claims

- TriviaQA questions, authored independently of evidence documents, exhibit syntactic variation in 69% of Wikipedia examples and require synonym resolution in 41%, creating a harder test than crowdsourced datasets where questions are written against pre-selected passages. (Table 5) [[triviaqa]]
- 40% of TriviaQA Wikipedia examples require multi-sentence reasoning, more than three times the rate in SQuAD, making it a more demanding testbed for cross-sentence inference. (Table 5; Section 4) [[multi-sentence-reasoning]]
- BiDAF reaches only 40% exact match on TriviaQA distant supervision against a human annotator baseline of 79.7% on Wikipedia, a 40-point gap that the paper attributes to compositional question difficulty and lexical mismatch rather than dataset noise. (Table 7; Section 6.3) [[exact-match-f1]]
- Section 2 footnote 2 explicitly notes that TriviaQA could support a task where models learn when to abstain because some evidence documents do not contain the answer, anticipating abstention benchmarking that later work formalizes. (Section 2) [[abstention]]
- 92.85% of TriviaQA answers are Wikipedia entity titles, enabling alias-based evaluation that reduces false-negative exact-match errors compared to span-extraction datasets. (Table 4; Section 4) [[triviaqa]]
