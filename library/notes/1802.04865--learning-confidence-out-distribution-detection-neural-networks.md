## Abstract

Modern neural networks are very powerful predictive models, but they are often incapable of recognizing when their predictions may be wrong. Closely related to this is the task of out-of-distribution detection, where a network must determine whether or not an input is outside of the set on which it is expected to safely perform. To jointly address these issues, we propose a method of learning confidence estimates for neural networks that is simple to implement and produces intuitively interpretable outputs. We demonstrate that on the task of out-of-distribution detection, our technique surpasses recently proposed techniques which construct confidence based on the network's output distribution, without requiring any additional labels or access to out-of-distribution examples. Additionally, we address the problem of calibrating out-of-distribution detectors, where we demonstrate that misclassified in-distribution examples can be used as a proxy for out-of-distribution examples.

## Summary

DeVries and Taylor augment a standard classifier with a confidence branch: a small parallel head that outputs a single sigmoid scalar c. During training the network may request a "hint" by interpolating its softmax predictions toward the ground-truth label via p'_i = c*p_i + (1-c)*y_i, paying a log-penalty L_c = -log(c) for each hint taken. A budget hyperparameter beta and an adaptive confidence-loss weight lambda prevent c from collapsing to 0 (always taking hints) or to 1 (never taking them, losing the signal). Three practical stabilizers make the scheme robust: an adaptive lambda schedule driven by the gap between current L_c and beta; a 50% hint-application rate to prevent excessive regularization from suppressing complex decision boundaries; and aggressive data augmentation including Cutout to retain misclassified examples through training. At inference, a threshold on c directly flags OOD inputs via g(x; delta) = 1 if c &lt;= delta. The paper evaluates DenseNet-BC (L=100, k=12), WideResNet (WRN-16-8 for SVHN, WRN-28-10 for CIFAR-10), and VGG13 against SVHN and CIFAR-10 as in-distribution datasets and seven named OOD datasets (TinyImageNet crop/resize, LSUN crop/resize, iSUN, Uniform Noise, Gaussian Noise) plus an All Images aggregate. Confidence thresholding beats the max-softmax baseline in almost all settings across all three architectures. With input preprocessing adapted from ODIN (gradient-sign perturbation toward c -> 1), the method beats ODIN in all settings except DenseNet-BC on CIFAR-10. A secondary experiment shows misclassified in-distribution examples serve as a conservative proxy when selecting the OOD detection threshold, incurring roughly 0.9 pp detection error overhead (Table 3), eliminating the need for a held-out OOD set. Classification accuracy is preserved across all architectures, with test error changes within approximately 0.2 pp.

## Relevance to experiment

The hint-budget mechanism is a direct conceptual ancestor of the aux_head confidence engine in the experiment: both attach a learned scalar readout head alongside a prediction head and train it with a proper-scoring-style penalty so the scalar tracks the model's actual uncertainty. The result that the confidence scalar is driven low on misclassified inputs without any explicit supervision on those examples parallels Amendment P's finding that the KUQ-fit probe reads SelfAware COLD at AUROC 0.983: a signal trained on in-distribution difficulty generalizes to distribution shift. The misclassified-as-proxy finding (Table 3, roughly 0.9 pp detection-error overhead) is directly analogous to the cross-dataset transfer design in Amendment P -- if the model's own hard examples encode something real about its knowledge boundary, no cross-domain OOD set is needed to calibrate the readout. The confirmed task-accuracy preservation (classification error within approximately 0.2 pp across architectures) supports the alignment-tax-free property that motivates comparing SFT vs DPO vs KTO arms in Phase 1. The scope boundary is image classification (SVHN/CIFAR-10 vs natural image OOD), where the ground-truth label is always available during training; this differs from LLM factual answerability, where the model must reason about the boundary between known and unknown without per-example label supervision.
