## Abstract

We study whether language models can evaluate the validity of their own claims and predict which questions they will be able to answer correctly. We first show that larger models are well-calibrated on diverse multiple choice and true/false questions when they are provided in the right format. Thus we can approach self-evaluation on open-ended sampling tasks by asking models to first propose answers, and then to evaluate the probability "P(True)" that their answers are correct. We find encouraging performance, calibration, and scaling for P(True) on a diverse array of tasks. Performance at self-evaluation further improves when we allow models to consider many of their own samples before predicting the validity of one specific possibility. Next, we investigate whether models can be trained to predict "P(IK)", the probability that "I know" the answer to a question, without reference to any particular proposed answer. Models perform well at predicting P(IK) and partially generalize across tasks, though they struggle with calibration of P(IK) on new tasks. The predicted P(IK) probabilities also increase appropriately in the presence of relevant source materials in the context, and in the presence of hints towards the solution of mathematical word problems. We hope these observations lay the groundwork for training more honest models, and for investigating how honesty generalizes to cases where models are trained on objectives other than the imitation of human writing.

## Summary

Kadavath et al. study two related but distinct questions: whether language models can assess the validity of their own outputs (self-evaluation), and whether they can be trained to predict in advance which questions they will answer correctly (self-knowledge). The paper answers both affirmatively for large models, with important caveats about calibration.

The first half establishes that 52B-parameter Anthropic models (pretrained for 850B tokens) are well-calibrated on diverse multiple-choice tasks when answer options are shown as visible lettered alternatives before the model commits to a choice. Calibration improves monotonically with scale from 800M to 52B parameters and with few-shot prompting, across BIG Bench, MMLU, TruthfulQA, QuALITY, and LogiQA. Crucially, replacing an answer option with "none of the above" breaks both accuracy and calibration even at 52B, revealing that calibrated discrimination among concrete options is easier than open-ended knowledge assessment.

The paper then introduces P(True), a prompting-only self-evaluation technique: generate a candidate answer, then ask the model to assign probability to "True" versus "False" for that candidate. Accuracy is marginal zero-shot but improves substantially with few-shot prompting, and further improves when the model is shown multiple of its own temperature-1 samples before evaluating one (the brainstorming variant). Across TriviaQA, Lambada, GSM8k, Codex HumanEval, and arithmetic, the conditional accuracy of responses where P(True) > 0.5 is substantially higher than unconditional accuracy. The gap between conditional and base accuracy grows with model size, supporting the claim that verification capability scales faster than generation quality.

The second half introduces P(IK), trained by adding a binary value head on top of the language model and finetuning on empirical pass rates from 30 temperature-1 samples per question. Trained on TriviaQA, P(IK) achieves AUROC 0.864 in-distribution. It generalizes discriminatively to math (AUROC 0.928) and code (AUROC 0.687) without retraining, but OOD calibration is poor (Brier 0.194 on Mixed-Arithmetic). Training on all four tasks substantially restores calibration. A cross-model experiment with two 12B models having distinct pretraining distributions finds a roughly 6% P(IK) advantage when a model predicts its own correctness rather than the other model's, providing weak but real evidence that P(IK) encodes model-specific self-knowledge rather than merely question difficulty. Finally, P(IK) responds appropriately to in-context evidence it was never trained on: adding a relevant Wikipedia article raises P(IK) from 18% to 78% on one example, and correct math hints raise P(IK) while distractors and wrong hints lower it.

## Relevance to experiment

### Baseline architecture for the Phase 1 abstention study

P(True) and P(IK) are the two primary baselines the Phase 1 SFT/DPO/KTO study implicitly competes against. P(True) shows what prompt-only self-evaluation can achieve without parameter updates; P(IK) shows what a lightweight supervised head can achieve on top of a pretrained model. Both are output-level signals. The Phase 1 training arms ask whether preference-based finetuning on abstention behavior can improve beyond these baselines on calibrated uncertainty expression.

### The verification-advantage-over-generation mechanism as a mechanism program hypothesis

The paper documents that the conditional-accuracy gap (P(True)>0.5 versus unconditional base) grows with model size, interpreted as verification scaling faster than generation (Figure 11, Section 4.2). For mechinterp, this translates into a testable prediction: if larger models have stronger truth-direction signal in their activations relative to generation quality, probing should detect cleaner separation at larger scales. If post-training (SFT or DPO) sharpens this signal, activation probes before and after training should detect it.

### P(IK) context-sensitivity as an ICL-generalization probe

The P(IK) generalization to in-context Wikipedia passages and math hints shows the model updates its self-knowledge signal based on context, even without training on such inputs. This is relevant to the Phase 1 design because a model finetuned with DPO or KTO on abstention behavior should, if the training is mechanistically sound, also update P(IK)-like internal signals in the presence of new context. A pre/post probe measuring P(IK) under context-augmented conditions would test whether training sharpens the connection between in-context evidence and expressed uncertainty.

### The OOD calibration gap as a guard rail

Table 1 makes the OOD calibration gap explicit: TriviaQA training transfers discriminability but not calibration to code, arithmetic, and story tasks. The Phase 1 evaluation should not assume that good in-domain abstention calibration generalizes across task domains without multi-domain training data. If the Phase 1 abstention arms train on a narrow domain, OOD calibration checks are required before reporting results as generally applicable.

### RLHF miscalibration finding as prior for temperature tuning

The T=2.5 fix for RLHF-induced miscalibration (Figure 9, Section 3.3) establishes that at least some finetuning-induced calibration problems are surface-level (logit shift) rather than representational. Before concluding that DPO or SFT improves calibration, the Phase 1 analysis should confirm the improvement is not an artifact of distribution shift that could be removed by temperature scaling.
