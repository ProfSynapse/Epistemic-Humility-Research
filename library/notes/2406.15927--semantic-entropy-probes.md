## Abstract

We propose semantic entropy probes (SEPs), a cheap and reliable method for uncertainty quantification in Large Language Models (LLMs). Hallucinations, which are plausible-sounding but factually incorrect and arbitrary model generations, present a major challenge to the practical adoption of LLMs. Recent work by Farquhar et al. (2024) proposes semantic entropy (SE), which can detect hallucinations by estimating uncertainty in the space semantic meaning for a set of model generations. However, the 5-to-10-fold increase in computation cost associated with SE computation hinders practical adoption. To address this, we propose SEPs, which directly approximate SE from the hidden states of a single generation. SEPs are simple to train and do not require sampling multiple model generations at test time, reducing the overhead of semantic uncertainty quantification to almost zero. We show that SEPs retain high performance for hallucination detection and generalize better to out-of-distribution data than previous probing methods that directly predict model accuracy. Our results across models and tasks suggest that model hidden states capture SE, and our ablation studies give further insights into the token positions and model layers for which this is the case.

## Summary

Kossen et al. propose Semantic Entropy Probes (SEPs), linear classifiers trained on a single model generation's hidden states to approximate semantic entropy (SE), the uncertainty measure over clusters of equivalent semantic meaning introduced by Farquhar et al. (2024). The motivation is practical: computing SE requires 5-to-10 additional model generations per query, a cost that blocks deployment. SEPs eliminate that overhead at test time by reading SE off one forward pass, using N=10 high-temperature samples only at training time to generate SE labels. Two token positions are studied: the second-last-token (SLT) after the response ends, and the token-before-generating (TBG), which reads uncertainty off the last input token before any generation begins. The TBG result is particularly striking, as the model appears to encode SE before producing a single output token.

Evaluation spans five models (Llama-2 7B and 70B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-3.8B, Llama-3-70B) across four QA tasks (TriviaQA, SQuAD, BioASQ, NQ Open) in both short- and long-form generation settings. In-distribution, SEPs and accuracy probes (probes supervised with ground-truth correctness labels) perform similarly, with differences of -2.0 to +2.8 AUROC points across model-setting pairs. The critical advantage emerges under leave-one-out task generalization: SEPs beat accuracy probes by 7.7 to 10.5 points for smaller short-form models and 2.2 to 6.2 points for large long-form models. The authors attribute this to SE being a more intrinsic model property than factual correctness, which makes it easier to extract in a way that transfers across task distributions.

The counterfactual context experiment on TriviaQA with Llama-2-7B validates the mechanism: adding the supporting passage raises accuracy from 26% to 78% and reduces ground-truth SE from 1.84 to 0.50, and SEP predictions track this shift despite the probe never being trained on context-augmented inputs. Sampling-based baselines (SE, naive entropy, p(True)) remain stronger at hallucination detection but require 10x the generation cost. For long-form generations, AUROC often peaks at intermediate rather than final layers, consistent with later layers being occupied with next-token syntax rather than semantic certainty.

## Relevance to experiment

SEPs are directly relevant to all three phases of the Epistemic Humility experiment. For Phase 1 (SFT vs DPO vs KTO training arms), a SEP trained on a base model could serve as a read-out attached to each training arm: after each intervention, running the probe on held-out QA inputs would reveal whether internal SE signal sharpened, flattened, or shifted, without the cost of 10-sample SE computation. This gives a cheap internal-state counterpart to the output-level metrics (verbalized confidence, p(True), consistency-based confidence) already in the graph, and the OOD generalization advantage of SEPs over accuracy probes is especially relevant here since post-training distribution shift is expected.

For Phase 3 (mechanistic interpretability), SEPs add a hidden-state probing candidate that complements the gradient-space signal from Grad Detect. If SEP AUROC and gradient-based discriminability both localize to later layers, that convergence is mechanistic evidence about where epistemic signal lives. If they disagree, the divergence is informative. The TBG result is particularly interesting for Phase 3: a model encodes SE before generating, implying epistemic signal is present at the prompt-encoding stage rather than only in the generation residual stream.

The key caveat is that OOD generalization is evaluated only across QA task distributions, not across post-training behavior regimes. It is unknown whether SE is as well-encoded after SFT, DPO, or KTO as it is in the base or instruction-tuned checkpoints the paper studies. That question is exactly what a Phase 1 probe read-out would target. The paper does not test any post-training intervention, so it does not populate effects.csv directly, but it provides the probe infrastructure and the mechanistic hypothesis that motivates doing so.
