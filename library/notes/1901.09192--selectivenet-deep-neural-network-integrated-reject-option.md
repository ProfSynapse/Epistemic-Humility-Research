## Abstract

We consider the problem of selective prediction (also known as reject option) in deep neural networks, and introduce SelectiveNet, a deep neural architecture with an integrated reject option. Existing rejection mechanisms are based mostly on a threshold over the prediction confidence of a pre-trained network. In contrast, SelectiveNet is trained to optimize both classification (or regression) and rejection simultaneously, end-to-end. The result is a deep neural network that is optimized over the covered domain. In our experiments, we show a consistently improved risk-coverage trade-off over several well-known classification and regression datasets, thus reaching new state-of-the-art results for deep selective classification.

## Summary

SelectiveNet is a three-headed deep network (prediction head f, selection head g, auxiliary head h) built on a shared body that trains end-to-end for a user-specified target coverage. The selection head emits a scalar in [0,1] via sigmoid; at inference the model predicts only when g(x) >= 0.5 and abstains otherwise. The selective loss minimizes empirical risk on the covered subset subject to a target-coverage constraint enforced by a quadratic interior-point penalty. An auxiliary head h is trained on the full training distribution without any coverage restriction and combined with the selective objective as L = alpha * L(f,g) + (1 - alpha) * Lh with alpha = 0.5 and lambda = 32 fixed across all experiments. Without h, SelectiveNet would overfit to the easy covered fraction before useful low-level features form. Post-training threshold calibration (setting a rejection threshold tau at the (1-c) percentile of g-values on a held-out validation set) further tightens coverage adherence with Hoeffding-bounded guarantees. On CIFAR-10, SelectiveNet outperforms softmax response (SR) and MC-dropout at all tested sub-unit coverages, with relative gains ranging from 8.5% at coverage 0.95 up to 26.9% at coverage 0.75 against SR. On SVHN the advantage holds at coverages up to 0.90; at coverage 0.95, SelectiveNet (1.40 +/- 0.01) is marginally worse than SR (1.39 +/- 0.05) though the paper explicitly notes all methods are statistically indistinguishable there. Cats vs. Dogs shows SelectiveNet's largest gains, including 48% over SR at coverage 0.80. For Concrete Compressive Strength regression -- where SR cannot be applied -- SelectiveNet is the first fast single-model alternative to MC-dropout and outperforms it at all coverage rates below 0.9. A coverage confusion matrix shows the diagonal is nearly optimal: a model trained and calibrated to its target coverage beats those trained for other coverages. t-SNE visualization confirms that SelectiveNet does not waste representational capacity to separate rejected classes, clustering them loosely at the center while the covered classes remain well separated.

## Relevance to experiment

SelectiveNet is the theoretical backbone for Phase 1's aux_head engine design in three concrete respects. First, the three-head architecture -- prediction f, selection g, auxiliary h on a shared body -- is the structural template motivating the aux_head engine's separation of a trainable readout head from the main predictor head over shared representations; the paper's ablation that removing h degrades selective risk confirms the auxiliary head is mechanistically necessary, not decorative. Second, the empirical result that coverage-specific training beats post-hoc SR/MC-dropout thresholding grounds the design-rationale claim that joint optimization over the covered domain is necessary: Amendment O's oracle ceiling (AUROC 0.997) and Amendment Q's trainable readout (AUROC 0.983 transfer) follow the same principle -- a head co-trained with the predictor over the joint objective beats a post-hoc probe over a frozen network. Third, Table 6's diagonal optimality -- a model trained for target coverage c is nearly optimal when calibrated to c but loses optimality when cross-calibrated -- motivates pre-registering separate coverage-targeted checkpoints rather than a single full-coverage model. Domain gap is substantial: SelectiveNet operates on fixed image/tabular inputs with a discrete selection function, while Phase 1 targets a language model generating variable-length text on open-domain questions and measuring epistemic humility through abstention rate and calibration. The coverage-constraint mechanism and the auxiliary-head rationale transfer as design principles; the specific risk-coverage magnitudes do not transfer quantitatively.
