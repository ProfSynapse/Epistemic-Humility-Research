## Abstract

Prior interpretability research studying narrow distributions has preliminarily identified self-repair, a phenomena where if components in large language models are ablated, later components will change their behavior to compensate. Our work builds off this past literature, demonstrating that self-repair exists on a variety of models families and sizes when ablating individual attention heads on the full training distribution. We further show that on the full training distribution self-repair is imperfect, as the original direct effect of the head is not fully restored, and noisy, since the degree of self-repair varies significantly across different prompts (sometimes overcorrecting beyond the original effect). We highlight two different mechanisms that contribute to self-repair, including changes in the final LayerNorm scaling factor and sparse sets of neurons implementing Anti-Erasure. We additionally discuss the implications of these results for interpretability practitioners and close with a more speculative discussion on the mystery of why self-repair occurs in these models at all, highlighting evidence for the Iterative Inference hypothesis in language models, a framework that predicts self-repair.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experiment. Self-repair of individual attention heads exists robustly across model families and sizes (Pythia, GPT2, Llama) when measured on the full pretraining distribution rather than a narrow task, but it is imperfect (ablated heads' direct effects are not fully restored on average) and noisy (varies greatly token-to-token, sometimes overcorrecting beyond the original effect) — Section 2, Figures 1-3.
- Evidence label: analysis. LayerNorm's scaling-factor change alone — not intentional attention/MLP compensation — accounts for roughly 30% of a head's direct effect being self-repaired on average across later layers, because ablation shrinks the residual stream's norm and thereby increases the post-ablation LayerNorm scale, amplifying the model's remaining logits — Section 3, Figures 4 and 6, Appendix B.
- Evidence label: experiment. MLP-layer self-repair ('Erasure') is powered by a sparse set of neurons performing Anti-Erasure: in Llama-7B, the top self-repairing neuron alone repairs over 10% of the ablated head's direct effect on more than 40% of tokens, and which neurons participate shifts across prompts (no neuron is in the top-10 self-repairing set on more than 16% of tokens for one Pythia-160M head) — Section 4, Figures 7-9, Appendix C.
- Evidence label: analysis. Because self-repair is imperfect and component importance is heavy-tailed, ablation-based circuit discovery (which only needs to detect whether a component is important, not its exact magnitude) is only partly threatened by self-repair; but the choice of ablation method matters — zero-ablation moves the model further off-distribution and induces larger, more misleading LayerNorm-driven self-repair than resample ablation — Section 3.2 and Section 5.1-5.2.
