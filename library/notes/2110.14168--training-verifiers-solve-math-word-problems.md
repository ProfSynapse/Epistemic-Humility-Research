## Abstract

State-of-the-art language models can match human performance on many tasks, but they still struggle to robustly perform multi-step mathematical reasoning. To diagnose the failures of current models and support research, we introduce GSM8K, a dataset of 8.5K high quality linguistically diverse grade school math word problems. We find that even the largest transformer models fail to achieve high test performance, despite the conceptual simplicity of this problem distribution. To increase performance, we propose training verifiers to judge the correctness of model completions. At test time, we generate many candidate solutions and select the one ranked highest by the verifier. We demonstrate that verification significantly improves performance on GSM8K, and we provide strong empirical evidence that verification scales more effectively with increased data than a finetuning baseline.

## Summary

Cobbe et al. introduce GSM8K, a curated benchmark of 8.5K grade school math word problems (7.5K train / 1K test, 2-8 steps each), and train verifiers to judge the correctness of generator completions at test time. The pipeline is: a GPT-3 family generator produces many candidate natural-language solutions; a separately trained verifier scores each; the top-ranked solution is submitted. Two verifier architectures are compared: a solution-level verifier that scores a complete solution with a single classification head, and a token-level value-function verifier that scores after every token and averages across the solution. The token-level variant outperforms the solution-level variant and is markedly less prone to overfitting, particularly because it receives a training signal from every token rather than once per solution. Adding a joint language-modeling auxiliary objective is a strict improvement in both settings. Generator scale dominates verifier scale: a large generator paired with a small verifier substantially beats the reverse. Test-time search is non-monotonic: performance improves up to approximately 400 completions per problem, then degrades as adversarial solutions begin to fool the verifier. Majority voting among only the top-ranked subset of completions (top 3-5 at 100 total; top ~30 at 3200 total) extends the useful search range. The headline finding: a 6B verifier selecting among 100 candidates slightly outperforms a finetuned 175B model on the full training set, a gain the paper characterizes as approximately equivalent to a 30x model size increase for the finetuning baseline. Verification also scales more favorably with additional training data than finetuning.

## Relevance to experiment

The paper is relevant to the Phase 1 epistemic-humility experiment at three levels. First, GSM8K is already registered in the project dataset set and used as a benchmark surface; this note provides exact dataset statistics and the originating training protocol needed for provenance. Second, the token-level value-function verifier is a direct antecedent of process reward models and of the probe-as-readout paradigm: it demonstrates that a discriminator trained on the same data as the generator can extract a correctness signal the generator cannot express through its output alone. This is the generation-discrimination gap in a controlled math setting, and it mirrors the ITI finding (probe accuracy 84% vs generation truthfulness 31%) and the Amendment O result (linear readout AUROC 0.997 on an axis the model behavior does not express). The team's framing of the verifier as the generation-discrimination gap in a math domain is interpretive synthesis rather than a claim made in the paper. Third, the data-scaling comparison (Fig 5) shows that verification improves faster with more training data than finetuning, which suggests that trained-probe or readout-based paths may scale differently from behavior-only training, a hypothesis the auxiliary-head arc (Amendments O-Q-R) is testing. The caveat that limits direct transfer: this paper's verifier is a trained classifier over complete solution text, while the project's probe work reads internal activations. Both exploit the discrimination/generation distinction, but the signal source differs and the correctness probe underpowering concern (Amendment O / correctness-probe-underpowered memory) applies to the activation-readout path, not to this paper's verifier path.
