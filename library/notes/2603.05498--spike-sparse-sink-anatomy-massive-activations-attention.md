## Abstract

We study two recurring phenomena in Transformer language models: massive activations, in which a small number of tokens exhibit extreme outliers in a few channels, and attention sinks, in which certain tokens attract disproportionate attention mass regardless of semantic relevance. Prior work observes that these phenomena frequently co-occur and often involve the same tokens, but their functional roles and causal relationship remain unclear. Through systematic experiments, we show that the co-occurrence is largely an architectural artifact of modern Transformer design, and that the two phenomena serve related but distinct functions. Massive activations operate globally: they induce near-constant hidden representations that persist across layers, effectively functioning as implicit parameters of the model. Attention sinks operate locally: they modulate attention outputs across heads and bias individual heads toward short-range dependencies. We identify the pre-norm configuration as the key choice that enables the co-occurrence, and show that ablating it causes the two phenomena to decouple.

## Claims

- Evidence label: cross-model empirical characterization. Massive activations follow a reliable three-stage "step-up / plateau / step-down" life cycle: one or two early feed-forward blocks inject extreme values into a handful of channels via the residual connection, intermediate blocks passively propagate them, and one or a few late blocks neutralize them with opposite-sign contributions, confining the outliers to intermediate layers across all seven evaluated Llama/Qwen models (Figure 1; Table 1).
- Evidence label: ablation experiment. Ablating the normalization configuration decouples massive activations from attention sinks: sandwich normalization and DynamicTanh suppress spike magnitude by roughly 86-96% (3818 to 520/153) relative to the pre-norm RMSNorm baseline while leaving the sink ratio nearly unchanged or higher, showing the co-occurrence is an artifact of pre-norm RMSNorm rather than a functional necessity (Table 5; Section 4.2.2).
- Evidence label: ablation experiment. Attention sinks can be eliminated by input-conditioned (per-channel or per-head) attention gating: sink ratio drops from 46.0% to 4.5%/6.4% and spike magnitude falls from 3818 to roughly 190-200 with negligible perplexity change, whereas unconditional or static (positional/token-embedding) gates fail to suppress sinks, indicating sinks function as a learned, input-dependent gating workaround the model abandons once given an explicit gate (Table 7; Section 4.3.2).
- Evidence label: ablation experiment. Attention sinks are induced by short-context training rather than being an inherent property of attention: restricting the training loss to long-range-only sequence positions collapses the sink ratio from about 46% down to 1.2-13.0%, showing sinks primarily support short-range dependency prediction within a globally-attending mechanism (Table 8; Section 4.3.3).
