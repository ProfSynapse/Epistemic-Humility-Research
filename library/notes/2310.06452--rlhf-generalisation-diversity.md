## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) have been used in some of the most widely deployed AI models to date, such as OpenAI's ChatGPT or Anthropic's Claude. While there has been significant work developing these methods, our understanding of the benefits and downsides of each stage in RLHF is still limited. To fill this gap, we present an extensive analysis of how each stage of the process (i.e. supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modelling, and RLHF) affects two key properties: out-of-distribution (OOD) generalisation and output diversity. OOD generalisation is crucial given the wide range of real-world scenarios in which these models are being used, while output diversity refers to the model's ability to generate varied outputs and is important for a variety of use cases. We perform our analysis across two base models on both summarisation and instruction following tasks, the latter being highly relevant for current LLM use cases. We find that RLHF generalises better than SFT to new inputs, particularly as the distribution shift between train and test becomes larger. However, RLHF significantly reduces output diversity compared to SFT across a variety of measures, implying a tradeoff in current LLM fine-tuning methods between generalisation and diversity. Our results provide guidance on which fine-tuning method should be used depending on the application, and show that more research is needed to improve the tradeoff between generalisation and diversity.

## Summary

Kirk et al. (2023) run a systematic study of how each stage of the standard RLHF pipeline (SFT, reward modelling, and on-policy RL via PPO) affects out-of-distribution (OOD) generalisation and output diversity. They fine-tune LLaMA 7B on two tasks: reddit-post summarisation (TL;DR dataset, with CNN/DailyMail as the OOD target) and instruction following (AlpacaFarm Self-Instruct as in-distribution, AlpacaEval and a novel Sequential Instructions set as OOD). Best-of-N sampling (BoN, N=16) is evaluated as an analysis baseline that isolates reward-model effects from RL-optimization effects; it is not used in practice due to high inference cost. The main finding is a clean tension: RLHF improves absolute performance both in-distribution and OOD relative to SFT, and this generalisation advantage becomes pronounced only for hard distribution shifts (Sequential Instructions). On easy shifts the relative generalisation gap can favour SFT (Section 7 qualification: "in less difficult shifts RLHF generalises similarly or slightly worse than SFT as measured by generalisation gap"). At the same time, RLHF substantially collapses per-input output diversity on EAD (syntactic) and Sentence-BERT (semantic) metrics; NLI diversity shows no meaningful difference. Across-input diversity is also reduced, though less dramatically, and BoN does not replicate this collapse, implicating the RL optimisation step rather than reward-model filtering. Increasing the KL penalty coefficient fails to recover diversity and instead simultaneously hurts performance and diversity.

- Reward model OOD accuracy on CNN/DailyMail: 71.6%, vs 75.8% in-distribution on TL;DR; authors argue this small drop likely reflects no real OOD degradation, so all of BoN's OOD performance loss is attributable to the underlying SFT model degrading OOD rather than reward-model failure (Section 6.1, Summarisation)
- BoN (N=16) outperforms RLHF which outperforms SFT both in-distribution and OOD on summarisation (GPT-4 PvR win rate vs reference) (Figure 2, Section 6.1)
- In instruction following, RLHF outperforms BoN across all three datasets (ID AlpacaFarm Self-Instruct, OOD AlpacaEval, OOD Sequential Instructions); this ordering inverts the summarisation result (Figure 3, Section 6.1)
- RLHF and BoN head-to-head win rate vs SFT both improve by approximately 3.5 percentage points from AlpacaFarm Self-Instruct (ID) to AlpacaEval (OOD), indicating both methods generalise better than SFT even on the easier OOD shift (Figure 4, Section 6.1)
- On the harder Sequential Instructions OOD task, RLHF generalises much better than SFT; on the easier AlpacaEval OOD shift, GPT-4 PvR shows similar generalisation gaps across methods (Section 6.1, Instruction Following)
- Per-input output diversity: RLHF much lower than SFT on EAD and Sentence-BERT metrics; NLI diversity shows no meaningful difference between models (Figure 5, Section 6.2)
- Across-input diversity: SFT slightly higher than RLHF on EAD and Sentence-BERT, but gap is much smaller than in per-input case; BoN shows similar or higher across-input diversity than SFT on these metrics, ruling out reward-model filtering as the primary driver of collapse (Figure 6, Section 6.2)
- Increasing KL penalty coefficient: leads to a drop in performance and a drop in per-input diversity rather than a recovery of diversity, showing the KL penalty does not mediate the generalisation-diversity tradeoff (Section 6.3, Appendix I)

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides a direct empirical prior for the Phase 1 SFT vs DPO vs KTO abstention study. The finding that RLHF generalises better than SFT primarily at hard distribution shifts sets up a testable prediction: if DPO or KTO produce RLHF-like policy improvement (as argued by their derivations), they should also show stronger OOD abstention generalisation than SFT, but mainly on the harder evaluation splits. The diversity-collapse finding is directly relevant to Phase 1 outcome interpretation: RLHF-trained models may appear to learn abstention but do so by collapsing to a narrow output mode rather than by sharpening genuine uncertainty signal. A per-input diversity measure on abstention outputs across training arms would test whether the SFT arm preserves more varied hedging and whether KTO (which preserves more SFT character by construction) sits between SFT and DPO on this axis. The BoN analysis baseline is the structural analogue of reward-model ablation: because BoN uses the reward model without RL optimisation and does not collapse per-input diversity, the mechanism implicated is the RL training loop itself, not preference-score selection. That framing maps onto Phase 3 mech-interp: the question of whether abstention signal in hidden states degrades after RLHF-style RL (but not after SFT or a BoN-like selection step) is exactly the kind of causal question the activation-candidate inventory is designed to test.
