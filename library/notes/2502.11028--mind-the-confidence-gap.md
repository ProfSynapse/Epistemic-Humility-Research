## Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) show remarkable proficiency in natural language tasks, yet their frequent overconfidence-misalignment between predicted confidence and true correctness-poses significant risks in critical decision-making applications. We present a comprehensive analysis on calibration in LLMs across nine LLMs and three factual Question-Answering (QA) datasets, systematically comparing standard free-generation settings against structured distractor-augmented prompts. Our evaluation reveals that explicitly incorporating distractors can substantially mitigate miscalibration, achieving relative accuracy improvements up to 460% and ECE reductions up to 90%. Despite general trends, we uncover nuanced findings: large RLHF-tuned models display inherent calibration strengths but can paradoxically suffer increased miscalibration on easier queries, whereas smaller models benefit disproportionately from distractor prompts but remain significantly miscalibrated. Through detailed analyses across question types, we identify persistent calibration failures, particularly in person-based queries. We conclude with concrete recommendations-targeted fine-tuning, structured prompting, and strategic model choice-to ensure reliable, trustworthy LLM deployments.

## Summary

Chhikara (2025) runs a systematic calibration audit of six LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA3-8B, LLaMA3.1-8B, Gemma2-9B) on the SimpleQA factual-QA benchmark (4,326 items), comparing two prompt regimes: a free-generation normal setting (N) where the model answers without answer options, and a distractor-augmented setting (D) where the model receives the correct answer plus three plausible but incorrect distractors in shuffled multiple-choice format. The distractor format reliably drives accuracy up and ECE down across all six models, but the magnitude and direction of the effect splits sharply by model scale. Smaller models (LLaMA, Gemma) gain the most in accuracy from structured choices, benefiting from elimination strategies, yet remain substantially miscalibrated in both settings. Larger RLHF-tuned models (GPT-4o) start with better calibration and gain the largest absolute ECE reduction in the distractor setting, but they are also more susceptible to distractor-induced errors: GPT-4o has 8.03% of questions harmed by the distractor format, higher than any smaller model. Question-type analysis (Table 2) shows that across six models the mean baseline ECE is 0.72 for Date, Number, and Person categories (a three-way tie), and 0.64 for Place; in the distractor setting Person sees the largest relative ECE drop (69%, from 0.72 to 0.22) while Place sees the smallest (50%, from 0.64 to 0.32). The paper uses GPT-4o-mini as an LLM judge to classify responses as CORRECT, INCORRECT, or NOT_ATTEMPTED, validated against human annotation at Cohen's kappa = 0.82. Recommendations include calibration-aware fine-tuning, structured prompting with confidence regularization, and task-specific model selection.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper bears on three aspects of the Epistemic Humility experiment design. First, it provides a direct baseline gap measurement between free-generation and structured-prompt conditions on a factual-QA benchmark, exactly the kind of input-format manipulation that could serve as a diagnostic arm alongside the Phase 1 SFT/DPO/KTO training interventions: if training arms reduce the N-setting ECE gap, that is a meaningful outcome; if the distractor setting still rescues miscalibrated models after training, it implies the training effect is shallow. Second, the finding that GPT-4o (a heavily RLHF-tuned model) suffers a higher rate of distractor-induced harm (8.03%) than smaller open models (4.43-5.71%) is directionally consistent with the existing graph mechanisms [[reward-model-confidence-bias-drives-rlhf-overconfidence]] and [[rlhf-degrades-conditional-calibration]], though the paper does not itself attribute the pattern to RLHF and GPT-4o-mini (also RLHF-tuned) shows a lower harm rate (6.22%), so the inference is speculative. Third, the question-type stratification (Date/Number/Person/Place) suggests that any calibration intervention study should stratify its evaluation by question category, not just report aggregate ECE, a methodological norm directly applicable to the Phase 1 evaluation protocol. The verbalized-confidence evaluation methodology (models self-report a numeric confidence score) connects to [[verbalized-confidence]] and [[few-shot-unlocks-verbalized-calibration]], and the LLM-as-judge validation at kappa=0.82 supports the [[llm-as-judge]] evaluation convention the Phase 1 study inherits from AbstentionBench.
