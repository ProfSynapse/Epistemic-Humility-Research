## Abstract

When large language models are aligned via supervised fine-tuning, they may encounter new factual information that was not acquired through pre-training. It is often conjectured that this can teach the model the behavior of hallucinating factually incorrect responses, as the model is trained to generate facts that are not grounded in its pre-existing knowledge. In this work, we study the impact of such exposure to new knowledge on the capability of the fine-tuned model to utilize its pre-existing knowledge. To this end, we design a controlled setup, focused on closed-book QA, where we vary the proportion of the fine-tuning examples that introduce new knowledge. We demonstrate that large language models struggle to acquire new factual knowledge through fine-tuning, as fine-tuning examples that introduce new knowledge are learned significantly slower than those consistent with the model's knowledge. However, we also find that as the examples with new knowledge are eventually learned, they linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate. Taken together, our results highlight the risk in introducing new factual knowledge through fine-tuning, and support the view that large language models mostly acquire factual knowledge through pre-training, whereas fine-tuning teaches them to use it more efficiently.

## Summary

Gekhman et al. design a controlled closed-book QA study using PaLM 2-M and EntityQuestions to measure how the proportion of knowledge-boundary-crossing fine-tuning examples shapes the fine-tuned model's tendency to hallucinate. They introduce SliCK (Sampling-based Categorization of Knowledge), a four-tier taxonomy that labels each (question, answer) pair as HighlyKnown, MaybeKnown, WeaklyKnown, or Unknown relative to the model, by computing P_Correct from 10 random 4-shot prompts with greedy and sampled decoding. Using this taxonomy, they vary the Unknown fraction in fixed-size SFT datasets and measure test accuracy as a proxy for hallucination in a disjoint train/test setup. Three main findings emerge. First, fitting Unknown examples linearly increases hallucination: a linear model of in-distribution test accuracy yields beta_unk = -8.3 (R^2 = 0.86), while the same model for out-of-distribution relations yields beta_unk = -3.0 (R^2 = 0.95). The negative coefficient for Unknown and positive coefficient for Known (beta_kn = +7.3 in-distribution, +3.2 OOD) hold simultaneously, and both effects generalize across held-out relation types, implying the model learns a hallucination behavior rather than merely forgetting known facts. Second, Unknown examples are learned substantially slower than Known examples: at early stopping, the model has fitted most Known examples but very few Unknown ones, which is why early stopping largely neutralizes the Unknown hallucination risk. When training runs to convergence, Unknown examples become severely harmful, and filtering them out eliminates this risk without cost to early-stop performance. Third, fine-tuning composition matters within the Known tier: the MaybeKnown-only dataset (D_MaybeKnown) achieves the highest overall accuracy at early stopping (43.6%), outperforming the HighlyKnown-only dataset (40.5%), because MaybeKnown training examples teach the model to handle borderline test questions it can sometimes but not always answer greedily. The paper also provides a preliminary Appendix K experiment showing that relabeling Unknown training examples with uncertainty expressions ("I don't know") reduces their negative effect, pointing toward that intervention as the most promising mitigation.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper bears directly on three aspects of Phase 1 experiment design. First, Table 1's linear coefficients (beta_unk = -8.3 in-distribution, -3.0 OOD) quantify the per-unit cost of including knowledge-boundary-crossing examples in an SFT corpus, giving the experiment a concrete prior: even a modest Unknown fraction in the SFT arm's training data can degrade downstream question-answering accuracy, which motivates auditing each Phase 1 SFT recipe against a SliCK-style annotation before locking the data mixture. Second, Section 7 notes that early stopping works when all tasks share a single optimal stopping point, but becomes unreliable in multi-task instruction tuning where distinct tasks peak at different epochs; this is precisely the Phase 1 scenario, making Unknown-example filtering (rather than early stopping) the more robust mitigation to implement. Third, Appendix K's preliminary relabeling experiment, where Unknown training examples are replaced with "I don't know" supervision targets, directly previews the idk-sft arm: the positive preliminary result implies that relabeling is potentially the best-of-both-worlds approach that avoids hallucination without simply discarding training signal. A caution from Section 5 is that filtering all the way to HighlyKnown examples is suboptimal; MaybeKnown examples are needed to handle borderline test questions, which means any knowledge-boundary filter in Phase 1 should preserve MaybeKnown examples rather than cutting at a hard Known/Unknown threshold. Because this paper uses exact-match QA accuracy as its outcome rather than a calibration or abstention metric, it does not populate effects.csv directly, but it provides the causal mechanism that motivates the SFT data-curation choices the experiment tests.

## Claims

- The proportion of Unknown SFT examples is linearly and negatively correlated with closed-book QA test accuracy (beta_unk=-8.3, R^2=0.86 in-distribution; beta_unk=-3.0, R^2=0.95 out-of-distribution), confirming that fitting new knowledge through fine-tuning drives hallucination. (Table 1, Section 4.4) [[slick]]
- Unknown fine-tuning examples are learned substantially slower than Known examples during SFT; at early stopping, the model has fitted most Known but very few Unknown examples, explaining why early stopping neutralizes the hallucination risk of Unknown examples. (Figure 1, Figure 4, Section 4.3) [[hallucination]]
- Filtering out Unknown examples from the fine-tuning dataset achieves performance equivalent to early stopping, without sacrificing accuracy — providing a practical mitigation that avoids the need for development-set monitoring. (Figure 3(b), Section 4.2) [[supervised-finetuning]]
- Fine-tuning only on HighlyKnown examples does not yield the best results; incorporating MaybeKnown examples (facts with lower certainty) is important for handling borderline questions at test time, indicating that the optimal SFT data composition is not purely the highest-confidence Known examples.
