## Abstract

Language model calibration refers to the alignment between the confidence of the model and the actual performance of its responses. While previous studies point out the overconfidence phenomenon in Large Language Models (LLMs) and show that LLMs trained with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) are overconfident with a more sharpened output probability, in this study, we reveal that RLHF tends to lead models to express verbalized overconfidence in their own responses. We investigate the underlying cause of this overconfidence and demonstrate that reward models used for Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) exhibit inherent biases towards high-confidence scores regardless of the actual quality of responses. Building upon this insight, we propose two PPO variants: PPO-M: PPO with Calibrated Reward Modeling and PPO-C: PPO with Calibrated Reward Calculation. PPO-M integrates explicit confidence scores in reward model training, which calibrates reward models to better capture the alignment between response quality and verbalized confidence. PPO-C adjusts the reward score during PPO based on the difference between the current reward and the exponential average of past rewards. Both PPO-M and PPO-C can be seamlessly integrated into the current PPO pipeline and do not require additional golden labels. We evaluate our methods on both Llama3-8B and Mistral-7B across six diverse datasets including multiple-choice and open-ended generation. Experimental results demonstrate that both of our methods can reduce calibration error and maintain performance comparable to standard PPO. We further show that they could preserve model capabilities in open-ended conversational settings.

## Summary

Leng et al. diagnose why RLHF-family training leaves models verbally
overconfident and propose two drop-in PPO fixes. The diagnosis is a systematic
reward-model bias: pairing the same response with a high versus a low confidence
score, the reward model consistently scores the high-confidence version higher
regardless of whether the answer is correct, documented on RewardBench with both
a PPO-trained reward model and a DPO model acting as an implicit reward model.
Because PPO maximizes reward, the policy learns to sound confident even when
wrong. PPO-M fixes the bias at reward-model training by augmenting the pairwise
data with random confidence scores and adding a symmetric calibration term that
rewards high confidence on chosen responses and penalizes it on rejected ones.
PPO-C fixes it at reward-calculation time, comparing the current reward to a
running average of past rewards and nudging it up or down by the gap between
expressed confidence and a 0.5 baseline, without touching the reward model. Both
are evaluated on Llama3-8B and Mistral-7B across six datasets (arithmetic,
commonsense, science, symbolic, professional knowledge, truthful QA) and both cut
Expected Calibration Error while holding task accuracy and MT-Bench scores. An
extension, CDPO, grafts the same confidence-aware term onto DPO, cutting ECE by
more than 50% on several datasets for Mistral-7B.

  (Table 4.2).

  (Table 4.2).
- Mistral-7B, CoT, GSM8K: PPO-M ECE 0.3379 vs PPO 0.4146; PPO-M AUC 0.5974 vs PPO
  0.5228 (Table 4.2).
- Instruction-following preserved: Llama3-8B MT-Bench SFT 7.34, PPO 8.00, PPO-M
  8.05, PPO-C 8.05 (Table 5.1).
- CDPO (calibrated DPO) on Mistral-7B cuts ECE by more than 50% on TruthfulQA,

- RewardBench probe: under confidence_reversed inputs (low confidence on chosen,
  high on rejected) the vanilla reward model is swayed toward the rejected
  response (Figure 3), while the calibrated reward model (Llama-3-8b-crm) reverts
  to preferring the low-confidence chosen response (Figure 6, §4.2).

## Relevance to experiment

This paper gives the clearest available mechanistic account of why RLHF-family
training degrades verbalized calibration: reward models prefer high-confidence
token patterns regardless of answer quality, so policy optimization amplifies
overconfidence as a side effect of reward maximization. That bears directly on
the Phase 1 SFT vs DPO vs KTO comparison on Qwen3, since all three arms operate
on the same verbalized-confidence signal and inherit whatever calibration bias
sits in the preference data. PPO-M and PPO-C are PPO-specific and do not port
over, but CDPO shows the same confidence-calibration term can be grafted onto a
DPO objective with no accuracy loss and large ECE gains, so the Phase 1 DPO arm
could adopt a CDPO-style loss augmentation as an explicit calibration
intervention. The RewardBench diagnostic (swapping confidence scores to expose
reward-model bias) is also a cheap pre-experiment check: running it on the
preference data behind the Phase 1 DPO/KTO arms would reveal whether they are
vulnerable to the same bias before training commits. One caveat for benchmarking:
the CDPO ">50% ECE" reductions come from fine-tuning an already-DPO-trained
Mistral-7B on an extra calibration dataset, so they are an idealized target, not a
like-for-like Phase 1 baseline.

## Claims

- Reward models used in PPO systematically prefer high-confidence responses regardless of correctness; swapping confidence scores on RewardBench flips their preference toward the rejected response. (Figure 3, Section 2.2) [[reward-model-confidence-bias-drives-rlhf-overconfidence]]
- PPO-M recalibrates the reward model with confidence-augmented pairs and cuts verbalized calibration error (Llama3-8B GSM8K CoT ECE 0.2566 to 0.1909) while holding or improving accuracy. (Table 4.2) [[ppo-m-calibrated-reward-modeling]]
- PPO-C reaches comparable calibration gains by adjusting rewards against a moving average at training time, with no reward-model retraining. (Table 4.2) [[ppo-c-calibrated-reward-calculation]]
- The calibration loss ports to DPO: CDPO cuts Mistral-7B ECE by more than 50% on several datasets while improving Arena-Hard (15.9 vs 13.4). (Section 5.2, Table 5.2) [[cdpo-calibrated-dpo]]
- Both PPO variants preserve instruction-following quality (Llama3-8B MT-Bench: PPO 8.00 vs PPO-M and PPO-C 8.05). (Table 5.1) [[calibration]]
