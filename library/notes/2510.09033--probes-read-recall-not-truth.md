## Abstract

Recent work suggests that LLMs "know what they don't know", positing that hallucinated and factually correct outputs arise from distinct internal processes and can therefore be distinguished using internal signals. However, hallucinations have multifaceted causes: beyond simple knowledge gaps, they can emerge from training incentives that encourage models to exploit statistical shortcuts or spurious associations learned during pretraining. In this paper, we argue that when LLMs rely on such learned associations to produce hallucinations, their internal processes are mechanistically similar to those of factual recall, as both stem from strong statistical correlations encoded in the model's parameters. To verify this, we propose a novel taxonomy categorizing hallucinations into Unassociated Hallucinations (UHs), where outputs lack parametric grounding, and Associated Hallucinations (AHs), which are driven by spurious associations. Through mechanistic analysis, we compare their computational processes and hidden-state geometries with factually correct outputs. Our results show that hidden states primarily reflect whether the model is recalling parametric knowledge rather than the truthfulness of the output itself. Consequently, AHs exhibit hidden-state geometries that largely overlap with factual outputs, rendering standard detection methods ineffective. In contrast, UHs exhibit distinctive, clustered representations that facilitate reliable detection.

## Summary

Cheang et al. argue that LLM internal states encode knowledge recall, not truthfulness, which explains why hallucination detectors built on hidden states or confidence signals have a systematic blind spot. The paper proposes a three-way taxonomy: Factual Associations (FAs, correct outputs grounded in subject knowledge), Associated Hallucinations (AHs, errors that still draw on parametric associations triggered by the subject), and Unassociated Hallucinations (UHs, errors detached from subject parametric knowledge). Using causal mediation analysis across LLaMA-3-8B and Mistral-7B-v0.3, the authors show that FAs and AHs follow the same information-flow signature through early-layer subject representations, mid-layer attention propagation from subject to last token, and late-layer last-token accumulation, while UHs show weaker activations at all three stages. As a result, UHs form a compact, separable cluster in representation space while AHs overlap extensively with FAs. Across five detection methods (subject-representation probe, attention-flow probe, last-token probe, answer-token probability, and subject-popularity heuristic), UH-only AUROC reaches 0.81-0.93 depending on method and model, but AH-only AUROC spans a much wider and lower range: from 0.46 (answer-token probability, Mistral) and 0.48 (subject popularity, LLaMA) at the floor, up to 0.69 (last-token probe, LLaMA) at the ceiling. The subject-popularity and answer-probability baselines are essentially at chance on AHs. Mixing AHs into training further dilutes UH detection (AUROC drops from approximately 0.9 to approximately 0.8). Refusal tuning trained on UH samples generalizes within the UH category (82% refusal ratio, LLaMA) but barely transfers to AHs (28%), while also spuriously refusing FAs at 29.5%. Refusal tuning on AH samples performs even worse in both directions.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper directly informs three aspects of the Epistemic Humility experiment.

First, for Phase 1 training-data stratification, the paper's refusal-tuning results show that UH samples and AH samples must be treated as separate categories, not pooled. Refusal tuning generalizes within the UH category (82% refusal ratio) but barely transfers to AHs (28%), and the reverse is even worse. This is a practical constraint on how refusal or abstention training data should be constructed: a dataset that mixes AH and UH samples without stratification will produce a model that is neither reliable at refusing UH inputs nor at refusing AH inputs. Any Phase 1 arm that trains on mixed hallucination samples without this distinction risks the same dilution effect seen in Figure 10 (AUROC dropping from 0.9 to 0.8).

Second, for Phase 3 activation candidate inventory, the paper supplies a mechanistic map of which hidden-state sites differ between categories: subject-token norms in early MLP layers, attention-contribution norms from subject to last token in mid-layers, and last-token hidden states at layer 25 (LLaMA). Each of these is a candidate probe site, and the paper gives expected ranges. For UH detection, white-box probes at any of these sites reach 0.81-0.93 AUROC; for AH detection, even the best probe (last-token, LLaMA) reaches only 0.69, and black-box baselines fall to 0.46-0.52. These ranges vary meaningfully by probe type and model: Mistral subject-representation probe reaches 0.81 on UH but only 0.57 on AH, while the LLaMA last-token probe reaches 0.93 on UH and 0.69 on AH. Any Phase 3 result should be interpreted against the specific probe type and model rather than a single expected target number.

Third, the paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why internal probes have this limitation. Hidden states reflect the information-flow pattern (subject-driven recall versus not) rather than output correctness. This is the key caveat when evaluating whether Phase 3 probes read epistemic signal or familiarity signal, and it aligns with the concern already documented at [[estimator-divergence-invalidates-single-probe-faithfulness]] and [[p-ik-ood-generalization-gap]].

## Claims

- LLMs employ the same internal knowledge-recall information flow (subject representations → attention propagation → last-token accumulation) when generating AHs as when generating FAs, causing AH hidden-state geometries to overlap with FA geometries and rendering internal probes ineffective for AH detection. (Section 4.1, Figure 2) [[probe-reads-recall-not-truth]]
- UHs concentrate among low-popularity subjects (94% of that stratum in LLaMA-3-8B are UHs) while AHs arise mainly on popular subjects (14% of the high-popularity stratum), showing that subject-representation norms reflect training frequency rather than factual correctness and contradicting the assumption that popularity predicts hallucination uniformly. (Section 4.2.3, Figure 5) [[ah-uh-hallucination-taxonomy]]
- Across five detection methods and two models, AH-only AUROC spans 0.46-0.69 (with black-box baselines at or near chance) while UH-only AUROC spans 0.81-0.93, confirming that hallucination type determines detectability more than detector choice. (Table 2) [[probe-reads-recall-not-truth]]
- Refusal tuning on UH samples generalizes within the UH category (82% refusal ratio on held-out UHs in LLaMA-3-8B) but fails to transfer to AHs (28% refusal ratio), because UHs share a common activation subspace while AH representations are subject-specific and diverse. (Section 6, Figure 11) [[refusal-tuning-fails-to-generalize-across-hallucination-types]]
- Mixing AH and UH samples in hallucination detection training dilutes UH detection AUROC from approximately 0.9 (UH Only) to approximately 0.8 (Full setting), showing that heterogeneous hallucination categories interfere with probe training. (Section 5, Figure 10) [[refusal-tuning-fails-to-generalize-across-hallucination-types]]
