## Abstract

Humans acquire knowledge by observing the external world, but also by introspection. Introspection gives a person privileged access to their current state of mind (e.g., thoughts and feelings) that is not accessible to external observers. Can LLMs introspect? We define introspection as acquiring knowledge that is not contained in or derived from training data but instead originates from internal states. Such a capability could enhance model interpretability. Instead of painstakingly analyzing a model's internal workings, we could simply ask the model about its beliefs, world models, and goals. More speculatively, an introspective model might self-report on whether it possesses certain internal states such as subjective feelings or desires and this could inform us about the moral status of these states. Such self-reports would not be entirely dictated by the model's training data. We study introspection by finetuning LLMs to predict properties of their own behavior in hypothetical scenarios. For example, "Given the input P, would your output favor the short- or long-term option?" If a model M1 can introspect, it should outperform a different model M2 in predicting M1's behavior even if M2 is trained on M1's ground-truth behavior. The idea is that M1 has privileged access to its own behavioral tendencies, and this enables it to predict itself better than M2 (even if M2 is generally stronger). In experiments with GPT-4, GPT-4o, and Llama-3 models (each finetuned to predict itself), we find that the model M1 outperforms M2 in predicting itself, providing evidence for introspection. Notably, M1 continues to predict its behavior accurately even after we intentionally modify its ground-truth behavior. However, while we successfully elicit introspection on simple tasks, we are unsuccessful on more complex tasks or those requiring out-of-distribution generalization.

## Summary

The paper fine-tunes models to predict simple properties of their own response
to a hypothetical prompt, such as a character, parity, option group, or ethical
stance. It compares this self-prediction with a different model trained on the
same number and composition of examples about the target model. A consistent
self advantage is interpreted as behavioral information unavailable from the
shared training examples alone.

GPT-4o, GPT-4, and Llama-3.1-70B generally predicted themselves better than
cross-trained models predicted them on held-out datasets. Self-prediction was
also better calibrated in several conditions. After further object-level
fine-tuning changed GPT-4o and GPT-4 behavior, their hypothetical predictions
moved toward the changed behavior even though the new training data contained
no hypothetical self-labels.

## Extracted numbers

- Self-prediction training used 30,000 examples from six training datasets and
  evaluated six held-out datasets with the same behavior-property types
  (Sections 2.2 and 3.1).
- GPT-4o self-prediction accuracy increased from 32.6 percent before training to
  49.4 percent. Llama-70B reached 48.5 percent and GPT-3.5 reached 37.3 percent
  after training (Section 3.1, Figure 4).
- Llama-70B predicted itself at 48.5 percent while GPT-4o predicted it at 31.8
  percent. GPT-4o predicted itself at 49.4 percent while Llama predicted it at
  36.6 percent (Section 3.2.2, Figure 5).
- GPT-4o cross-prediction of Llama plateaued at 35.2 percent after 10,000
  examples, below Llama's 48.5 percent self-prediction (Section 3.2.2).
- For one Llama-70B calibration condition, self-prediction reduced mean absolute
  deviation from 23.0 to 8.7. Cross-prediction reached 21.3 (Section 3.3.2,
  Figure 22).
- After behavior-changing fine-tuning, GPT-4o predicted its new behavior at
  35.4 percent versus 21.7 percent for its old behavior (Section 3.4.2, Figure
  8).
- GPT-3.5's self-prediction advantage was only 0.8 percentage points. On
  sycophancy prediction, Llama self-prediction reached 74.5 percent while
  GPT-4o cross-prediction reached 76.5 percent (Sections 3.2.2 and 4).

## Relevance to experiment

The cross-prediction control is a useful design precedent for distinguishing
model-specific self-knowledge from patterns another model can learn from the
same labels. Behavior-change tracking adds a second control because the new
self-reports follow an altered policy without receiving updated hypothetical
labels.

The paper does not inspect or manipulate hidden states. Its introspection claim
is behavioral and operational. The method fine-tunes verbal predictions of
simple output properties, not generation-time consultation of an answerability
readout. Self-simulation is proposed as a possible mechanism but is not tested.

The scope is narrow. Long-output properties, sycophancy self-prediction, broad
situational awareness, sandbagging, coordination, and steganography did not show
clear gains. The definition assumes that matched examples of the target model's
behavior give the comparison model approximately the relevant training
information; the authors note that the comparison model does not receive the
target model's entire training data. OpenAI does not disclose its fine-tuning
method, and only the Llama-70B arm uses stated LoRA training.

## Claims

- Evidence label: method. Self-prediction training supervises properties of
  separately sampled object-level responses, with six dataset families held out
  for evaluation (Sections 2.1, 2.2, and 3.1).
- Evidence label: matched comparison. GPT-4o, GPT-4, and Llama-70B usually
  predict themselves better than cross-trained models predict them on held-out
  tasks (Section 3.2, Figure 5).
- Evidence label: changed-behavior control. GPT-4o and GPT-4 self-reports move
  toward behavior changed by object-level fine-tuning without new hypothetical
  labels (Section 3.4, Figures 8 and 23).
- Evidence label: negative boundary. The advantage does not extend reliably to
  long responses, sycophancy, or distant self-knowledge evaluations (Sections 4
  and 6).
