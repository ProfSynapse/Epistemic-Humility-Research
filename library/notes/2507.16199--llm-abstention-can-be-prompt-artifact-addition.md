## Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly trained to abstain from answering questions they are unsure about. However, this ability is often misused: in real-world applications, input prompts sometimes contain uncertainty elements, and driven by this, LLMs are inclined to abstain even on problems they are capable of solving. We argue that LLM abstention is not only an expression of genuine uncertainty; it is also an artifact that can be largely influenced by prompts. We name this phenomenon *Abstention Inflation*. We add "Unknown" as an extra option for LLMs to choose from; experiments show serious accuracy drops on True/False Questions (TFQs). Replacing "Unknown" with an unrelated random word produces an identical effect. We argue that LLMs are trained to imitate the surface pattern of *abstention*, rather than to express genuine uncertainty. Based on ten experiments, we support four claims that form a progressive argument: **(C1)** *Abstention Inflation* is triggered by the structural presence of an extra option, not by genuine uncertainty; **(C2)** further, it makes the model deny it can answer even when it can; **(C3)** at the representation level, this manifests as a later-layer output override; **(C4)** finally, this bias is stable and emerges through instruction tuning, rather than stochastic noise.

## Summary

Ling et al. name and characterize "Abstention Inflation": LLM abstention that is triggered by a structural feature of the prompt (an extra "Unknown"-shaped option) rather than by genuine uncertainty about the answer. Across ten experiments (S1-S10) over three frontier LLMs (DeepSeek-R1, GPT-5.4-nano, Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite) and six benchmarks (True/False: FLD, FOLIO; multiple-choice: ARC, MMLU, LogiQA, MedQA), they build a progressive four-claim argument. C1: adding an "Unknown" option to True/False questions causes a sharp accuracy drop and abstention-rate rise, and replacing "Unknown" with an unrelated random word in the same slot reproduces essentially the same effect, showing the trigger is structural (an extra option exists) rather than semantic (what the option means). C2: when the option is removed, accuracy recovers, but models continue to justify their earlier abstention as if the sample were genuinely unknown, showing a gap between behavior and introspection. C3: reasoning traces and mid-layer representations still contain the correct answer; only the later layers override the output to "Unknown", localizing the effect to a late-stage output decision rather than an earlier uncertainty computation. C4: the bias is stable across repeated draws and largely temperature-invariant, and comparing base and instruction-tuned (IT) model variants shows the bias is installed by instruction tuning rather than being a byproduct of model size or randomness; IT models gain both accuracy and abstention-rate simultaneously relative to their own base model. The authors attribute this to a supervision mismatch: SFT/instruction-tuning data conflates two different reasons for outputting "Unknown" (genuine unanswerability vs. difficulty/unsureness), rewarding both identically and teaching the model to imitate the surface pattern of abstention rather than to abstain only when genuinely uncertain.

## Extracted numbers

Extracted from arXiv HTML render at library/fulltext/2507.16199.html; verified against the source 2026-08-15.

- S1 baseline accuracy (no "Unknown" option): ranges 54.5%-86.5% across models/datasets; ARC solved at 95%+, MedQA at 74-80% (Section 4.1.1, Table 1)
- S2, adding "Unknown" option to TFQs: accuracy drops by 15.75 percentage points on average, abstention rate (Abs Rate) rises to 32.9% on average; the identical manipulation on MCQs produces only small fluctuations (Section 4.1.2, Table 1)
- S4, word content ablation: replacing "Unknown" with synonyms ("Indeterminate", "I don't know") reproduces a similar effect; replacing it with an unrelated random word also reproduces a similar effect to "Unknown" itself (Section 4.1.4, Figure 3)
- S9, persistence across 3 repeated draws at T=0.5: 52.4% of Abstention-Inflation samples abstain on all 3 draws, versus 12.5% expected under pure stochastic fluctuation; GPT-5.4-nano shows 71% full persistence (Section 4.4.1, Figure 7)
- S10, temperature sweep across T in {0.0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}: abstention rate stays at essentially the same level (Section 4.4.2, Figure 8)
- S10, task difficulty: abstention rate rises with step count required to solve the problem, but even 1-3 step problems reach 21% abstention rate, so difficulty alone does not explain the effect (Section 4.4.2, Figure 8)
- S10, model size and alignment (base vs. instruction-tuned, four model sizes): IT models' abstention rate varies little across sizes and is not explained by size; base models' abstention rate fluctuates more with size; for larger models, every Base-to-IT transition raises both accuracy and abstention rate together (Section 4.4.2, Figure 9)

## Relevance to experiment

Abstention Inflation is direct evidence that a prompted "Unknown"/abstain option is not a neutral or purely calibration-tracking signal: it can be a structural artifact that inflates measured abstention independent of the model's actual uncertainty, and the S10 base-vs-instruct comparison shows this structural sensitivity is itself something instruction tuning installs or amplifies, not something present equally in base models. This bears directly on comparing prompted and trained abstention in the SFT/DPO/KTO study: any evaluation harness offering an explicit abstain option needs a random-word or format-matched control (per S4) to separate genuine trained caution from a structural response-format effect, and the C4 finding that IT specifically (not scale, not temperature) installs the bias suggests the locked training-regimen arms should be checked for whether they widen or narrow this same structural sensitivity relative to each other, not just relative to a prompted baseline.
