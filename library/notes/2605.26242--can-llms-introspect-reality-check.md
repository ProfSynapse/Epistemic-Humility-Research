## Abstract

Can large language models detect and report their own internal states? A number of recent studies have argued that they can. Drawing on lessons from human metacognition research, we argue that this conclusion may be premature. We identify two conditions that a paradigm needs to meet in order to establish introspection. First, the test needs to require privileged access: it should not be solvable using cues available in the input. Second, it needs to require second-order computation: second-order, meta-representations of first-order, task-related representations. This condition cannot be satisfied by task performance alone: it requires designs under which second-order and first-order accounts make divergent predictions. We re-examine two paradigms that have been used to argue for model introspection in light of these conditions. In the first, models must predict labels derived from their own hidden states; we find that classifiers that can only access the input match the models' in-context predictions, indicating that the original results do not demonstrate privileged access to internal representations. In the second paradigm, models must detect whether their internal states have been tampered with; we find they cannot reliably distinguish such interventions from manipulations of the input, suggesting that their success reflects generic anomaly detection rather than sensitivity to internal interventions in particular. We conclude that current evidence is insufficient to establish metacognitive monitoring in LLMs.

## Summary

The paper argues that strong evidence for introspection requires both privileged access to information unavailable from the input and a dissociable second-order computation about a first-order representation. It reassesses hidden-state self-report classification using input-only baselines and randomized labels. Layer-0 input features match the reported in-context predictions, while performance drops near baseline when semantic correlations are removed. It then extends concept-injection detection with a prompt-only gaslight condition. Open-weight models that detect activation interventions in a two-way task fail to distinguish activation changes from prompt-induced concept bias in a three-way task. The authors interpret both results as insufficient evidence for metacognitive monitoring.

## Extracted numbers

- In the Belief Dominance task, Llama-3-70B in-context accuracy was 0.46 plus or minus 0.02 for the BASE cluster and 0.54 plus or minus 0.05 for COUNTER. Layer-0 entity probes scored 0.49 to 0.50 and 0.55 to 0.57, respectively (Section 4.2, Table 1).
- For Gemma-3-27B, in-context accuracy was 0.48 plus or minus 0.02 for BASE and 0.42 plus or minus 0.04 for COUNTER. Layer-0 probes scored 0.55 to 0.57 and 0.48 to 0.50 (Section 4.2, Table 1).
- The Belief Dominance control used 900 examples, split evenly into train and test sets across 15 random splits. Each training set was balanced across three classes (Section 4.2.1).
- The gaslight condition used 500 samples per concept. The initial steering sweep used 500 samples total across concepts per layer-strength setting, followed by 250 samples per concept at the selected setting (Section 4.3.1).
- Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen-3-32B reproduced above-chance activation-intervention detection with few control false positives in the two-way task. Both failed to separate prompt and activation interventions in the three-way task; Gemma-3-27B showed the same qualitative pattern in the appendix (Section 4.3.2, Figure 3).

## Relevance to experiment

The input-only and randomized-label controls test whether an apparent internal-state report can be solved through visible semantic cues. The three-way source control forces generic anomaly detection and activation-specific monitoring to make different predictions. These controls are relevant to training studies because improved task accuracy alone would not show that a new second-order monitor was installed. The paper does not test answerability readouts, generation-time use of such readouts, or weights-level routing.

## Claims

- Evidence label: input-only baseline. Mean-pooled layer-0 probes match or exceed in-context prediction of PCA-derived and Belief Dominance labels, so success does not require access to the later hidden states used to construct those labels (Sections 4.1 and 4.2).
- Evidence label: semantic-decorrelation control. Randomly permuting labels before fitting the hidden-state proxy removes semantic regularities and reduces in-context performance near the majority baseline (Section 4.1, Figure 2).
- Evidence label: source-discrimination control. Models that detect activation injections in a binary task often classify prompt-only gaslight trials as injections and fail the explicit three-way distinction (Section 4.3, Figure 3).
- Evidence label: construct-validity argument. Privileged access is necessary but not sufficient for strong introspection; behavioral evidence must distinguish first-order sensitivity from a second-order representation of the first-order state (Section 3).
- Evidence label: qualification of prior work. The paper does not directly retest the inaccessible Claude models used by Lindsey 2025. Its open-weight results challenge the activation-specific interpretation of binary detection, not the reported Claude response frequencies themselves (Sections 4.3 and A).
- Evidence label: limitation. Passing the new three-way source test would still not establish a meta-representation because source-specific detection can also arise through a first-order signature detector (Appendix A).
