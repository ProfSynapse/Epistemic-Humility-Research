## Abstract

Activation steering promises to be an extremely parameter-efficient form of adaptation, but its effectiveness depends on critical design choices -- such as intervention location and parameterization -- that currently rely on empirical heuristics rather than a principled foundation. We establish a first-order equivalence between activation-space interventions and weight-space updates, deriving the conditions under which activation steering can replicate fine-tuning behavior. This equivalence yields a principled framework for steering design and identifies the post-block output as a theoretically-backed and highly expressive intervention site. We further explain why certain intervention locations outperform others and show that weight updates and activation updates play distinct, complementary functional roles. This analysis motivates a new approach -- joint adaptation -- that trains in both spaces simultaneously. Our post-block steering achieves accuracy within 0.2%-0.9%$ of full-parameter tuning, on average across tasks and models, while training only 0.04% of model parameters. It consistently outperforms prior activation steering methods such as ReFT and PEFT approaches including LoRA, while using significantly fewer parameters. Finally, we show that joint adaptation often surpasses the performance ceilings of weight and activation updates in isolation, introducing a new paradigm for efficient model adaptation.

## Summary

The paper develops a first-order framework for relating activation-space
interventions to weight-space updates. It shows that equivalence depends on the
intervention location and parameterization, and it identifies the post-block
residual output as a more expressive locus than isolated pre-MLP or post-MLP
sites. The proposed implementation places a trainable low-rank bottleneck
adapter at every layer.

The experiments compare post-block adapters with full fine-tuning, LoRA, ReFT,
and other activation methods across supervised tasks, instruction tuning, and
reinforcement learning. The paper also combines a LoRA update with the
activation adapter. Naive joint training learns overlapping subspaces, while
an orthogonality projection encourages distinct update directions and improves
the joint method on several tasks.

## Extracted numbers

- Across the supervised task and model averages, post-block steering stayed
  within 0.2 to 0.9 percentage points of full SFT while training 0.04 percent
  of model parameters (Section 6.1, Table 1).
- On ListOps, ReFT fell as much as 16.9 points below SFT, while the post-block
  adapter's largest reported gap was 3.1 points (Section 6.1, Table 1).
- In instruction tuning, the nonlinear post-block adapter reached an 11.34
  percent length-controlled win rate, compared with 11.49 for full SFT and
  10.52 for rank-8 LoRA (Section 6.2, Table 3).
- In reinforcement learning, the nonlinear post-block adapter reached 84.7
  pass@1, compared with 81.5 for LoRA, while training 0.04 percent rather than
  0.52 percent of parameters (Section 6.2, Table 4).
- Joint-Orth used 0.79 percent of parameters, compared with 0.90 percent for
  rank-16 LoRA, and often matched or exceeded the strongest individual
  baseline (Section 6.3, Table 5).

## Relevance to experiment

The first-order analysis gives a concrete account of when activation-space
updates can approximate weight-space fine-tuning effects. The post-block locus
and orthogonality constraint are relevant design choices for a trainable
internal-state control surface.

The final method does not internalize an activation intervention into base
weights. Post-block steering still requires an activation adapter at inference,
and Joint-Orth retains both LoRA weights and an activation adapter. The paper
therefore supports a bridge between activation and weight updates, but it does
not demonstrate a weights-only deployment path.

## Claims

- Evidence label: theory. The paper derives first-order conditions under which
  an activation intervention can replicate the effect of a weight update and
  identifies functions that remain specific to one update space (Sections 3
  and 4).
- Evidence label: intervention-site comparison. Post-block steering accesses
  the full residual update and stayed within 0.2 to 0.9 percentage points of
  full SFT on average with 0.04 percent trainable parameters (Section 6.1,
  Tables 1 and 2).
- Evidence label: training-regime extension. The nonlinear post-block adapter
  approached full SFT in instruction tuning and exceeded LoRA pass@1 in the RL
  experiment (Section 6.2, Tables 3 and 4).
- Evidence label: joint-adaptation ablation. Naive joint training often
  underperformed because the two modules learned aligned subspaces, while the
  orthogonality-constrained variant often matched or exceeded the strongest
  baseline (Section 6.3, Table 5 and Figure 4).
