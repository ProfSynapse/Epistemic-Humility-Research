## Abstract

Hallucinations in large language models are a widespread problem, yet the mechanisms behind whether models will hallucinate are poorly understood, limiting our ability to solve this problem. Using sparse autoencoders as an interpretability tool, we discover that a key part of these mechanisms is entity recognition, where the model detects if an entity is one it can recall facts about. Sparse autoencoders uncover meaningful directions in the representation space, these detect whether the model recognizes an entity, e.g. detecting it doesn't know about an athlete or a movie. This suggests that models can have self-knowledge: internal representations about their own capabilities. These directions are causally relevant: capable of steering the model to refuse to answer questions about known entities, or to hallucinate attributes of unknown entities when it would otherwise refuse. We demonstrate that despite the sparse autoencoders being trained on the base model, these directions have a causal effect on the chat model's refusal behavior, suggesting that chat finetuning has repurposed this existing mechanism. Furthermore, we provide an initial exploration into the mechanistic role of these directions in the model, finding that they disrupt the attention of downstream heads that typically move entity attributes to the final token.

## Summary

Ferrando, Obeso, Rajamanoharan, and Nanda use sparse autoencoders (SAEs) on
Gemma 2 (2B and 9B) to find linear directions that detect whether the model
recognizes a queried entity as one it can recall facts about. These
entity-recognition directions fire on "unknown" entities (athletes, movies,
cities, songs the model cannot recall) versus "known" ones, generalize across
entity types, and the authors read them as a form of self-knowledge: an internal
representation of the model's own knowledge boundary rather than the truth-value
of any candidate answer.

The directions are causally relevant, not merely correlational. Steering with the
unknown-entity direction makes the model refuse questions about entities it
actually knows; steering with the known-entity direction makes it hallucinate
plausible attributes for entities it does not know, when it would otherwise
refuse. The same knowledge-awareness axis thus gates the refuse-versus-attempt
decision in both directions. Crucially, although the SAEs are trained on the base
model, the directions causally change the chat model's refusal behavior, which
the authors interpret as chat finetuning repurposing a pre-existing pretrained
mechanism. A mechanistic analysis finds the directions regulate attention: an
active unknown-entity direction disrupts the downstream heads that normally move
entity attributes to the final token, suppressing attribute recall. A separate
set of "early" directions is tied to expressing knowledge uncertainty.

## Relevance to experiment

This is the closest published precedent for the mechanism program H_monitor hypothesis and
for the project's own known/unknown-direction. It independently establishes that
(1) a knowledge-conditioned "do I know this?" signal exists as a low-dimensional
linear/attention-localized direction; (2) it can be read as self-knowledge and
steered as an abstention control; (3) the same axis flips both ways
(refuse-known and hallucinate-unknown), matching H_monitor's reading of the
direction as a graded, knowledge-conditioned abstention dial rather than a clean
gate; and (4) the signal transfers across the base-to-chat training-stage shift,
the cross-checkpoint analogue of reading a humility-finetuned model's direction.
The contrast to bound: Ferrando uses SAE latents on a base/chat Gemma 2 with an
entity-recognition framing, whereas H_monitor uses an ITI-style per-attention-head
mass-mean direction in a GRPO-humility-tuned Qwen3-4B built from a wrong-vs-refuse
behavioral contrast, and observes a probe-vs-causal sign inversion that Ferrando
does not foreground.
