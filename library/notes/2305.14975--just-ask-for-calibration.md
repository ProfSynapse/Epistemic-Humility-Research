## Abstract

A trustworthy real-world prediction system should produce well-calibrated confidence scores; that is, its confidence in an answer should be indicative of the likelihood that the answer is correct, enabling deferral to an expert in cases of low-confidence predictions. Recent studies have shown that unsupervised pre-training produces large language models (LMs) whose conditional probabilities are remarkably well-calibrated. However, the most widely-used LMs are fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF-LMs), and some studies have suggested that RLHF-LMs produce conditional probabilities that are very poorly calibrated. In light of this perceived weakness, we conduct a broad evaluation of methods for extracting confidence scores from RLHF-LMs. For RLHF-LMs such as ChatGPT, GPT-4, and Claude, we find that verbalized confidences emitted as output tokens are typically better-calibrated than the model's conditional probabilities on the TriviaQA, SciQ, and TruthfulQA benchmarks, often reducing the expected calibration error by a relative 50%.

## Summary

Tian et al. ask whether RLHF-tuned models can recover well-calibrated confidence estimates without any calibration-specific fine-tuning. They evaluate five models (gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4, Claude-1, Claude-2, and Llama-2-70B-Chat) on TriviaQA, SciQ, and TruthfulQA using a systematic grid of confidence-elicitation strategies: log-probability baselines, single-step verbalization (Verb. 1S top-k, k in {1,2,4}), two-step brainstorm-then-score verbalization (Verb. 2S top-k), chain-of-thought verbalization (Verb. 2S CoT), and linguistic expressions of uncertainty (Ling. 1S human / Ling. 1S-opt.), all optionally combined with post-hoc temperature scaling. The paper's central finding is that verbalized confidence is typically better-calibrated than conditional log-probabilities for these RLHF models, often reducing ECE by a relative 50%. Multi-hypothesis verbalization (asking the model to brainstorm several candidate answers before assigning probabilities) further improves calibration over single-answer verbalization. Chain-of-thought prompting provides no consistent benefit and in many conditions makes calibration worse. Linguistic uncertainty expressions are competitive with numerical verbalization on TriviaQA and SciQ but substantially outperform numerical verbalization on TruthfulQA, where the best linguistic method (Ling. 1S-opt.) reaches ECE 0.125 against the best numerical method's 0.270 for gpt-3.5-turbo. A key diagnostic using Llama-2-70B-Chat and its base model shows that RLHF training systematically worsens the calibration of conditional log-probabilities on both ECE and selective-classification AUC. Model-level nuances: GPT-4 verbalized probabilities are substantially better-calibrated than its own conditional probabilities even after temperature scaling; Claude-1 has competitive conditional log-probability calibration but is weaker at verbalizing well-calibrated confidences than the GPT family; Claude-2 has weaker conditional probabilities than Claude-1 but its verbalized calibration is comparable to gpt-3.5-turbo and surpasses GPT models on TruthfulQA; Llama-2-70B-Chat shows only inconsistent gains from verbalization.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is directly relevant to Phase 1 on two dimensions. First, it provides ready-made verbalization elicitation protocols (Verb. 1S top-k and Verb. 2S top-k) that can be applied verbatim to post-trained Qwen3 checkpoints in each SFT, DPO, and KTO arm, using ECE and selective-classification AUC as the primary calibration read-outs. Because verbalization requires no access to token log-probabilities, it is the natural primary calibration signal whenever DPO or KTO training alters the output distribution in ways that decouple logits from generation behavior. Second, Figure 2 and the Llama-2-70B-Chat analysis provide direct evidence that RLHF degrades conditional log-probability calibration, which motivates using verbalized ECE rather than logit-based ECE as the headline metric for the DPO and KTO arms. The CoT non-result (Result 4) is a cautionary note for reasoning-oriented training: if the Phase 1 study includes chain-of-thought abstention variants, this paper predicts that CoT-style reasoning steps will not help and may hurt verbalized calibration. The Llama-2-70B-Chat result appropriately contextualizes potential null results on open-source models in the study. The cross-model asymmetry between conditional and verbalized calibration (Claude-1 has good logits but poor verbalization; Claude-2 reverses this) is a useful comparator when interpreting model-specific calibration patterns in the Qwen3 family after different training methods.
