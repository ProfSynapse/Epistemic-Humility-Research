## Abstract

Classical reinforcement learning (RL) typically seeks a deterministic policy that maximizes the expected sum of a scalar reward. Yet, modern applications such as language model fine-tuning or scientific discovery demand diversity. Existing remedies such as entropy regularization or diversity bonuses often require fragile trade-offs that sacrifice performance for stochasticity or rely on heuristic metrics that can misalign policy rankings. We argue that diversity is more naturally understood as the rational response to uncertainty in the reward. When the reward function is not perfectly known--as is the case with ambiguous preferences or imperfect reward models--committing to a single action can be sub-optimal. Building on this, we propose a fundamental reformulation of the RL objective by replacing the scalar reward with a distribution over reward functions, and applying a non-linear objective over sets of actions. The result is a framework in which calibrated behavioural diversity emerges naturally, remains controllable through the reward function distribution, and is obtained without sacrificing expected reward. Focusing on the contextual bandit setting, we derive a principled gradient estimator for this objective and prove that our formulation naturally generalizes both vanilla policy gradient and more recently developed action-set approaches. Our empirical results demonstrate that this framework offers a robust and theoretically grounded alternative for complex RL tasks where the traditional formulation of the problem fails to induce the desired breadth of agent behaviour.

## Summary

GX-Chen et al. (lead author at NYU; co-authors at Google DeepMind) propose ROSA (Randomized Objectives, Set Actions), a reformulation of the reinforcement learning objective that replaces the scalar reward with a distribution over reward functions and applies a nonlinear objective over sets of actions rather than single actions. The core argument is that behavioral diversity is the rational response to reward uncertainty: when the reward function is imperfectly known, as is the case with ambiguous human preferences or noisy reward models, committing to a single deterministic action is sub-optimal. Standard remedies such as entropy regularization or diversity bonuses require fragile trade-offs, either sacrificing expected reward for stochasticity or relying on heuristic metrics that can misalign policy rankings. ROSA avoids these trade-offs by grounding diversity in the uncertainty structure of the reward itself. Working in the contextual bandit setting, the paper derives a principled gradient estimator and proves that the ROSA objective generalizes both vanilla policy gradient (Proposition 3.2) and action-set approaches (Theorem 4.1). Two concrete variants are studied empirically: ROSA+Max, which selects the maximum reward under the distribution, and ROSA+Softmax, which applies a softmax aggregation. Both yield calibrated behavioral diversity that is controllable through the choice of reward function distribution and does not sacrifice expected reward. Empirical results show the framework outperforms standard RL formulations on complex tasks where the traditional single-objective formulation fails to produce adequate response breadth.

## Relevance to experiment

The paper's central claim is that imperfect reward models make single-action commitment sub-optimal and that a distribution over reward functions induces calibrated diversity without a performance penalty. That directly touches the Phase 1 concern: reward model imperfection is the reason the SFT vs DPO vs KTO abstention study cannot simply maximize a single reward signal. ROSA offers a theoretical framing for why a policy trained under reward uncertainty should exhibit broader behavioral coverage, which is a testable prediction: a model trained with ROSA-style objectives may show wider hidden-state coverage across candidate responses compared with a policy trained against a fixed scalar reward. The paper does not itself report calibration or abstention metrics, so it does not populate effects.csv directly.
