## Abstract

As large language models attract increasing attention and find widespread application, concurrent challenges of reliability also arise at the same time. Confidence calibration, an effective analysis method for gauging the reliability of deep models, serves as a crucial tool for assessing and improving their reliability. However, such investigation has been comparatively underexplored. In this work, we conduct a systematic examination of the calibration of aligned language models throughout the entire construction process, including pretraining and alignment training. At each stage, we investigate how different training settings, such as parameter scales and training data, affect model calibration. To thoroughly assess model calibration, we evaluate models on three most concerned aspects: generation, factuality and understanding. Our work sheds light on whether popular LLMs are well-calibrated and how the training process influences model calibration.

## Summary

Zhu et al. conduct a lifecycle calibration study covering the full LLM construction pipeline: pretraining (Pythia suite, 70M to 12B parameters) and alignment training (LLaMA-7B under instruction tuning and RLHF). Calibration is measured with ECE on logit-space next-token probabilities across three tasks: causal language modeling (CLM, evaluated on The PILE), factual entity prediction (T-REx), and multiple-choice reasoning (MMLU). In pretraining, larger scale and more training steps both improve calibration, though the effect is mild on CLM (where all 8 Pythia scales already achieve ECE below 0.1) and stronger on T-REx and MMLU. On MMLU specifically, ECE decreases monotonically with scale even though task accuracy stays near random chance, suggesting larger models become more aware of their own capability limits. In alignment training, instruction tuning consistently degrades ECE across all three tasks, and the harm grows with additional training epochs. The damage is worse when fine-tuning on Alpaca (a synthetic, self-instruct corpus) than on OpenAssistant (OA, a human-labeled crowd-sourced corpus). MPNET plus t-SNE analysis shows Alpaca response features cluster densely while OA features are more evenly distributed, and the authors attribute the calibration gap to this diversity difference: 52k Alpaca items were generated from only 175 human seed tasks via GPT-3 self-instruct, leading to homogeneous format and content. LoRA tuning consistently outperforms full fine-tuning in calibration across all instruction settings, with ECE deterioration described as "often in a level of 0.001" under LoRA versus visible worsening under full fine-tuning. RLHF (PPO on LLaMA, starting from Alpaca-SFT, ranked by GPT-4) adds no significant further calibration degradation beyond the SFT baseline across all three tasks, even when run for 3 epochs. The core lifecycle narrative: pretraining builds calibration; instruction tuning erodes it; RLHF does little additional damage once SFT is done; LoRA and diverse data both partially protect against the SFT erosion.

- T-REx facts generation: both calibration and task accuracy show stronger positive correlation with parameter scale than on CLM (Section 5.2, Figure 3 panels c-d).
- MMLU: ECE decreases monotonically as Pythia scale increases even though task accuracy stays near random chance for all sizes, suggesting larger models are more aware of their own capability limits (Section 5.2, Figure 3 panels e-f).
- Pythia-1.4B (Pythia-1B4) training dynamics (checkpoints step 256 to step 143k): accuracy and calibration both improve in the early pretraining stage; ECE then stabilizes at a low level on CLM for the remainder of training (Sections 5.1, 5.3, Figure 4).
- Instruction tuning training data: direct fine-tuning with Alpaca harms calibration most; OA-trained models show better calibration, with ECE on MMLU sometimes better than base LLaMA in epoch 1 (Section 6.2, Figure 5).
- LoRA vs full fine-tuning: LoRA-tuned models outperform fully fine-tuned models in calibration across all three tasks; ECE deterioration under LoRA is described as 'often in a level of 0.001' (noted as a magnitude, not necessarily a strict per-epoch rate) while full fine-tuning becomes visibly worse (Section 6.2, Figure 5).
- Training dynamics of instruction tuning: in almost all instruction tuning groups, more training epochs lead to worse calibration, indicating the model is 'affected severely by the small instruction dataset' (the paper uses this phrase, not 'overfitting') (Section 6.2, Figure 5).
- RLHF (PPO on LLaMA-7B starting from Alpaca-SFT, using GPT-4-ranked responses): no significant additional calibration degradation beyond the SFT baseline across all three tasks; calibration does not deteriorate as RLHF runs for 3 epochs (Section 6.3, Table 3, Figure 5).
- Semantic diversity (MPNET + t-SNE): Alpaca response features cluster densely while OA features are more evenly distributed; Alpaca was generated from 175 human seed tasks fed into GPT-3 via self-instruct and expanded to 52k items; OA was crowd-sourced with around 11k English single-turn pairs (Section 6.2, Figure 6).

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is the closest lifecycle calibration reference for the SFT-then-preference paradigm used in the Epistemic Humility experiment. Three findings are directly actionable. First, SFT degrades ECE as a baseline expectation: the locked training-regimen SFT arms will almost certainly worsen calibration relative to the base Qwen3 model, and the magnitude will depend on dataset diversity. Second, LoRA is predicted to produce better-calibrated models than full SFT across all tasks, which favors the parameter-efficient arms in the sweep design. Third, data diversity is a covariate: the Alpaca-vs-OA comparison gives a concrete mechanism (semantic feature clustering) that motivates auditing the abstention training corpus for instruction homogeneity before deployment. On the RLHF side, the finding that PPO adds no significant calibration harm beyond SFT baseline (across 3 epochs) partly counters the `rlhf-degrades-conditional-calibration` mechanism documented from the just-ask-for-calibration paper (2305.14975); the difference is that Zhu et al. start from a fully SFT'd model (Alpaca-trained LLaMA) rather than a raw base model, suggesting the SFT step absorbs most of the calibration cost. The paper does not study abstention-specific behavior or provide effect sizes that map directly onto effects.csv. It also does not cover DPO or KTO, which are the preference optimization arms in the locked training-regimen study. The most direct use is as a calibration-trajectory reference: after each training arm, comparing the ECE trajectories against the Zhu et al. lifecycle pattern will show whether the abstention training produces anomalous calibration behavior beyond what standard SFT-to-RLHF already induces.

- LoRA-tuned models consistently achieve better ECE than fully fine-tuned models across all instruction datasets and all three evaluation tasks; ECE deterioration under LoRA is described as 'often in a level of 0.001' while full fine-tuning becomes visibly worse across epochs, confirming LoRA as a calibration regularizer. - RLHF applied on top of an instruction-tuned LLaMA-7B (Alpaca-SFT starting point, PPO with GPT-4-ranked responses) produces no significant additional ECE degradation beyond the SFT baseline across all three tasks, even after 3 RLHF epochs, suggesting RLHF does not compound the calibration harm already introduced by SFT. (Section 6.3, Table 3, Figure 5) (library/fulltext/2311.13240.html) [[rlhf-preserves-calibration-after-sft]]
