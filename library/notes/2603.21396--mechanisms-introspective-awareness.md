## Abstract

Recent work has shown that LLMs can sometimes detect when steering vectors are injected into their residual stream and identify the injected concept -- a phenomenon termed "introspective awareness." We investigate the mechanisms underlying this capability in open-weights models. First, we find that it is behaviorally robust: models detect injected steering vectors at moderate rates with 0% false positives across diverse prompts and dialogue formats. Notably, this capability emerges specifically from post-training; we show that preference optimization algorithms like DPO can elicit it, but standard supervised finetuning does not. We provide evidence that detection cannot be explained by simple linear association between certain steering vectors and directions promoting affirmative responses. We trace the detection mechanism to a two-stage circuit in which "evidence carrier" features in early post-injection layers detect perturbations monotonically along diverse directions, suppressing downstream "gate" features that implement a default negative response. This circuit is absent in base models and robust to refusal ablation. Identification of injected concepts relies on largely distinct later-layer mechanisms that only weakly overlap with those involved in detection. Finally, we show that introspective capability is substantially underelicited: ablating refusal directions improves detection by +53%, and a trained bias vector improves it by +75% on held-out concepts, both without meaningfully increasing false positives. Our results suggest that this introspective awareness of injected concepts is robust and mechanistically nontrivial, and could be substantially amplified in future models. Code: this https URL .

## Summary

The paper studies how open-weight chat models detect and identify concept-vector
injections into their residual streams. It finds nonzero detection with low
false-positive rates across several prompt and dialogue variants, but the
effect depends strongly on post-training and the assistant role. OLMo checkpoint
and training ablations indicate that contrastive preference optimization first
installs reliable injection-control discrimination, while ordinary supervised
fine-tuning does not. Causal MLP and transcoder interventions identify a
distributed two-stage detection circuit. Early evidence-carrier features react
to diverse perturbations and suppress later features that favor a default
negative answer. Concept identification relies on a largely separate later
pathway. Refusal-direction ablation and a learned additive bias vector expose
more of the narrow detection capability, but neither establishes broad
self-knowledge.

## Extracted numbers

- Across 500 concepts in Gemma3-27B at layer 37 and strength 4, mean detection
  is 38.2 percent and median detection is 30.0 percent. Fifty-five concepts
  reach at least 90 percent, while 63 reach 0 percent (Figure 19; Appendix B.4).
- OLMo-3.1-32B DPO reaches a 9.8 percentage-point true-minus-false detection
  rate in the official checkpoint. The authors' DPO run reaches 14.4 points,
  while the official SFT checkpoint is -11.5 points (Table 3; Section 3.3).
- Swapping either the mean-direction projection or its orthogonal residual
  reduces success-concept detection from 66.1 percent to 39.0 or 44.4 percent,
  respectively. Opposite directions both trigger detection for 23.3 percent of
  success-success pairs (Figures 6 and 7; Sections 4.1 and 4.2).
- Mean-ablation of layer-45 MLP output reduces detection from 39.0 percent to
  24.2 percent for layer-37 injection. The same component is the only tested
  layer whose steered activation significantly raises detection when patched
  into an unsteered run (Figure 10; Section 5.2).
- Progressive ablation of 200 negative-attribution gate features reduces
  detection from 39.5 percent to 10.1 percent. Patching steered gate
  activations into controls reaches 25.1 percent detection (Figure 11; Section
  5.3).
- Refusal-direction ablation raises Gemma3-27B detection from 10.8 percent to
  63.8 percent and introspection from 4.6 percent to 24.1 percent at strength 2.
  False positives rise from 0.0 percent to 7.3 percent (Figure 4; Section 3.3).
- A learned additive bias at layer 29 raises held-out detection by 74.7 points,
  forced identification by 21.9 points, and joint introspection by 54.7 points,
  with 0 percent false positives on the held-out concepts (Figures 16 and 17;
  Section 6).
- The same learned bias reduces chain-of-thought faithfulness by 16.0 points on
  MMLU and 25.2 points on GPQA, and reduces prefill detection from 36.3 percent
  to 16.1 percent (Tables 12 and 13; Appendix S).

## Relevance to experiment

The paper gives causal evidence that post-training can install a distributed
route from an injected residual-stream anomaly to an explicit report. Its
checkpoint comparison separates contrastive preference training from ordinary
supervised fine-tuning, and its circuit interventions separate early evidence
transport from a later output gate. These results concern a synthetic injected
concept and a binary detection task. The paper does not test a naturally
occurring answerability representation or show that such a representation
controls normal generation. It also does not include the matched prompt-only
source control used to distinguish activation-specific access from generic
irregularity detection. The paper itself describes the circuit as anomaly
detection and warns that the learned bias improves this narrow task without
improving broad self-knowledge.

## Claims

- Evidence label: behavioral stress test. Some open-weight chat models
  discriminate injection from no-injection trials across prompt and dialogue
  variants, but reliability drops outside the trained assistant persona and
  some formats induce confabulation (Figures 2 and 3; Sections 3.1 and 3.2).
- Evidence label: training-stage intervention. Contrastive preference objectives
  produce positive true-minus-false detection on OLMo and Gemma checkpoints,
  while non-contrastive supervised objectives do not (Figure 5; Table 3;
  Section 3.3; Appendix D).
- Evidence label: geometric intervention. Detection depends on multiple
  directions and nonlinear downstream features rather than a single direction
  that promotes affirmative answers (Figures 6 through 8; Section 4).
- Evidence label: causal circuit analysis. Early, distributed evidence carriers
  suppress a compact set of later default-negative gate features. MLP ablation,
  activation patching, and feature interventions change detection in the
  predicted directions (Figures 10 through 15; Sections 5.2 through 5.4).
- Evidence label: circuit dissociation. Detection peaks in middle layers and
  depends strongly on layer-45 MLP and gate features, while forced concept
  identification peaks later and remains comparatively intact under gate
  ablation (Figures 9 through 11; Sections 5.1 through 5.3).
- Evidence label: elicitation intervention. Refusal-direction ablation and a
  trained additive bias substantially increase injection reports, but the bias
  does not improve hallucination detection, chain-of-thought faithfulness, or
  prefill detection (Section 6; Appendices C, R, and S).
- Evidence label: source limitation. Most mechanistic experiments use one
  Gemma3-27B checkpoint and an LLM judge. The reported 0 percent false-positive
  results are scoped to trial numbers 1 through 10, and other trial numbers can
  trigger systematic confabulation (Section 8; Appendix B.3).
