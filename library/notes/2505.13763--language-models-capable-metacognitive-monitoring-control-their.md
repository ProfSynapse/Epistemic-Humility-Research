## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can sometimes report the strategies they actually use to solve tasks, yet at other times seem unable to recognize those strategies that govern their behavior. This suggests a limited degree of metacognition - the capacity to monitor one's own cognitive processes for subsequent reporting and self-control. Metacognition enhances LLMs' capabilities in solving complex tasks but also raises safety concerns, as models may obfuscate their internal processes to evade neural-activation-based oversight (e.g., safety detector). Given society's increased reliance on these models, it is critical that we understand their metacognitive abilities. To address this, we introduce a neuroscience-inspired neurofeedback paradigm that uses in-context learning to quantify metacognitive abilities of LLMs to report and control their activation patterns. We demonstrate that their abilities depend on several factors: the number of in-context examples provided, the semantic interpretability of the neural activation direction (to be reported/controlled), and the variance explained by that direction. These directions span a "metacognitive space" with dimensionality much lower than the model's neural space, suggesting LLMs can monitor only a small subset of their neural activations. Our paradigm provides empirical evidence to quantify metacognition in LLMs, with significant implications for AI safety (e.g., adversarial attack and defense).

## Summary

The paper introduces a neurofeedback paradigm that labels sentences with a
discretized projection of the model's own residual-stream activation. After
seeing sentence-label pairs in context, a model either reports the label of a
new sentence or tries to move its activation toward a requested label. Target
directions come from logistic regression on semantic labels or principal
component analysis of activation variance.

Reporting and control improve with more examples. Semantically interpretable
logistic-regression directions are easiest to access, and early high-variance
principal components are more accessible than later components. Models also
show implicit control: the same externally supplied sentence acquires different
activations under different target-label instructions. Control is stronger in
larger models and later layers, but it has off-target effects.

## Extracted numbers

- The study evaluates four Llama instruction models from 1B to 70B parameters
  and three Qwen2.5 instruction models from 1B to 7B parameters (Appendix A.2).
- Each dataset contributes 1,200 balanced sentences. The authors use 600 to fit
  directions and 600 for neurofeedback experiments (Appendix A.3).
- Main control summaries use 256 in-context examples. Each control-effect curve
  averages 100 experiments (Sections 4.1 and 4.2, Figures 3 and 4).
- Control precision exceeded one for principal components 1 through 32 but not
  for components 128 through 512. The authors place controllable-space
  dimensionality between 32 and 128 under this test (Appendix B.2, Figure B.2).
- Explicit control was consistently stronger than implicit control. Larger
  models and later target layers generally produced stronger effects (Sections
  4.2 and 4.3, Figures 4 and 5).
- The main ETHICS result was replicated on True-False, emotion, and synthesized
  sycophancy contrasts (Appendix B.6, Figure B.7).

## Relevance to experiment

The implicit-control condition is direct evidence that prompt-conditioned model
computation can influence a selected internal direction while input text is
held fixed. It also warns that a model can manipulate a linear activation
monitor after learning its feedback labels.

This is in-context control, not weights-level consultation. The target label is
provided by the experimenter through many demonstrations. The paper does not
train a persistent connection between an internal answerability readout and an
action policy, and it does not study answerability. For logistic-regression
axes, activation labels correlate with semantic dataset labels, so those tests
do not isolate access to an otherwise hidden scalar from semantic pattern
learning. Principal-component tests reduce this concern but remain tied to
prompt-conditioned label inference.

The authors use an operational definition of metacognition and explicitly avoid
claims about consciousness or human equivalence. Other limits include one-shot
control of one direction, residual-stream activations only, weaker implicit
effects, substantial off-target movement, and four short-sentence semantic
contrasts.

## Claims

- Evidence label: method. Neurofeedback labels discretize a sentence's
  projection onto a fitted residual-stream direction and present those labels
  as in-context examples (Sections 2.2 to 2.5).
- Evidence label: reporting. Models predict activation-derived labels better as
  examples increase, with stronger performance for semantic and high-variance
  directions (Section 3, Figure 2).
- Evidence label: causal control. With the evaluated sentence held fixed,
  target-label instructions shift its activation along the requested direction,
  although less than when the model generates the sentence (Section 4.2,
  Figure 4).
- Evidence label: distributed mechanism. Target-axis effects build across
  multiple layers before reaching the layer used to define the direction
  (Section 4.4, Figure 6).
