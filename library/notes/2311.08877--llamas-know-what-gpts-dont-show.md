## Abstract

To maintain user trust, large language models (LLMs) should signal low confidence on examples where they are incorrect, instead of misleading the user. The standard approach of estimating confidence is to use the softmax probabilities of these models, but as of November 2023, state-of-the-art LLMs such as GPT-4 and Claude-v1.3 do not provide access to these probabilities. We first study eliciting confidence linguistically -- asking an LLM for its confidence in its answer -- which performs reasonably (80.5% AUC on GPT-4 averaged across 12 question-answering datasets -- 7% above a random baseline) but leaves room for improvement. We then explore using a surrogate confidence model -- using a model where we do have probabilities to evaluate the original model's confidence in a given question. Surprisingly, even though these probabilities come from a different and often weaker model, this method leads to higher AUC than linguistic confidences on 9 out of 12 datasets. Our best method composing linguistic confidences and surrogate model probabilities gives state-of-the-art confidence estimates on all 12 datasets (84.6% average AUC on GPT-4).

## Summary

Shrivastava et al. address a practical gap: GPT-4 and Claude-v1.3 (as of late 2023) expose no token probabilities, yet good confidence scores are essential for selective classification. The paper evaluates two ways to fill that gap and then combines them. First, linguistic confidences (prompting a model to state its own confidence numerically) work better for stronger models: GPT-4 achieves 80.5% selective-classification AUC averaged across 12 QA datasets, 7 percentage points above the random baseline that equals accuracy. But linguistic confidences are coarse. GPT-4 emits confidence 0.9 on fully half of all examples, producing only 8 unique values across 12 datasets, whereas Llama 2 70B generates 1,456 distinct probability values over the same set. As a result, linguistic signals are substantially weaker than model probabilities for open-weight models: Llama 2 probability-based AUC exceeds its linguistic AUC by 10.7 points, and AUROC by 19.0 points. Second, surrogate confidence estimation borrows the open-weight model's probability for the question and pairs it with the closed model's answer. Llama 2 70B as surrogate raises GPT-4 AUC from 80.5% to 82.1% and wins on 9 of 12 datasets. The key insight is that question difficulty transfers across models: the Pearson correlation between GPT-4 and Llama 2 70B correct-answer indicators is 0.39, against 0.19 for the smaller Llama 2 13B, and even weak surrogates (47.2% accuracy) outperform each target model's own linguistic confidences. Mixing linguistic and surrogate scores (optimizing a scalar mixture weight alpha) pushes GPT-4 AUC to 83.4% and beats the concurrent self-consistency baseline (AUC 82.8%) while requiring far fewer samples. Layering self-consistency on top, the SC Mixture method reaches 84.6% AUC and 74.1% AUROC for GPT-4, gains of 4.1 and 9.1 points over vanilla linguistic confidences. A tiebreaking variant that sets alpha to a near-zero value (0.001) recovers 86% of the AUC gain and 87% of the AUROC gain, demonstrating that even a negligible surrogate weight breaks ties among identical linguistic scores and captures most of the benefit.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is directly relevant on three axes. First, it establishes a practical baseline for confidence-score quality on closed-source models: the 80.5% linguistic AUC for GPT-4 is the benchmark any locked training-regimen abstention-trained checkpoint should be compared against, because a fine-tuned open-weight model that degrades below this ceiling has regressed rather than improved. Second, the surrogate method provides a tool for locked training-regimen evaluation: if Qwen3 checkpoints after SFT or DPO are opaque to token-level inspection (e.g., after quantization or packaging), a Llama 2 70B surrogate can still rank questions by difficulty and supply a confidence signal, with the caveat that the paper's surrogates share the same pretraining distribution. Third, the finding that question difficulty transfers between models (Pearson r = 0.39 for GPT-4 vs. Llama 2 70B) directly supports the mechanism program abstention-design assumption that a model's internal knowledge-boundary signal correlates with factual difficulty across architectures. One constraint: the paper evaluates API models without fine-tuning access and uses fixed multiple-choice QA benchmarks; whether surrogate transfer holds after alignment training that shifts a model's error pattern is an open question the experiment can probe.

## Claims

- Linguistic confidence elicitation (prompting a model to state its own confidence numerically) achieves 80.5% average selective-classification AUC for GPT-4 across 12 QA datasets, 7 points above the random baseline derived from model accuracy, but is limited by extreme coarseness: GPT-4 emits only 8 unique confidence values and assigns 0.9 to 50% of all examples. (Table 1; Section 3; Section 6) [[verbalized-confidence]]
- A surrogate model's token probabilities, paired with the target model's answer, outperform the target model's own linguistic confidences on 9 of 12 datasets for GPT-4 (AUC 82.1% vs. 80.5%), even when the surrogate is far weaker: Llama 2 13B at 47.2% accuracy still matches or beats GPT-4 and Claude-v1.3 linguistic baselines. (Table 2; Section 4.1) [[surrogate-confidence-estimation]]
- Mixing linguistic confidences with surrogate probabilities at an optimized alpha surpasses the self-consistency baseline (83.4% vs. 82.8% AUC for GPT-4) while requiring only one model call rather than five; adding self-consistency on top (SC Mixture) reaches 84.6% AUC and 74.1% AUROC, gains of 4.1 and 9.1 points over vanilla linguistic confidences. (Table 2; Table 3) [[surrogate-confidence-estimation]]
- A tiebreaking variant setting alpha to 0.001 recovers 86% of the AUC gain and 87% of the AUROC gain of the optimal alpha for GPT-4, demonstrating that the value of surrogate probabilities is primarily in resolving ties among identical linguistic scores rather than in wholesale replacement. (Section 5.1) [[question-difficulty-transfer]]
- The Pearson correlation between GPT-4 and Llama 2 70B correct-answer indicators is 0.39, compared to 0.19 for Llama 2 13B, suggesting that question-difficulty structure is partially shared across model families and scales with surrogate capacity, grounding why surrogate confidence transfer works. (Section 6; Appendix A.8) [[question-difficulty-transfer]]
