## Abstract

How is knowledge stored in an LLM's weights? We study this via layer pruning: if removing a certain layer does not affect model performance in common question-answering benchmarks, then the weights in that layer are not necessary for storing the knowledge needed to answer those questions. To find these unnecessary parameters, we identify the optimal block of layers to prune by considering similarity across layers; then, to "heal" the damage, we perform a small amount of finetuning. Surprisingly, with this method we find minimal degradation of performance until after a large fraction (up to half) of the layers are removed for some common open-weight models. From a scientific perspective, the robustness of these LLMs to the deletion of layers implies either that current pretraining methods are not properly leveraging the parameters in the deeper layers of the network or that the shallow layers play a critical role in storing knowledge. For our study, we use parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods, specifically quantization and Low Rank Adapters (QLoRA), such that each of our experiments can be performed on a single 40GB A100 GPU.

## Claims

- Evidence label: experiment. For Llama-2-70B, similarity-informed layer pruning with QLoRA healing removes up to roughly half of the model's layers before MMLU/BoolQ accuracy collapses to random guessing (Figure 1).
- Evidence label: experiment. Across seven open-weight models spanning 2.7B-70B parameters (Llama-2, Qwen, Mistral-7B, Phi-2), the robust-pruning threshold before the QA-accuracy phase transition to random guessing is model-family dependent: ~45%-55% for Llama-2, ~35% for Mistral-7B, ~25% for Phi-2, and ~20% for Qwen (Figure 2, Section 4.1).
- Evidence label: experiment. After healing, next-token-prediction loss on C4 grows smoothly and continuously with pruning fraction even through the pruning fractions where QA-benchmark accuracy sharply transitions to random guessing, showing that perplexity-style loss and downstream QA accuracy are decoupled/miscalibrated under pruning (Figure 3, Section 4.2).
- Evidence label: analysis. Angular-distance heatmaps across all seven models show deep layer blocks are consistently more similar to their neighbors than shallow blocks (except blocks touching the final layer, which are near-maximally dissimilar), motivating a simpler "drop-the-deepest-block" heuristic that, after healing, performs comparably to the full similarity-informed strategy (Figure 4, Figure 5, Section 4.3-4.4).
