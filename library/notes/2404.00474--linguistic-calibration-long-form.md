## Abstract

Language models (LMs) may lead their users to make suboptimal downstream decisions when they confidently hallucinate. This issue can be mitigated by having the LM verbally convey the probability that its claims are correct, but existing models cannot produce long-form text with calibrated confidence statements. Through the lens of decision-making, we define linguistic calibration for long-form generations: an LM is linguistically calibrated if its generations enable its users to make calibrated probabilistic predictions. This definition enables a training framework where a supervised finetuning step bootstraps an LM to emit long-form generations with confidence statements such as "I estimate a 30% chance of..." or "I am certain that...", followed by a reinforcement learning step which rewards generations that enable a user to provide calibrated answers to related questions. We linguistically calibrate Llama 2 7B and find in automated and human evaluations of long-form generations that it is significantly more calibrated than strong finetuned factuality baselines with comparable accuracy. These findings generalize under significant domain shifts to scientific and biomedical questions and to an entirely held-out person biography generation task. Our results demonstrate that long-form generations may be calibrated end-to-end by constructing an objective in the space of the predictions that users make in downstream decision-making.

## Summary

Band et al. (2024) introduce Linguistic Calibration (LC), a two-stage training framework for calibrating the verbalized confidence statements in long-form language model outputs. The problem they address is that existing calibration methods apply only to classification settings or short outputs; they cannot calibrate the many distinct claims inside a paragraph-length generation. The framework reframes the problem through a decision-theoretic lens: a model is linguistically calibrated if its long-form outputs enable downstream users to make calibrated probabilistic forecasts.

Stage 1 (summary distillation) bootstraps the base model. The base LM is sampled M times for each query to produce many long-form responses; an API-based LLM then summarizes those samples into a single consensus paragraph that includes explicit confidence statements calibrated to claim-level frequency ("I estimate a 30% chance of..."). The base model is then fine-tuned via SFT on these query-summary pairs to produce LC SFT, an initial policy that can express graded confidence in natural language.

Stage 2 (decision-based RL) optimizes the LC objective end-to-end using PPO. The reward is the log-likelihood of the correct answer under a neural-net surrogate forecaster conditioned on the model's generated context. The surrogate forecaster decomposes into two components: ExtractAnswers (which identifies candidate answers from the generated text) and ForecastProbs (which assigns probabilities to those answers). Both are fine-tuned LMs trained offline before the RL loop begins, making the RL training cheap relative to involving a human or frontier-LLM forecaster in each step. The KL penalty from LC SFT prevents over-optimization. The resulting LC RL model is evaluated not on whether it outputs the right answer but on whether its generation enables a user (or surrogate) to make well-calibrated forecasts, measured by forecast ECE.

Experiments calibrate Llama 2 7B using TriviaQA as the training distribution. LC RL Pareto-dominates strong factuality baselines (Factuality SFT, Factuality RL trained on binary correctness rewards) on the accuracy-ECE frontier: it substantially reduces forecast ECE while matching or slightly exceeding their accuracy. The improvement in ECE from LC SFT to LC RL is larger than the improvement from Factuality SFT to Factuality RL, supporting the claim that optimizing a proper scoring rule is more effective for calibration than optimizing a binary correctness signal. Simulated forecasters closely track human forecasters on held-out evaluations, validating the automated pipeline. Results generalize zero-shot to the Jeopardy out-of-distribution QA dataset and, more strikingly, to an entirely held-out person biography generation task evaluated via a claim-level FactScore-style pipeline over 500 people from the unlabeled FactScore split. LC RL also achieves forecast ECE comparable to much more factual GPT-4 baselines, demonstrating that calibration and factuality are separable objectives even in small models.

## Relevance to experiment

This paper is directly relevant to Phase 1 of the Epistemic-Humility experiment in three ways.

First, it shows that binary correctness rewards (Factuality RL) produce worse calibration than a log-likelihood proper scoring rule reward (LC RL), even when both start from the same SFT checkpoint. Phase 1 trains SFT, DPO, and KTO arms on binary or pairwise correctness signals. The LC result is a concrete warning that those arms may improve accuracy while leaving calibration flat or even making it worse. Adding forecast ECE as a supplementary evaluation signal (not just abstention-accuracy) would directly test this concern.

Second, the summary distillation step is a lightweight recipe for bootstrapping any base LM to produce verbalized confidence statements without a proprietary frontier model in the RL loop. The Phase 1 base models (Qwen3 checkpoints, locally accessible for white-box PPO) could use a structurally identical summary distillation pass to create an LC SFT variant, then compare it against the DPO/KTO arms as an alternative calibration-first training path.

Third, the OOD generalization results (Jeopardy, biography generation) suggest that calibration training on TriviaQA-style QA transfers broadly. If the Phase 1 abstention training generalizes to the transfer evaluation tasks already in the experiment design, the LC approach would likely generalize equally or better, making it a useful positive-control baseline for those transfer evaluations.

The primary constraint is that PPO requires white-box access to compute gradients and a surrogate forecaster fine-tuning loop. This is fully satisfied by locally hosted Qwen3 checkpoints. The surrogate forecaster itself requires an API-based LLM for the data-generation phase (the summary distillation and ExtractAnswers steps) but not during the RL loop itself, making compute costs tractable.

## Claims

- LC RL, trained with a log-likelihood proper scoring rule reward via PPO, achieves forecast ECE of 0.108 (simulated) and 0.116 (human) on TriviaQA, substantially below LC SFT (0.166 / 0.163), with accuracy matching or exceeding factuality-trained baselines. (Figure 4) [[linguistic-calibration-lc]]
- The gain in forecast ECE from LC SFT to LC RL is larger than the corresponding gain from Factuality SFT to Factuality RL, demonstrating that optimizing a proper scoring rule of downstream forecasts is more effective for calibration than optimizing binary factuality rewards. (Section 4.2) [[proper-scoring-rule-rl-reward-calibrates-verbalized-confidence]]
- LC RL achieves forecast ECE comparable to GPT-4 baselines despite significantly worse factuality, showing that calibration and factuality are separable objectives and that even a small LLM can be well-calibrated with the right training objective. (Section 4.1) [[linguistic-calibration-lc]]
- LC RL trained on TriviaQA generalizes zero-shot to person biography generation (an entirely held-out task), with claim-level ECE of 0.266 vs LC SFT's 0.301 over 500 people from the FactScore unlabeled split. (Figure 7, Section 4.3) [[summary-distillation]]
- Simulated neural-net surrogate forecasters closely track human forecaster calibration results on TriviaQA, validating the automated evaluation framework and making the training loop practical without per-step human annotation. (Figure 4 caption, Section 4.2) [[surrogate-confidence-estimation]]
