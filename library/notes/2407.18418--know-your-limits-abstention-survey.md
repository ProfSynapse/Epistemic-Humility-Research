## Abstract

Abstention, the refusal of large language models (LLMs) to provide an answer, is increasingly recognized for its potential to mitigate hallucinations and enhance safety in LLM systems. In this survey, we introduce a framework to examine abstention from three perspectives: the query, the model, and human values. We organize the literature on abstention methods, benchmarks, and evaluation metrics using this framework, and discuss merits and limitations of prior work. We further identify and motivate areas for future research, such as whether abstention can be achieved as a meta-capability that transcends specific tasks or domains, and opportunities to optimize abstention abilities in specific contexts. In doing so, we aim to broaden the scope and impact of abstention methodologies in AI systems.

## Summary

Wen et al. (2024) survey abstention in large language models under the title "The Art of Refusal," proposing a unified framework that treats the decision to abstain as a conjunction of three threshold conditions: query answerability a(x), model confidence c(x,y), and human value alignment h(x,y) or h(x). Violation of any one triggers refusal (r(x,y)=1). The survey then organizes the literature along the LLM lifecycle: pretraining (rare, one documented case), alignment (instruction tuning and preference optimization), and inference (input-processing, in-processing, output-processing). Key empirical patterns: instruction tuning on abstention-aware data reliably improves abstention but routinely induces over-refusal on benign inputs; direct preference optimization (DPO) is the most commonly deployed corrective. Safety alignment is vulnerable to erosion from finetuning, including finetuning on ostensibly safe data (Qi et al., 2024). The evaluation section formalizes a five-cell asymmetric confusion matrix (N1 through N5) that captures the structural asymmetry between answer-expected and abstention-expected queries, and from it derives a family of metrics including abstention accuracy, precision, recall, F1, coverage, abstention rate, reliable accuracy, effective reliability, C@Acc, AUROC, AUACC, and AURCC. The paper recommends balancing all four metric families rather than relying on any single number. Secondary findings cover differential abstention across demographic groups (less abstention on Africa and Asia election queries in ElectionQA23), the user-trust cost of first-person uncertainty expressions ("I'm not sure, but..."), and the widespread absence of cross-task or cross-domain abstention evaluation.

- Five-cell asymmetric abstention confusion matrix (Table 1): N1 = correctly answered when answer expected, N2 = incorrectly answered when answer expected, N3 = abstained when answer expected, N4 = correctly abstained when abstention expected, N5 = incorrectly answered when abstention expected; the abstention-expected row has no correctly-answered cell because a correct answer does not exist for those queries (Table 1, §4.2)
- Abstention Accuracy formula: (N1 + N4) / (N1 + N2 + N3 + N4 + N5); confirmed by §4.2 metric definitions; unambiguously fixes N4 as a correct outcome (§4.2)
- Effective Reliability formula: (N1 - N2 - N4) / total; rewards correct answers, penalizes wrong answers and excess abstentions simultaneously (§4.2)
- Metric battery recommendation: task performance (P/R/F1/Acc), abstention performance (abstention P/R/F1/Acc), abstention coverage (coverage, abstention rate), accuracy-coverage trade-off (ER, C@Acc, AUROC, AUACC, AURCC); no single metric captures all aspects (§4.2 Summary box)
- Three threshold conditions for abstention: a(x) < theta_a, c(x,y) < theta_c, or h(x,y) < theta_h triggers r(x,y)=1; framework also defines a query-only human value function h(x) (§2.2, Eq. 1)
- Pretraining abstention methods are rare: Neeman et al. (2023) is the only documented example in the survey, augmenting pretraining data to predict unanswerable on empty or randomly sampled documents (§3.1)
- Bianchi et al. (2024) show a small number of safety instructions in instruction tuning reduces harmful responses without capability loss, but an excessive number makes LLMs overly defensive (§3.2 Instruction Tuning)
- Lyu et al. (2024) note that benign finetuning can increase unsafe behaviors in aligned LLMs, citing Qi et al. (2024) as the primary demonstrators; the causal demonstration belongs to Qi et al., while Lyu et al. address the related prompt-template alignment setting (uncertain: survey credits Lyu et al. as noting, not demonstrating) (§3.2 Instruction Tuning)
- Kim et al. (2024b) find that first-person uncertainty expressions such as 'I'm not sure, but...' lower user confidence in system reliability and willingness to accept responses as correct (§4.2 Human-centric Evaluation)
- Feng et al. (2024b) show LLMs abstain less when predicting future election outcomes for Africa and Asia in ElectionQA23, raising fairness concerns about differential abstention rates across demographic groups (§5 Introducing biases)

## Relevance to experiment

This survey is the primary framing document for how abstention research connects to the Phase 1 SFT vs. DPO vs. KTO study. Three connections are direct. First, the survey's evidence that instruction tuning causes over-abstention while DPO corrects it (§3.2) is the theoretical motivation for comparing those training arms; the mechanisms instruction-tuning-causes-over-abstention and dpo-reduces-over-abstention in the graph trace here. Second, the five-family metric battery from §4.2 specifies what evaluation instruments the study should include; relying only on abstention rate or accuracy would miss the N3 and N4 failure modes the confusion matrix distinguishes. Third, the survey's §3.3.2 inference-stage methods (probing inner state, uncertainty estimation) map directly onto the Phase 3 mechanistic interpretability sweep: inner-state probes and gradient probes such as Grad Detect are precisely the in-processing abstention methods this survey catalogs, and the survey's warning that aligned LLMs may have poorly calibrated logits motivates why activation-space signals might diverge from output-level confidence after training.

## Claims

- Abstention methods can be organized along three axes of the LLM lifecycle (pretraining, alignment, inference) and three decision perspectives (query answerability, model confidence, human value alignment), providing a unified framework absent from prior work. (§2.1, Fig. 1, Fig. 2) [[abstention-framework-query-model-values]]
- Instruction tuning on abstention-aware data improves abstention ability but can lead to over-abstention; researchers disagree on whether it enables abstention as a meta-capability that generalizes across tasks and domains. (§3.2 Instruction Tuning Summary) [[over-abstention]]
- No single metric captures all aspects of abstention performance; the survey recommends balancing task accuracy, abstention precision/recall/F1, abstention rate, and accuracy-coverage trade-off measures (ER, C@Acc, AUROC, AUACC, AURCC). (§4.2, Table 1) [[effective-reliability]]
- Existing abstention evaluation benchmarks primarily focus on a single perspective (query, model, or human values) rather than providing holistic, system-wide assessment; abstention research has been largely confined to question-answering rather than general chatbot settings. (§4.1, §1 p3) [[abstention]]
