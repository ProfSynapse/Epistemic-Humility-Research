## Abstract

Despite the significant progress made in practical applications of aligned language models (LMs), they tend to be overconfident in output answers compared to the corresponding pre-trained LMs. In this work, we systematically evaluate the impact of the alignment process on logit-based uncertainty calibration of LMs under the multiple-choice setting. We first conduct a thoughtful empirical study on how aligned LMs differ in calibration from their pre-trained counterparts. Experimental results reveal that there are two distinct uncertainties in LMs under the multiple-choice setting, which are responsible for the answer decision and the format preference of the LMs, respectively. Then, we investigate the role of these two uncertainties on aligned LM's calibration through fine-tuning in simple synthetic alignment schemes and conclude that one reason for aligned LMs' overconfidence is the conflation of these two types of uncertainty. Furthermore, we examine the utility of common post-hoc calibration methods for aligned LMs and propose an easy-to-implement and sample-efficient method to calibrate aligned LMs. We hope our findings could provide insights into the design of more reliable alignment processes for LMs.

## Summary

He et al. (2023) conduct a systematic study of logit-based uncertainty calibration in aligned language models (LMs) under the multiple-choice question (MCQ) setting. The central finding is that the predictive probability a model assigns to a choice letter is the product of two separable factors (Equation 2): answer uncertainty, reflecting how the model ranks the candidate answers given that it will respond in a direct choice format, and format uncertainty, reflecting whether the model prefers to begin its response with a choice letter at all. Pre-trained LMs are poorly calibrated in zero-shot settings because format uncertainty drives down the probability mass on choice letters, but five-shot in-context learning (ICL) eliminates that uncertainty and restores calibration to near-zero ECE in large models. Aligned LMs are systematically more overconfident at every tested model size (7B, 13B, 33B, 70B), and crucially, ICL cannot restore their calibration because alignment has already corrupted the answer uncertainty component, not just format uncertainty. Six synthetic fine-tuning schemes on Llama-1 7B confirm the mechanism: schemes that touch answer uncertainty (SFT-Choice, SFT-Mixed, DPO-Choice, DPO-Mixed) induce overconfidence on MMLU, while schemes that target only format uncertainty (SFT-Format, DPO-Format) preserve calibration close to the pretrained ICL baseline. DPO-Choice causes the most severe overconfidence. In the Mistral-to-Zephyr pipeline, Mistral after SFT is the only aligned model in the study that preserves pretrained calibration; adding DPO sharply inflates confidence without a proportional accuracy gain. As a practical remedy, the authors propose temperature scaling using KL divergence between the aligned model's distribution and the (well-calibrated) pretrained distribution, optimized from only five held-out examples per task. This pretrained-distribution temperature scaling is the only post-hoc method that outperforms out-of-the-box calibration on all seven tasks for Llama-2-Chat 70B, whereas standard temperature scaling and KDE fail on some tasks (e.g., OpenbookQA). The paper uses seven benchmarks: MMLU, HellaSWAG, OpenbookQA, TruthfulQA, LogiQA, CivilComments, and IMDB.

- Pre-trained LMs show a large ECE gap between zero-shot and five-shot ICL settings, especially for large models; ICL largely eliminates format uncertainty and restores calibration to near-zero ECE in large pretrained models (Figure 3, §3.2).

- SFT-Format and DPO-Format synthetic alignment schemes preserve accuracy, ECE, and confidence close to the pretrained ICL baseline on MMLU; SFT-Choice, SFT-Mixed, DPO-Choice, and DPO-Mixed all exhibit overconfidence (Figure 7, §4.3).
- DPO-Choice (preference pairs sharing format but differing in choice) causes the most severe overconfidence on MMLU among all six synthetic alignment conditions (Figure 7, §4.3).

- Pretrained-distribution temperature scaling outperforms standard TS and KDE on all tested tasks for Llama-2-Chat 70B with a five-example calibration set; standard TS and KDE can perform worse than out-of-the-box calibration on some tasks (e.g., OpenbookQA) (Figure 8, §5.3).

## Relevance to experiment

This paper provides the most direct mechanistic account of why alignment degrades logit-based calibration, which is exactly the failure mode that the locked training-regimen Synaptic Tuner experiment is designed to detect and counter. Three findings are immediately actionable. First, the decomposition into answer uncertainty and format uncertainty predicts that any alignment stage touching answer probabilities (SFT-Choice-style or DPO with answer-contrastive pairs) will cause overconfidence on MCQ evaluation tasks regardless of task coverage during training. The locked training-regimen DPO arms use answer-contrastive preference pairs, placing them squarely in the DPO-Choice regime where this paper finds the most severe overconfidence. Second, the finding that ICL cannot restore calibration that alignment has destroyed (§3.3) rules out a simple inference-time fix: if DPO arm models are miscalibrated, few-shot prompting alone will not recover ECE. Third, the pretrained-distribution TS method requires only five held-out examples and access to the pretrained base model. Both are available in the Synaptic Tuner setup (the Qwen3 base model is the pretrained counterpart), making this a ready-to-implement post-hoc diagnostic layer after each training arm. For the Qwen3 base-vs-instruct comparison, the paper predicts the instruct model will have higher ECE on MCQ tasks even if its accuracy is similar or better, and that the gap will be visible in zero-shot but not in five-shot ICL (because ICL can change format preference but not the corrupted answer uncertainty). That combination of predictions is falsifiable against MMLU or similar benchmarks already in the experiment suite.
