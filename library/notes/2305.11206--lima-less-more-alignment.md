## Abstract

Large language models are trained in two stages: (1) unsupervised pretraining from raw text, to learn general-purpose representations, and (2) large scale instruction tuning and reinforcement learning, to better align to end tasks and user preferences. We measure the relative importance of these two stages by training LIMA, a 65B parameter LLaMa language model fine-tuned with the standard supervised loss on only 1,000 carefully curated prompts and responses, without any reinforcement learning or human preference modeling. LIMA demonstrates remarkably strong performance, learning to follow specific response formats from only a handful of examples in the training data, including complex queries that range from planning trip itineraries to speculating about alternate history. Moreover, the model tends to generalize well to unseen tasks that did not appear in the training data. In a controlled human study, responses from LIMA are either equivalent or strictly preferred to GPT-4 in 43% of cases; this statistic is as high as 58% when compared to Bard and 65% versus DaVinci003, which was trained with human feedback. Taken together, these results strongly suggest that almost all knowledge in large language models is learned during pretraining, and only limited instruction tuning data is necessary to teach models to produce high quality output.

## Summary

Zhou et al. propose the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis: a model's knowledge and capabilities are learned almost entirely during pretraining, and alignment (instruction tuning, RLHF) mainly teaches which subdistribution of formats and styles to use. To test this, they fine-tune a 65B-parameter LLaMa model, LIMA, with only supervised next-token loss on 1,000 carefully curated (prompt, response) pairs and no reinforcement learning or preference modeling. The 1,000 training examples are drawn from 750 curated community sources (Stack Exchange STEM/Other, wikiHow, r/WritingPrompts, Natural Instructions) plus 250 manually authored by the paper's own authors in a consistent "helpful AI assistant" voice; a 300-prompt test set and 50-prompt dev set are held out. LIMA generalizes to unseen task types not present in training and, in a controlled human preference study over the 300 test prompts, its responses are judged equivalent to or strictly better than GPT-4 in 43% of comparisons, Bard in 58%, and DaVinci003 (RLHF-trained) in 65%. A GPT-4-as-annotator replication of the same study shows the same trends. The authors also report that adding only 30 hand-crafted multi-turn dialogue examples substantially improves multi-turn conversational ability, reinforcing the claim that alignment data quality and diversity, not quantity, drives outcomes.

## Relevance to experiment

LIMA is the primary anchor for the "training is mostly format-teaching, not capability-teaching" reading of the SFT vs. DPO vs. KTO comparison: if 1,000 curated SFT examples with no RL stage can approach RLHF-trained systems on general instruction-following, the interesting residual question is what abstention-specific training adds beyond exposing the base model's latent behavior. This motivates reading trained-vs-prompted abstention gaps as evidence about which capability is genuinely new versus merely surfaced, and pairs directly with URIAL (arXiv:2312.01552), which pushes the same claim further by removing gradient updates from the alignment step entirely.
